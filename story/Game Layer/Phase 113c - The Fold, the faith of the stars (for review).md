## 1. What the books leave open, and what this is built on

- **No star, constellation, planet or comet is named in any book or volume.** Nothing here collides with a name.
- **There was an old faith that looked up.** The dead are held under the Weeping Canopy "the way the old faith said heaven held the dead" (B4P1:1370). The eight gods went unvisited "as the faiths died and the stairs were sealed", for "three hundred years of nobody" (B4P1:2081). No name, rite or priest is given.
- **The gods are under the eight cities** (B4P1:2081). So this faith puts no god in the sky.
- **"The planet does not rotate. It sustains."** (V1:16). The game's stars have never wheeled. The faith makes that its heart: the stars keep their places.
- **Already in the game** (the game layer, Vol VIII): the Chord, the band of star-dust, kept and named by you (R21:121); the eight-day moon; the Octave lights' eight hues, one to a note; *The Eight Keepers, and the Sparrow Who Asked*, Constance Farthing's fable of 409, in which the eight Anchors' keepers are a Badger, a Salamander, a Tortoise, a Heron, a Cockerel, a Hare, a Magpie and an Owl (V8:1595); the seven birth signs (V8:842); the standing-stone ring outside the Hedge, "ruled canon" with "what it was ... undecided" (V8:195); the Grey Wethers, "a flock of ringing sarsens", five of them raddled for the drovers' call "three, one, four, two, five" (V8:2175); the three fold-fire hills; Ewe Barrow, "older than any name for it", with a lunula among its grave goods (V8:221, 232).
- **The sky since the Fracture.** "The year the sky went grey" (B1:5392). "The Spire of Decay's end of the sky had that faint wrongness to it that everybody had stopped mentioning" (B1:5848). Over the Rift it is "black and starless" (B3P2:143).
- **Kept clear of:** "the ninth will be born" (B4P1:514). The faith counts eight lamps on the Chord and says nothing of a ninth. The Church of the Pure Note is not contradicted, and the Synod already lets the Reach's own festivals stand, "tolerated as "agricultural custom"" (V7b:14).

## 2. The faith

**Its name: the Fold.** The hill people of the Reach say "we keep the Fold". Townspeople say "the Fold-faith" when they are being polite and "sheep-stones" when they are not. It has no priests and no house. It has **lamp-tellers**: people, mostly old, mostly drovers' and shepherds' women, who can tell the lamps, which in the Reach means both to count them and to say their stories.

**Where it comes from.** When the stairs were shut and nobody went down to the Keepers any more, the hill people, who had never been let down the stairs in the first place, went on doing the only half of it they had ever been allowed. They looked up.

**What a lamp-teller will say** (her five sayings, in the game as her lines):

1. "The Keepers are under the ground, and they are tired. We cannot go down to them. The stairs were shut before my grandmother's grandmother. So we look up, where their lamps are, and say good night to each by name. It is not much. It is what there is."
2. "Every lamp up there was lit for somebody. Nobody is lost who has been counted. That is the whole of it."
3. "They do not move. The sun goes over, and the Lantern goes over, and the five loose ones go where they like. The Fold stays where it was put. Set a stone to a lamp and it will be there for your children's children. Name me another thing you can say that of."
4. "Since the year the sky went grey the count comes up short, down toward the Spire of Decay. We count them anyway. You count the ones that are missing too. A shepherd knows that."
5. "The Church calls them sheep-stones and lets them stand. We call them sheep-stones too, when the Church is asking."

**The sky, as the Fold has it.**
- **The stars** are lamps, each lit for one of the dead. Together they are the Fold: the flock, brought in.
- **The Chord** is the road they were brought in by. (The hill people's by-name for it is the Drove; every line the player reads calls it the Chord, as you ruled.) Eight lamps on it are brighter than the rest: one at each Keeper's gate, one for each day of the week.
- **The moon** is the Lantern: the shepherd's lantern, trimmed over four nights and let down over four, the same as the week.
- **Five lamps move.** They are the loose five: what is out of the fold at night.
- **Three lamps are not lamps.** Looked at straight, they are smoke. They are the far folds: other flocks, on hills too far off to walk to.

## 3. The eight Keepers, along the Chord

Each is one of Constance Farthing's eight, in the week's own order, and each carries one of the eight bright lamps, in its note's hue.

**The Badger.** *Rootday. His lamp is the Sett-Lamp, green.*
A long low back, a blunt head, and one green lamp where the mouth of the sett would be. He is the first on the Chord and the lowest, because he is the one who says *down*. The hill people say every root in the Reach has heard him say it, and that is why roots go the way they do. He does not come out. You know he is in by the lamp. On Rootday you say good night to him first, and you say it toward the ground as well as the sky, in case.

**The Salamander.** *Hearthday. Her lamp is the Ember, red-gold.*
A bent line of six, like a poker left in the grate, with the Ember at the bend. She keeps the hearth of the world and may not let it out, so she is the only Keeper who is never cold and never asleep at once. Smiths' wives on the Downs bank the kitchen fire toward her of a Hearthday. If the Ember looks dull, they say, somebody's fire has gone out that should not have, and you go round the neighbours.

**The Tortoise.** *Massday. His lamp is the Still Stone, slate-grey.*
A ring of five with one in the middle: the shell, and what is under the shell. He holds the weight of everything and has never once shifted his feet. Drovers say that is why a stone stays where you put it. His lamp is the dullest of the eight and the steadiest; nobody has seen it flicker. Children are told that if the Tortoise ever takes a step, they will feel it in their teeth.

**The Heron.** *Stasisday. Her lamp is the Standing Eye, amber.*
Tall and thin: a long neck of four, a body, and one leg, because herons stand on one. The amber lamp is her eye. She keeps the hours by not moving through them. The longest night of the year is hers, and on it the hill people sit up with her, because it is a long watch for one bird. It is said the dial-men set the oldest stones to her eye. The stones are older than whoever said it.

**The Cockerel.** *Dawnday. His lamp is First Call, pale blue-green.*
A comb of three, a breast, a tail thrown up behind. He keeps the morning, and he is the only Keeper the Church has a use for: the sun-disc priests say his lamp is theirs. The hill people say he was crowing before there was a Church, and that he crows for the other seven as well, to tell them one more night is done. Since the sky went grey his lamp comes up pale. They say he is hoarse. They say you would be.

**The Hare.** *Driftday. Her lamp is the Scut, blue.*
Two long ears, a stretched back, and the blue lamp at the tail, which is all anybody ever sees of a hare. She keeps things going. She is drawn running along the Chord, and has been running along it for as long as there have been eyes, and has not got an inch further; and the hill people say that is what keeping is. Skyship men named their high wind for her day. They do not look up at her. They steer by ear.

**The Magpie.** *Memoryday. Her lamp is the Bright Thing, violet.*
A black-and-white bird is hard to make of stars, so she is drawn by what she carries: a wing, a tail, and one violet lamp held in the beak. She keeps what is remembered. She took one shining thing from every person who ever lived, the story goes, and has them all, and can put her foot on any one of them. People leave a button on the stone for her on Memoryday. Nobody has ever seen a button go. Nobody leaves the good buttons.

**The Owl.** *Nullday. His lamp is the Rest, grey-mauve.*
Two eyes, close together, and nothing else of him at all, because he keeps the rests: the quiet between one note and the next, which the hill people call *our* nothing. They are particular about the *our*. There is another quiet, they say, that belongs to nobody, and the Owl sits with his back to it so the rest of us need not look. Toward the Spire of Decay his lamp has been dim for seven years. Over the Rift it is not seen at all.

**The Sparrow.** *Off the Chord. Three small stars, and you have to be told.*
She is not a Keeper. She is the one who went along the Chord from gate to gate and asked each of them when they slept, and each said: *When somebody comes to say down for me.* She asked who was coming. The story does not have that part. The hill people say she is still asking, and that this is why her three stars sit a little off the road, on the side where a traveller would stand.

## 4. The seven Signs, along the sun's road

These are the game's seven birth signs, given their places in the sky. The old books call the seven together the Zodiac. With a sky that does not turn, a child cannot be born "under" a sign by the calendar, so the Fold's way is this: **the midwife goes out and looks, and whichever of the seven shows clearest when the child first cries is the child's.** Skyship men call the high current that runs under the seven the Zodiac Drift, which is the only place most of them have met the word.

A lamp-teller, of the signs: "It does not make you. It only says which door you came in by."

**The Open Door.** Four stars for the frame, and nothing in it. It is the first sign a child is shown, because anybody can see a door. The story is short. Somebody left it open. The hill people have never agreed whether that was carelessness or kindness, and have noticed that the ones born under it do not much mind which.

**The Cloister Walk.** A row of five arches, low on the road, with one bright star in the third. It is named for the walk at a house of sisters that nobody can now place, where one lamp was kept lit at the turn so the old ones would not fall. The lamp is the bright star. The rest is dark arches. Those born under it are said to be able to go a long way on one lamp.

**The Hound's Step.** Four prints in a line, and a fifth set off to one side, where it stopped and turned its head. No hound; only where one has been. Hunters say it is the track of the first hound that ever came in from the sea, and that it was put in the sky so you would know the pattern when you saw it in mud. Those born under it hear the floor go quiet before other people do.

**The Floating Light.** One bright star with a small ring of faint ones under it, like a lantern with nobody holding it. On the marsh roads they say it is a wisp, and bad. On the Downs they say it is the lamp a mother leaves in the window, seen from so far off that you cannot see the house. Both agree it is always a little higher than it ought to be.

**The Road-Song.** The longest sign: eleven stars in a wandering line that crosses a third of the sun's road and does not end in anything. Carters claim it. The song it is named for has a verse for every milestone between two towns nobody can name, and stops one verse short of home. You are meant to walk the last mile without it.

**The Mourner's Hour.** A bowed figure of six stars, beside one alone. The one alone is not the dead; the dead are in the Fold. It is the candle. She sits the whole night with it, and the hour the sign is named for is the last one before dawn. The hill people say she is not sad. They say she is staying, and that these are different.

**The Broken Fifth.** Five places in a line, evenly spaced, and four stars. The fourth place is dark. Every child shown it says, without being told, that one is missing; and that is the whole of its teaching. Since the sky went grey there are tellers who count the dark place as a lamp put out. The older ones say no: it was always so, and the sign is there to teach the difference between a lamp that has gone out and a gap that was always there.

If the traveller has a birth sign, the stone that sights it adds one line: `This is the door you came in by.`

## 5. The loose five (the wandering stars)

"The Fold does not move. Five lamps do." The hill people name them as a shepherd names what is out of the fold at night.

- **Yow.** White, and the brightest thing in the sky after the Lantern. An old ewe who knows the way home and will not come. She is seen at dusk or before dawn and never in the deep of the night, like any ewe with sense.
- **The Lamb.** Small and quick and never far from where the sun comes up. Most people have never seen it. The ones who have say it looks lost.
- **Tup.** Red. Slow to anger, and then not slow. When Tup stands in the Hound's Step the hill people bring the dogs in.
- **Old Wether.** Yellow and steady. He takes a man's whole working life to go once round the road. A child shown Old Wether in the Open Door is told: when he is back there, you will be old.
- **The Dog.** Faint and blue and always behind one of the others. Nobody has ever seen the Dog bring one in. It is the opinion of the Downs that he is not trying to; he is only seeing that none of them is out alone.

**The Grey Wethers and the five.** The five raddled sarsens already ring to the drovers' call, "three, one, four, two, five". The Fold's reading: the call is the order the loose five stand along the sun's road at the back-end of the year, and the stones were raddled so a drover with no letters could ring the sky.

## 6. The Lantern, and the three far folds

- **The Lantern** (the moon). The shepherd's lantern: trimmed over four nights and let down over four, the same as the week. The hill people do not think that is chance. When the Lantern is full the Fold is hard to count, and they say the shepherd is doing it for them that night.
- **The Far Fold** (the great tilted spiral). The nearest of the other folds. On the clearest nights you can see that it leans, the way a flock leans on a hillside.
- **The Wheel** (the small spiral seen face-on). A carter's wheel stood on its rim, as they stand one over a carter's grave. Whose, nobody says.
- **The Two Fleeces** (the pair of ragged clouds, low down). Two fleeces left on a hedge to dry by somebody who meant to come back for them.

A lamp-teller, of all three: "Other folds, on hills too far off to walk to. They have their own shepherds. On a clear night you are being looked at."

## 7. The stones

**Three kinds**, all with the Fold's mark: cup-marks pecked into the face, one cup for each star of the figure the stone is set to, and a scratched line joining them.

- **A needle-stone:** one standing stone with a hole through it at eye height. You look through the eye.
- **A notch-stone:** a tall stone and a short one a few paces apart; you stand at the short one and look over the tall one's notch.
- **A ring:** stones set round a middle stone; you stand at the middle and look over each in turn.

**Looking through one.**
- By day: `There is only sky in it by day. The stone keeps its own chart for the daytime: cup-marks pecked in its face, and a line scratched from one to the next.` and the figure's name.
- At night with too much light or cloud: `Too much light here tonight. The lamps are there. You cannot have them.`
- On a clear dark night: the sky in the stone's eye is shown large, the figure's stars drawn out and joined, and under it the figure's name, whose it is, and its story (the texts above).

## 8. For you to rule

1. **The name "the Fold"**, and "lamp-tellers".
2. **The Sparrow as a ninth figure.** She is in the fable already, and she is not called a ninth anywhere. If she sits too near "the ninth will be born", she can be left out of the sky.
3. **The Broken Fifth's dark place**, and the tellers who disagree about it.
4. **The dial-men** in the Heron's story: a hint, no more, that the oldest stones were set by reckoners from the east. The books' Tomb of the Astrolabe is not named.
5. **The ring outside the Hedge becomes the Fold's ring.** It has been "undecided" since October.
6. **The names of the loose five**: Yow, the Lamb, Tup, Old Wether, the Dog.
7. **The sky does not turn.** A stone frames its figure on any clear dark night. (If you would rather the sky wheeled and each stone lined up only at its hour, say so; it is a larger piece of work.)
