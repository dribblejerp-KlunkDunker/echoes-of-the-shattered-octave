# Echoes of the Shattered Octave — Book Three: The Hall of Carriage
## Part Two: The Organum (Chapters 5–8)

## PART TWO: THE ORGANUM

## CHAPTER FIVE
### The Last Basket

> *Names are returned at discharge.*
>
> — Null-Watch muster-roll, the column headed REMARKS, in every year since 413

*Null-Watch. Memoryday the twenty-third of Ashfall, to Nullday the twenty-fourth.*

---

The cutter came in out of the west at dusk on Memoryday, low and fast, under a black envelope with a silver scale painted on its flank, and Vane knew it for what it was before it was close enough to read.

He was on the wall above the mooring-yard with Mags, watching the light go. Below them the *Kestrel* swung on her lines at the Watch's mast with her patched bag and her three tanks, and on her foredeck the engineer sat with his back against the press and the sack on his arm. Vesper was beside him on the bitts with her coat wrapped tight round the thing that strained in it. Two-Eleven stood at the foot of the mast-ladder talking about his dog.

Mags saw the cutter first. She always saw things first. She touched his arm and pointed, and he followed her finger west along the line of the land, and there it was against the last of the sunset. A narrow hull under a narrow bag, built for speed and nothing else, with two swivel-guns on brackets at the bow and a long brass listening-horn on the stern like the bell of a trumpet. A silver scale on the envelope, the Arbiterium's mark, catching the red light.

"Equity," said Vane, reading the name on her bow through the glass as she came in. "They always name them after something they haven't got."

Mags read his lips and did not smile.

He counted the men on her deck as she came down to the Watch's second mast, fifty yards from the *Kestrel*. Black coats. Silver at the collar. The long-barrelled carbines the Justiciary carried, with the bayonets folded back along the stock. Twenty-four. Twenty-eight. Thirty-three, with the crew. And on the quarterdeck, standing very straight with his hands behind his back, looking at the walls of Null-Watch the way a man looks at a building he has been sent to condemn, a young officer in a black coat with a silver collar and no hat.

"Justiciar," said Vane. "Advance of the column. Come to post the writ at the gate for the statutory period." He lowered the glass. "Come to see the bread stops."

Mags looked at him. Then she looked down at the *Kestrel*, at the engineer on the foredeck, fifty yards from the cutter's mast. Then at Vane again.

*Him?* she signed.

"Him too," said Vane. "The writ for the *Kestrel* stands. *And is to be observed.*" He did not laugh. "Get Two-Eleven to take the engineer below. Now. Before that lad on the quarterdeck gets his glass out."

---

The Justiciar's name was Corvan Lisle, and he was twenty-six, and he was polite.

Vane would remember that most about him afterward. He came to the General's room at the top of Tower Nine at the first hour of the night with two of his men, and took off his gloves at the door. He bowed to the General as you bow to a superior officer of another service, exactly as deep as the protocol required. He did not look at Vane, who was standing by the window, or at Sable, who had come up from the Shelf in the basket at dusk and was sitting on the stool by the door with the Ledger on her knees. He looked at the General. He spoke to the General. And when 94 began to talk, quietly, about the price of lamp-oil, he looked at 94 with the faint courteous puzzlement of a man hearing a dialect he has read about and never heard.

"General Ward," said Corvan Lisle. "You'll have had the writ by chime. I'm instructed to post it at your gate and see it executed. It will be posted at first light. The statutory period runs from the posting." He drew a folded paper from his coat. "I'm also instructed to remind you of the prior writ, respecting the vessel *Drift-Kestrel*. I'm told she's at your mast."

The General looked at him. She did not tap.

"I'm told also," said Lisle, quite gently, "that provision continues to be made to the residuals at the lip. Nightly. By rope. From the buttress below Tower Nine." He looked at the paper in his hand and did not unfold it. "The writ directs that provision cease forthwith. It's been in force since its receipt. I'm sure it's an oversight."

The General lifted her hand and struck the iron over her heart. Four blows. Flat, heavy, slow.

"The General says," said 94, "*the Watch holds the wall.*"

Lisle waited for more. There was no more.

"I'm afraid I don't follow," he said.

"No," said Vane, from the window. "You wouldn't."

The Justiciar turned his head and looked at him for the first time. He had grey eyes, very clear, very young, and absolutely no fear in them at all. Vane had seen that look on boys at Fortis before their first wave. Not the absence of fear that Orrin Hale had. The other kind. The kind that has not yet been introduced to anything.

"Captain Vane," said Corvan Lisle. "I've read your file. *Reliable. Does not ask questions.*" He folded his gloves together and put them in his pocket. "I'd hoped that would still be true."

---

The Quartermaster went out to the buttress at the third hour, as he had every night for six years.

Vane went with him. He had not decided to. He had been standing at the postern at the foot of Tower Nine when the old man came out of the dark with the basket on his back and his lantern, and the old man had looked at him and not stopped. Vane had fallen in beside him the way you fall in beside a man walking to a funeral.

"They'll be at the crane," said Vane.

"I know," said the Quartermaster.

"Thirty of them. Carbines."

"I know." The old man walked on across the grey ground toward the buttress in the voiceless wind, steady, with the basket creaking on his back. "Two thousand and twenty-two, Captain. Last night was two thousand and twenty-two. Your scribe and the Cantor and the rest of you, up and down, I've knotted every one of you." He hitched the basket higher. "There's bread in this for three hundred and seventy-nine, counting the fourteen. It was four hundred and twenty-six, Fen says, till Dawnday morning. There's a blanket for the new one. Bridie's girl. My sister knitted it. I had Two-Eleven write to her for it in the summer when Bridie told me, and it came on the cart last week." He was quiet for a moment, which for a man of the Watch was a long time. "I'm not stopping, Captain. You can go back."

Vane did not go back.

---

They were at the crane.

He saw their lanterns from a hundred yards off: a ring of them on the end of the buttress where it ran out into the Rift like a pier into a sea, round the black oak gallows-frame of the crane and its wheel and its rope. Ten men in black at the crane, with carbines, and nineteen more strung back along the spine of the buttress behind them, and the Justiciar in the middle of them with his hands behind his back. Beyond them, nothing. The not-colour. The edge of the world, thirty yards off the end of the buttress, where the land stopped being there.

And between the buttress and the edge, at intervals along the lip, the Lead Brothers. Standing. Hooded. Quite still. Their faces sealed in lead.

The Quartermaster walked into the lantern-light and stopped in front of the Justiciar and set the basket down on the rock.

"Evening, sir," he said.

"Good evening," said Corvan Lisle. "You'll be the man who lowers the rope."

"I'm the Quartermaster."

"You'll know there's a writ."

"I've heard," said the Quartermaster. "I don't read. Never learned. I can write my name, and two words more, and that's the lot." He looked at the crane, at the wheel, at the rope wound on the drum with its knots. "That's my rope, sir. Begging your pardon. I'd like to get on."

Lisle looked at him for a long moment. Vane, standing at the edge of the light with his good hand in his coat on the butt of *Last Rites*, watched the young man look. He watched him see an old penal legionnaire in a lead coat with a basket of bread at his feet. He watched him consider it, carefully, the way he had been trained to consider things: as a problem in law. And he watched him come, in his clear young eyes, to the answer the law gave.

"I'm sorry," said the Justiciar. "I really am. Sergeant, cut the rope."

---

It happened very quickly. That was the thing about it, the thing Vane would never be able to make anybody understand who had not been there. Every terrible thing he had ever seen had happened quickly. Afterward he would have years to think about it, and it would have taken six seconds.

The sergeant stepped to the crane with a short-handled axe from his belt. The Quartermaster moved.

Not fast. He was sixty and he wore a lead coat. He took one step toward the crane and put his hand out, not at the sergeant, not at the axe, at the rope. Just at the rope, where it came off the drum over the wheel, as if to hold it. As if to keep it from being hurt.

The axe came down.

The rope parted at the wheel with a sound like a bowstring. The cut end whipped up and over the top of the crane and away out over the lip of the buttress into the Rift. It went down. Hundreds of feet of it, off the drum, spinning the drum, a long grey snake of hemp going over the edge into the dark with its knots.

Two thousand and twenty-two knots.

Vane watched them go. He could not help it; he counted. They went over the edge of the buttress in a blur, too fast to count, and he counted them anyway. *Two thousand, two thousand and one.* Gone into the dark of the Rift, down past the black glass, down toward a ledge three hundred feet below where three hundred and seventy-nine people were sitting round a cooking fire waiting for bread.

The Quartermaster went after it.

He did not jump. He grabbed. The last of the rope was running off the drum, and he caught it with both hands as it went. It took him. Vane saw it take him: the old man jerked off his feet toward the edge, his lead coat dragging on the rock, his heels digging and sliding. Both fists were locked on the rope, and the knots ran through them, tearing the palms, and he did not let go.

Vane was moving before he knew it. He dropped *Last Rites* back into his coat and threw himself flat on the rock and got his good hand into the back of the old man's collar. And the rope pulled, and the Quartermaster pulled, and Vane pulled, with his cracked ribs screaming, and the old man stopped. A yard from the edge of the buttress. Stretched out flat on the rock with both arms out over the drop, both fists still locked on the rope, and the rope going down into the dark taut as a bar.

"*Let go*," said Vane. "Let go of it, you old fool, it's—"

"*It's the knots*," said the Quartermaster through his teeth. "It's all the knots, Captain. It's six years. I can't. I can't—"

The shot was very loud.

---

He did not see who fired. He would find out afterward. A boy of nineteen from the Justiciary depot at Hob's Cutting, in his first year, who had been told by his sergeant to keep his carbine on the old man. He had seen the old man go toward the edge and the captain go after him, and had thought, as he would say at his court-martial, which never took place, that they were going over the edge together to get down to the residuals. He had thought it was an escape. He had been told to prevent provision.

The ball took the Quartermaster in the back, below the shoulder, through the lead coat. Vane felt it go in. He felt the old man's body jerk under his hand like a man struck with a hammer. He felt the fists open.

The rope went.

It went down into the Rift, all of it, the last of it, off the end of the buttress, gone. And the Quartermaster lay on his face on the rock with Vane's hand in his collar and his arms out over the drop and his palms open and torn and black with blood. He made a small sound. Not a word. The sound a man makes when he puts down something heavy at the end of a very long day.

Vane rolled him over.

He was alive. His eyes were open. He looked up past Vane at the sky over the Rift, which was black and starless, and then at Vane. His mouth moved.

"Did she get it," he said. "The basket. The blanket."

Vane looked at the basket. It was still sitting on the rock where the old man had set it down in front of the Justiciar, with the lid on, and the bread in it, and the knitted blanket.

"No," said Vane.

The Quartermaster closed his eyes.

---

He heard the General before he saw her.

Iron. Knuckles on iron, coming across the grey ground from the postern at Tower Nine at a run. Not one blow at a time. A rhythm. Fast, flat, hard, over and over, the same phrase. And behind it, running, more feet than he could count, and voices. The Watch. Talking. Coming out of the postern and across the ground toward the buttress, dozens of them, a hundred, in their lead coats, with their carbines and their swords and their battery-rammers, at a run. Every one of them talking, steadily, about nothing, the way they always talked, onions and days of the week and cable-eyes, a great rolling murmur of ordinary talk coming at the run across the edge of the world in the dark.

He had never heard anything so frightening in his life.

The Justiciary men turned toward it. He saw the young officer's face in the lantern-light go from puzzlement to understanding to something Vane did not think it had ever been before. He saw the carbines come up.

"*Stand*," said Corvan Lisle. Clear, young, carrying. "*Stand. Present. These men are in breach of a writ of the Arbiterium—*"

The General came out of the dark into the lantern-light at the head of her garrison. She stopped twenty yards from the crane. She stood with her feet apart and her short grey hair wet with sweat, and she looked at the Quartermaster lying on his back on the rock with Vane kneeling over him. Then she looked at Corvan Lisle.

Then she struck her breastplate. Once. A single blow, so hard that Vane heard the iron ring.

He did not need 94 to tell him what that one meant.

---

It was not a battle. Vane had been in battles. A battle had sides and a shape and a moment where it turned, and afterward you could draw it on a chart and say *here, this is where it went*. This had no shape. It was a hundred people in lead coats and thirty in black coming together on a spit of rock at the edge of the world in the dark, by the light of a dozen lanterns. The lanterns swung and fell and went out one by one.

The Justiciary fired first. A ragged volley, carbines at twenty yards, into the front of the Watch. He saw men go down. He saw a ball strike a lead coat and spark and whine away, and another go through and the man behind it fold. Then the Watch were in among them with rammers and swords and fists, and it was a brawl on a rock in the dark, and he could not see anything except bodies and lantern-light and the gleam of bayonets.

He pulled the Quartermaster back from the edge by the collar, a yard, two yards, and left him against the foot of the crane. He took out *Last Rites*. He did not know who to shoot. It was dark and everyone was moving and everyone was shouting—

No. Not everyone.

He heard it in the middle of the fight, and it went through him like cold water. The Watch were shouting. The Watch were *talking*. Every trooper on that rock in a lead coat, swinging a rammer or a sword or a fist, was talking as he fought. Steadily, breathlessly, about nothing. *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday. And then your onions. Forty-one, forty-two.* Talking through it the way they talked through everything, because they had been taught to talk the way other soldiers are taught to keep their guard up.

The Justiciary were not.

They had fired their volley, and shouted their orders, and then they were fighting for their lives in the dark on the edge of the world. And men fighting for their lives do not talk. They grunt. They gasp. They go quiet, the way men go quiet in a fight, every breath for the arm and the legs, nothing to spare for the mouth.

Thirty yards from the lip.

He saw the first one go.

---

A Justiciary trooper at the end of the buttress, nearest the edge. He had been fighting a big man in a lead coat with a rammer, bayonet against iron, and the big man had knocked him back a step. Toward the edge. Then another step. And the trooper stopped fighting.

He just stopped. He stood there on the rock with his carbine in his hands and his mouth a little open. He turned his head and looked toward the edge, toward the not-colour. Vane saw his face in the light of a fallen lantern. It was the face of the man at the far end of the Shelf, the one Lyra had told them about. Startled. Then not startled.

*Relieved.*

He lowered his carbine. He let it fall. And he walked toward the edge. Not ran. Walked, steadily, the way a man walks home at the end of a shift. Past the end of the buttress onto the bare grey ground beyond, between two of the Lead Brothers, who turned their sealed faces toward him and did not move to stop him, because he was not one of theirs. Toward the not-colour. And Vane looked away, because you could not look at it, and when he looked back the man was not there.

The big trooper with the rammer stood looking at the place where he had been. Then, without stopping talking — *and that's forty-four, and that's the south face* — he turned round and went back into the fight.

---

Vane understood it then. He understood the whole of it, the way he had understood the guns on the embankment at the Hanging Water, all at once, half-way down.

The Watch could not lose this. Not here. Not on this rock. Every one of them had stood on this wall for years with the edge of the world thirty yards away, and talked, and talked, and talked, and never once stopped. The Justiciary had been here one night. They did not know. Nobody had told them. Nobody had told them because the man who should have told them, the young officer who had read every file, had heard 94 talking about lamp-oil and thought it was a quaint provincial custom.

*Keep talking.*

He opened his mouth. He found that he had not been talking either. He had been fighting with his mouth shut, the way you fight. He had been thirty yards from the edge, kneeling by a dying man, with *Last Rites* in his hand, silent.

And he had not felt it. Not yet. But he understood, with a cold clear certainty, that he would have.

"*Two thousand and twenty-two*," said Vane aloud. Loudly. To nobody. To the rock. "Two thousand and twenty-two knots. Two thousand and twenty-two baskets. Bread and a blanket and a lantern. Six years. Two thousand and twenty-two—"

He began to count. He counted the knots. All of them. One, two, three, out loud, steadily, in the dark on the end of the buttress with the fight going on round him. He found as he counted that the cold place in the middle of him, the place he had not known was opening, closed up again like a hand.

---

He saw the rest of it as he counted. He would see it for the rest of his life.

He saw the Justiciary go. Not all of them. Not even most. The Watch killed most of them. Rammers and swords and fists in the dark, without mercy, the way you would expect from a garrison of the condemned who had just watched a man of theirs shot in the back for carrying bread. But some of them went to the edge. A man here, at the end of the buttress, who stopped fighting and turned and walked. A man there, on the rock, wounded, sitting down against the crane with his hand on his belly and his face turned toward the not-colour, very peaceful. He sat there until the big trooper with the rammer came and took him by the collar and dragged him back, still talking. Another, who simply knelt down, and put his carbine down beside him, and closed his eyes, and stopped. Vane did not see him breathe again.

*Fifty-one. Fifty-two.*

He saw Corvan Lisle.

The young Justiciar had his sword out. A narrow straight blade, a duelling sword, the kind they gave you with the silver collar. He was using it well. Vane had time to see that. He was very good, and very fast, and he had put two troopers of the Watch on the ground in the time it took Vane to count to thirty. He was fighting his way back along the buttress toward the postern, toward the mooring-yard, toward his cutter, with three of his men round him. And he was shouting orders. Clear and young and carrying. *To me. Close up. Fall back on the ship.* He had not stopped shouting.

And then he did.

He had broken through. He had cut his way out of the knot of the fight at the landward end of the buttress, and his three men with him. There was nothing between him and the postern but forty yards of grey ground, and he stopped shouting orders because there was nobody left to give them to. He drew breath to run.

And he looked back.

Not at the fight. Past it. Out past the end of the buttress, at the edge.

He stood still on the grey ground with his narrow sword in his hand, and looked. Vane, counting, kneeling by the crane, saw his face in the light of the one lantern still burning on the rock. He saw the clear grey eyes that had never been afraid of anything go wide. Then go soft.

"*Oh*," said Corvan Lisle. Quite clearly. In a tone of enormous surprise. "*Oh, it's—*"

He did not finish. His three men had stopped too.

He dropped the sword. He turned round. And he walked back the way he had come, toward the edge. Through the fight, as if it were not there, and nobody touched him, because there was something in his face that made men step back. Past the crane. Past Vane, kneeling by the Quartermaster, counting. Close enough that Vane could have reached out and caught his ankle.

He did not. He would think about that. He would think about it as he thought about the hawser and the strap and the sentence at the wheel. He was holding *Last Rites* in one hand and the Quartermaster's collar in the other, and the old man had just taken a breath, a bad one, wet. Vane did not let go of either to catch the boy who had ordered the rope cut. He watched him walk past, out to the end of the buttress and off it, between the Lead Brothers. They turned their sealed faces to watch him go and did not lift a hand.

*Seventy-three*, said Vane. Aloud. *Seventy-four.*

He did not look at the edge to see the Justiciar go. He looked at the old man's face.

---

It was over in perhaps five minutes. The Watch stood on the buttress in the light of the lanterns they had picked up and relit, talking, breathing hard, over the bodies of nineteen men in black coats. Seven of their own lay among them. Four more of their own were sitting on the rock being bound up, and every one of them was still talking. Eleven of the Justiciary were not on the buttress at all. They were on the grey ground beyond it, or past that, where nobody looked.

The General came and knelt down by the Quartermaster on the other side from Vane.

He was still alive. The ball had gone in under the left shoulder blade and not come out, and there was blood at the corner of his mouth, and his breath bubbled. Vane had heard that sound before, on the *Wave-Cutter*, in the Roaring Strait, from a man who had died before dawn. The Quartermaster's eyes were open. They found the General. And something came into his face that Vane would not have thought could be in a face that grey.

"General," said the Quartermaster.

She looked at him.

"I'm not stopping," he said. "Tell the lad. The one with the dog. Two-Eleven. Tell him. It's his rope now." He coughed, and there was more blood. "He'll have to get another rope. They took mine. Tell him to knot it. Every one. Tell him that's important. Every one."

The General nodded.

"And the blanket," said the Quartermaster. "Bridie's girl. My sister knitted it. Tell him. First basket. The blanket first."

She nodded again.

His eyes went past her, to the sky over the Rift. Black and starless. And then, for no reason Vane could see, he smiled.

"Keep talking," said the Quartermaster.

And stopped.

---

The General did not move for a long time.

She knelt on the rock beside him with her hands on her thighs. The Watch stood round in the lantern-light, talking, quietly now, lower, a long murmur of onions and days of the week, like a congregation praying in a language nobody remembered the meaning of. Vane knelt on the other side with his hand still in the old man's collar. He found he could not take it out.

Then the General lifted her right hand. She did not strike her breastplate. She laid her hand flat on the old man's chest, on the lead coat, over his heart. And she tapped there instead. On him. Slowly, with one finger. A long phrase. Vane did not know iron-cant, but he could see that it was not an order. It had the shape of something else. A name, perhaps. Several words.

94 had come up behind her. He stood at her shoulder with his face wet in the lantern-light, and when she had finished he spoke. His voice was quite steady.

"The General says," said 94, "*Halvard Greer. Of Greystairs, in the Peaks. Quartermaster of the old garrison at Null-Watch from 406. Penal legion from the spring of 413. Quartermaster.*" He drew a breath. "*Discharged.*"

The Watch stopped talking.

All of them, at once, on the buttress, in the lantern-light, at the edge of the world. A hundred people who had not been silent in six years. For one breath. Two. Three. The silence went out across the grey ground toward the not-colour like a held note. Vane felt it pull at him: the cold place opening, the warm water, the wanting to sit down.

Then the big trooper with the rammer said, very loudly, in a voice that cracked in the middle, "*Rootday.*"

And they all said it with him. *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday.* And went on.

---

He found the engineer in the mooring-yard, and the cutter taken.

He came back through the postern at Tower Six at the fifth hour, with blood on his coat that was not his and the Quartermaster's basket on his back. He had picked it up off the rock without thinking. He had carried it all the way back across the grey ground and only noticed when the strap cut into his shoulder at the foot of the stair. He came out into the mooring-yard on the west side of the Watch, and stopped.

The *Equity* was at her mast, fifty yards from the *Kestrel*, where she had moored at dusk. But she was not at rest. Her black envelope was full and straining. Her lift-tanks were open; he could hear them, a high hard hiss. Every line that had held her to the mast was gone, cut, the ends hanging. She should have been a thousand feet up and going west to report.

She was not, because the engineer was holding her.

He stood on the cobbles at the foot of the cutter's mast. Her bow mooring-hawser, a five-inch cable of tarred hemp, was wrapped round his left arm from the wrist to the elbow, three turns. The sack was off. The glass was bare, burning cyan in the dark, and the green vein in it was bright. The hawser ran from his arm up to the cutter's bow, taut as a bar, humming in the wind. And the *Equity*, with her tanks wide open and every pound of lift she had pulling, could not move him one inch.

He was not straining. That was the thing. He was standing quite still, with his feet apart on the cobbles and the hawser round his arm, and his face grey and set. The glass was not pulling against the cutter. It was simply not moving. As if the arm had been bolted to the world.

Velocity, Vane thought. The green. A drowned bell that had stopped a twelve-pound ball dead in the Valley. It was doing to the cutter what it had done to the ball. Every time she strained and began to move, the green drank the start of it, and held it, and she went nowhere.

"How long?" said Vane.

The engineer did not turn his head. "An hour," he said. His voice came out ringing, double, the Light and the bell. "Forty minutes. I don't know. They tried to cast off when they heard the shooting. I got the hawser. Vesper went aboard." He paused. "Don't tell me what happened at the crane. I heard the shooting. I don't want to know any more than that. Don't tell me anything."

Vane looked up at the cutter.

Vesper was standing in the open hatch of the *Equity*'s deckhouse, on the bow, in the light of the cutter's binnacle-lamp. She had one whole side of her white, and her coat drawn round her, and the thing in it pressing at the cloth. She looked down at him. She nodded once.

That was all.

He went up the cutter's ladder. He did not need to look. He looked anyway. He found what he had known he would find. In the deckhouse, by the chime-pair, three men sitting against the bulkhead, side by side, with two crossed cuts across each of their bellies and frost on the edges and no blood on the deck at all. One of them had his hand still on the chime-pair's key. He had not finished sending.

"Did they get anything off?" said Vane.

"Two words," said Vesper. "I heard them go. *Equity. Watch.*" She looked at the dead man's hand on the key. "Then he stopped."

"That's enough for someone."

"It's not enough for anyone," said Vesper. "Not tonight. It'll be enough tomorrow." She looked at him: the blood on his coat, the basket on his back. Her eyes went to the basket and stayed there. "Who?"

"The Quartermaster."

She did not say anything. Under her coat the thing that leaned toward the edge of the world stopped leaning, for a moment, and was still.

---

They went down to the Shelf in the Quartermaster's basket at dawn, on a new rope, to plan.

Two-Eleven lowered them. He had found a rope in the Watch's stores. Not the Quartermaster's: that was in the Rift with its two thousand and twenty-two knots. A coil of new hemp, stiff and yellow, that had never been in anybody's hands. He had spliced it onto the drum himself at the fourth hour, by lantern-light, with his big slow hands. And at the first grey of the morning he had put the Quartermaster's basket on the end of it, with the lid off, and the bread in it, and on top of the bread the knitted blanket for Bridie Cole's daughter. He had lowered it down the black glass into the Rift, talking.

Vane stood at his elbow and watched him do it. He heard what Two-Eleven said, all the way down. It was not about the dog.

It was about Halvard Greer. Everything Two-Eleven knew about him, which was not much and had been gathered over six years of talk on the wall. That he was from Greystairs. That his sister kept a bakery. That he had been quartermaster of the old garrison long before the crack, and had been condemned in the spring of '13 for striking an officer who had struck a boy, and kept on at the Watch because nobody else knew where anything was. That he had never learned to read. That he had seen a child on a ledge in the Rift one night in the summer of that year, and gone to the stores, and found a basket.

When the basket reached the Shelf and the rope went slack, Two-Eleven stopped. He looked at the rope where it came off the drum. Then he took the end of it in his big slow fingers and tied a knot. Small and hard. Pulled it tight. The first knot in the new rope.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Two thousand and twenty-three," said Two-Eleven.

Fen was at the foot of the rope when they came down.

She had seen the lantern fall. The whole Shelf had. At the third hour of the night, three hundred people sitting round a cooking fire waiting for bread had looked up at the sound of a shot on the buttress far above. Then they had seen the rope come down. All of it. Pouring down the black glass past the Shelf into the dark below, hundreds of feet of it, with no basket on the end. And somewhere in it, Fen said, they had seen the knots go by, very fast, like beads off a broken string.

She stood at the foot of the new rope in the Quartermaster's coat with the sleeves rolled back four times, and looked at Vane as he climbed out of the basket. She did not ask. She saw his face, and she did not need to.

She did not cry. Vane had expected her to cry. She stood there in the grey morning at the foot of the rope, eighteen years old and twelve, and she started to talk.

"His name was Halvard," said Fen. "Did you know that? Nobody knew that. He never told anybody. He used to write me notes in the basket, every night, very bad writing, he never learned properly. *Keep talking.* Every note. Six years. *Keep talking, Fen.* I've got them all. Nearly two thousand notes. I kept every one. In a box in my hole in the wall. Next to Orrin's tin." She was looking at the rope. "Who's going to write the notes now?"

Nobody answered her.

And then, from above, from three hundred feet up the black glass, very faint, a voice came down the new rope. A big slow voice, cracked, talking. About Halvard Greer, and a bakery in Greystairs, and a boy who had been struck, and a basket. Talking steadily, the way you talk to keep a man company, the way you talk down a rope.

Fen looked up.

She listened for a long time. Then she put her small hand on the new rope where it hung down the Rift wall beside her, stiff and yellow, and held it.

"Oh," said Fen. Very quietly. "It's the one with the dog."

He took the engineer the note at noon.

Kaelen was on the *Kestrel*'s foredeck with the sack back on his arm. Where the glass met the flesh at his shoulder the skin was rubbed raw and weeping; the hawser had dragged at the joint for an hour. He looked up as Vane came up the mast-ladder, and saw the paper in Vane's hand, and held out his own for it without a word.

Vane gave it to him.

*Take the* Kestrel *NW to the Woods' edge. Tobiah on the tanks. Lyra, Vesper, Hetty, Bettany, the Aegis, Solenne and the priest. Hide in the mist and wait. Ten days. Don't come looking. Don't ask.*

The engineer read it twice. Then he folded it once and put it in his coat.

"Ten days," he said. Aloud. In his ringing double voice. Within a foot of the glass.

Vane said nothing.

"You're going somewhere," said Kaelen. "On the cutter. You and the scribe and Mags. And you can't tell me where, because if you tell me, *he* knows." He was looking out over the rail at the walls of the Watch and the edge of the world. "And I'm going to the Woods to sit in the mist with the woman I've loved since I was eleven, and wait for ten days to find out if you're dead." He was quiet for a moment. "That's right, isn't it. That's the shape of it."

"That's the shape of it."

The engineer nodded slowly.

"The old man's listening," he said. "Right now. He's listening to me say *ten days* and *the Woods* and *the cutter*. He'll work out where you're going. He's not stupid. He'll have it by tonight." He turned his head and looked at Vane, and his face was grey. "You know that."

"I know that," said Vane.

"Then why—"

"Because it's ten days," said Vane, "and it's four to the Organum, and he'll know we're coming, and we're going anyway." He heard himself say *the Organum*. He heard it go into the glass. He did not care. It was a door, and the old man had known for a week that they would come to it. "Because everybody I know has told me that it's better if you don't know things, and they were right, every one of them, and I'm sick of it. I'm sick of sending you out of rooms." He laughed, the short one. It died three feet from his mouth. "So there it is. That's where. You can tell him. He can have it. It won't do him any good. The Watch holds the wall, and the scribe's got a key to a door, and the Quartermaster's dead, and I'm going."

The engineer looked at him for a long time.

Then, slowly, he held out his right hand.

Vane took it.

"Don't die," said Kaelen.

"I never do," said Vane. "That's the trick of it. You be somewhere else."

He went down the ladder. He did not look back. He had stopped looking back at things a long time ago.

But from the foot of the ladder he heard, behind him on the foredeck, the engineer begin to talk. Not to Vane. Not to anybody. Steadily, in his ringing double voice, to the glass on his own arm. The eight stages of a bore-head's calibration under load, and every tolerance in every stage. Seat clearance under a twelve-ton thrust. Flange torque at the third collar. Three hundred figures, one after another, aloud, so that the old man in his white room would have something to listen to that was not the sound of his friends leaving.

Vane stood at the foot of the ladder for a while and listened to it too.

Then he went to the cutter.

The Organum came up out of the grey on the morning of the fourth day. She had forgotten. She had walked its streets for three years, and lived there, and slept there, and she had forgotten. She stood at the cutter's rail with the Ledger in its oilcloth under her arm and watched it rise out of the low land east of the Woods, and it was like a word in a language she had once spoken fluently coming back to her all at once, with all its meanings.

It was a city built on a chord.

Under it, deep under the grey stone, the eight roots of the eight Anchors met. The Keynote, the old texts called it: the one place on the continent where every law could be reached at once. And the Synod had built its capital on top of it. Not as a palace or a fortress. As an *instrument*.

The towers were pipes. That was the first thing anyone saw. Hundreds of them, rising out of the low grey sprawl of the city in ranks, like the pipes of an organ standing on end. Tall and thin and grey, with narrow mouths cut in their sides near the top, and lead caps. Some were a hundred feet high and some were six hundred. They stood in rows, in tiers, in sweeping curved banks across the city from end to end. The streets ran between them in straight lines, laid out like the ranks of a keyboard. And when the wind came across the low land from the Woods it went into the mouths of the towers and they sounded. Not loudly. A low, vast, continuous breath, every pipe at once, a chord with no top and no bottom, felt in the chest and the teeth more than heard.

When the Synod spoke, the city played.

She had heard it play. Every hour, on the hour, the bellows under the Choir-Hall drove air into the ranks. The city sounded the hour in a single chord that went out across the low land for thirty miles, and every clerk in every office in the Organum looked up from his desk and then down again. On feast-days it played hymns. On the day the Edict of Symmetry was proclaimed, the old clerks said, it had played for nine hours without stopping, and three people in the lower city had bled from the ears.

She had walked to work through it every morning for three years. She had stopped hearing it in a week.

She heard it now, from the cutter's rail, from twenty miles out. The low vast breath of the pipes in the wind. And under it, so deep she was not sure she heard it at all, something else. From below. From under the city. A sound like a very large thing breathing slowly in its sleep.

She had stopped hearing that in a week too.

The *Equity* came in to the Arbiterium's mast at the eighth hour of the morning, under her own colours, and nobody fired.

The mast stood on the roof of the Arbiterium, on the north side of Registry Square, an iron spire sixty feet high with a mooring-ring at its head and a winch-house at its foot. A Justiciary clerk came out of the winch-house as Vane brought the cutter in, with a ledger under his arm and a pen behind his ear, and stood at the foot of the spire looking up at them. He looked at the silver scale on the envelope. He looked at the troopers in black lining the rail, talking. He did not look at anything else.

Sable watched him from the rail with her hands clasped behind her back. Her heart was going so hard that she could feel it in her throat. She stood quite still and let it.

Vane went down the ladder with Lisle's papers.

She watched him do it. She watched him hand the papers to the clerk at the foot of the spire with exactly the mixture of boredom and impatience of a cutter-master who has been four days in the air and wants his breakfast. She watched the clerk read them. The commission. The certificate of passage. The clerk read the letter twice. Then he looked up at the cutter.

"We had a chime from you," he said. "On Memoryday night. Two words. *Equity. Watch.* And then nothing." He made a mark in his ledger. "We wondered."

"Operator was taken bad," said Vane. "Rift-sickness. They get it at the Watch, you've heard. Went quiet on us at the key and never came back." He shrugged. "We've a new one. Took on some of the Watch's lads for crew, too. You'll hear them. They can't stop talking. It's a thing they have."

She went down the ladder into Registry Square, and it was exactly the same.

She had not known what she expected. Something. That it would look different, now that she knew what it was. That the grey stone would show it somehow, the way a face shows a fever. It did not. It was a rectangle of grey stone buildings, four storeys each, no ornament, with a brass plate by every door. The paving was grey. The sky over it was grey, the even northern grey of the Organum in Ashfall. In the windows of every building, at long desks, under lamps already lit at the eighth hour of the morning, clerks were writing.

And pigeons. She had forgotten the pigeons.

Clerks crossed the square in twos and threes with ledgers under their arms. They did not talk. Nobody in Registry Square talked in the open; it was not done. Their footsteps went across the paving, and the pipes breathed in the wind overhead, a low vast chord with no top and no bottom. And in the south-east corner of the square, under a striped canvas awning so faded you could not tell what colour the stripes had been, a coffee stall.

She stopped.

"Scribe," said Orrin Hale beside her. Low. "You're stopping."

"I know."

She went on. She had to go past it. It stood between the Office and the Hall, at the corner where the Office's south side met the Hall's east. She had gone past it every morning for three years, and stopped every morning for three years. She did not stop now.

"*Morning, miss.*"

She stopped.

Ambrose was a small round man of seventy with white whiskers and a leather apron. He had stood under that awning every morning for sixty years and served coffee to every clerk in Registry Square. He was looking at her from behind his copper urn with his round face creased in a smile. The same smile. Exactly the same.

"Haven't seen you a while," said Ambrose. "Been away?"

She looked at him.

"Yes," said Sable.

"Thought so." He was already pouring. Black, a little sugar, in a thick white cup with a chip on the rim. The way she had taken it for three years, every morning, at a quarter to the eighth hour. He had never asked her name. He set it on the counter in front of her. "Somewhere warm, I hope."

She looked at the cup.

She thought of the Shelf, three hundred feet down the black glass of the Rift. Of a line going quiet across the rock in the grey of the morning, and forty-seven people lying back into it as if into a bath. Of an old man lying on his back on the end of a buttress with his palms torn open, asking whether the blanket had got down.

"No," said Sable. "Not warm."

"Ah, well," said Ambrose. "It's not warm here either. Never is, in Ashfall." He pushed the cup an inch toward her. "Go on. On the house. Welcome back."

She picked it up. She drank it. It was exactly the same: black, a little sugar, too hot, the chip on the rim against her lip in exactly the same place. She set it down. She put a coin on the counter, one of the ones Vane had given her, and Ambrose pushed it back.

"On the house," he said. And then, past her, to Orrin Hale, with the same smile, the same creased round face: "And you, sir? You look like you could use one."

Orrin looked at him.

"I used to come here," said Orrin Hale. "Every morning. Nineteen years. Black, no sugar. You used to put it in the blue cup."

Ambrose's smile did not change.

"Did you, sir?" he said. "I'm sorry. I don't remember. So many, you know. So many faces." He reached under the counter for a cup. It was white. "Black, no sugar."

Orrin drank it standing there in the square, with the docket pinned to his breast. *SCHEDULE F. Incomplete draw. Residual to be conveyed under escort to Null-Watch and disposed of at the lip.* Ambrose wiped his counter and did not look at the docket, and did not ask.

He had never once asked anybody what they did.

There was no plate on the door of the Hall of Carriage. There was a door, of plain grey-painted oak, in the long grey front of the building, and a step. She went up the step. She put her hand on the brass knob, worn bright by thousands of hands, and turned it.

Inside it smelled of honey.

Not strongly. Faintly. Under the smells of ink and floor-polish and wet wool and coal-smoke that every building in Registry Square smelled of. A warm thin sweetness, like a hive in a wall. She had forgotten that too. She had forgotten it so completely that when it came to her she stopped on the threshold and could not, for a moment, go any further. Thirty-three, the Watch sergeant behind her, put a hand very lightly on her back.

She went in.

The first floor was Receiving. A long room under clerestory windows, with lamps lit all down it on brass arms, and a long counter like a bank's. Behind the counter, clerks at desks. In front of it, crates. Wooden crates on the stone floor in rows, nailed shut, stencilled. Some of them were stencilled *GLASS — FRAGILE*, and those were packed in straw. Some of them were stencilled with the marks of harvest-houses. *Ebb Cross. The Malting. Hollin Ford.* And those were not packed in anything, and they murmured.

She heard them as she came in. The sound the *Kestrel*'s hold made at night. Voices, hundreds of them, very faint, through the wood of the crates. Saying names. The clerks at their desks behind the counter were writing in their ledgers with their heads bent, under the lamps, in the warm faint smell of honey, and none of them looked up.

They had stopped hearing it in a week.

Orrin Hale stood beside her in the doorway and looked down the long room, at the counter and the clerks and the crates. He did not say anything. His face did not change at all. He had worked in this room for nineteen years. He had stood at that counter and signed for those crates. "He came down himself this morning," said Hester Vell. "The Registrar-General. At the seventh hour, before anyone was in. He went down to Sorting. He hasn't been down to Sorting in nine years, dear. Not since the old Registrar died. He doesn't like it down there. He says the honey gives him a headache." She picked up the needles again. Click. "And then he came up here, and he went into the corner room, and he came out again. And then he went upstairs. To the third floor. To the room at the end." Click. Click. "And then he went down to Sorting again. And he's there now. He's been there all morning. Sitting on a stool by the door of bin seven." Click. "With his hands in his lap."

She turned the row.

"I don't know what he took out of the room at the end," said Hester Vell. "I didn't see. I only saw him go up the carpet, and come down again with his hand in his pocket."

She began the new row, and her needles went click, click, click, very steady, in the quiet of the long grey room.

---

The corner room was not locked.

It was small and square, with one window, and shelves on three walls from floor to ceiling, and every shelf was ledgers. Hundreds of them. Brown calf, black buckram, grey cloth, by year and by house and by schedule. The accounts of the Office of the Catalogue since the day the Silent Veil had been absorbed into it. And on the fourth wall, on a lectern of black oak, open, a book.

It was the size of an altar-book. Larger. Two feet tall and eighteen inches wide, bound in grey leather gone dark at the edges with handling, with brass corners and brass clasps. It lay open on the lectern at a page near the middle. The page was ruled in red into columns, and the columns were filled with a small clear hand, close and regular, row after row after row.

She knew the hand. It was her own.

*The Concordance. Vol. III. 417–.*

On the shelf behind it, the first and second volumes, closed, on their sides. Third shelf on the left.

She took down the second. It was as heavy as Hester had said; she staggered, and Thirty-Three caught the corner. Between them they laid it on the floor and Sable knelt and opened the clasps and turned the pages.

*The Concordance. Vol. II. 416. The Whisper-Grove Harvest.*

Columns. The core's number, in the first. WG-40,001. WG-40,002. On and on, down the page, page after page. In the second column, the harvest-house where the press was done. In the third, the date. In the fourth, the grade. And in the fifth, the residual. Where the body went, when the name had been taken out of it.

*Plaza of the Willows, Whisper-Grove. Returned to loop.*

*The Malting, residual ward. Labour.*

*Hollin Ford, residual ward. Labour.*

*Schedule F, the lip.*

She turned the pages. She found the numbers she was looking for. WG-41,004. WG-41,005. WG-41,006.

*WG-41,005. Hollin Ford. 416. Grade: clear. Core assigned: Aegis project, Cloister of the Lost Choir. Residual: Hollin Ford, residual ward. Labour.*

*WG-41,006. Hollin Ford. 416. Grade: clear. Residual: the Malting, residual ward. Labour.*

Annet Sowerby. Six years old. Who had asked Pim Carder his name through the side of a crate. Whose body had been in the residual ward at the Malting, working. Until five weeks ago, when a scribe walked into the Malting and put one woman's name back. And the Office, very efficiently, closed the ward, and put two hundred residuals in carts, and sent them east to be disposed of at the lip.

Twenty-two had lived. Eight had gone into the quiet. Fourteen were sitting at the far end of the Shelf with their hands in their laps.

Sable knelt on the floor of the corner room with the Concordance open in front of her. She put her finger on the line, *WG-41,006*, and kept it there. She did not move for a long time.

Thirty-Three put a hand very lightly on her shoulder.

"Officer," she said. It was what they had agreed she would call her, in the Hall. "Officer. Time."

Sable closed the book.

---

It took both of the troopers to carry it. They wrapped it in Thirty-Three's black coat and slung it between them on a carbine-sling, and Thirty-Three went ahead with her carbine. Sable went down the long room of Assignment after them, past the clerks at their desks, who did not look up. Past Hester Vell at her desk under the last window, knitting.

Hester did not look up either. But as Sable passed the desk, the needles stopped. And Hester said, very quietly, to the knitting in her lap:

"Your tray's empty, dear. I'm sorry. I thought you should know before you went up."

Sable stopped.

"I won't tell anyone you were here," said Hester Vell. "I don't suppose it matters. He knows. But I won't." Click. "Go on now. They'll be waiting."

She did not look up.

Sable stood by the desk for a moment longer. Then she said, "Thank you for the tea, Hester. On the first day."

The needles went still.

"Oh," said Hester Vell, after a while. Very softly. "Oh, my dear. You remember that."

Sable did not wait to see whether she would look up. She went out of the long room and onto the stair. When she was half-way down to the first landing, she heard, from the room behind her, very faint, the needles begin again. They went on for a few stitches. Then they stopped. And they did not start again.

---

She sent the troopers down with the book. She went up.

She did not know why. Hester had told her. *Your tray's empty.* There was nothing up there for her. There was no time. Orrin and Seraphina and Mags were already in the basement, in Sorting, walking slowly toward a man sitting on a stool by the door of bin seven with his hands in his lap. Every minute she spent on the stair was a minute they were down there without her.

She went up anyway. She went up because she had to see. Because she was a scribe, and a scribe does not take another person's word for a thing she can go and look at.

She felt the carpet before she saw it.

Orrin had said she would. *You'll feel it under your feet before you see it, coming up the stair. It changes how you walk.* She came up round the last turn of the stone stair, and her boot came down on something soft, and her whole body went quiet without her asking it to. Her step shortened. Her breath dropped. She came up onto the third floor of the Hall of Carriage walking the way you walk into a church.

A long corridor. Red carpet, thick, running the length of the building. Doors on either side, shut. Oil lamps on brass brackets on the walls, lit, turned low. No windows. The honey was stronger here, much stronger, warm and close and sweet, like a hive.

And at the far end of the corridor, an open door, and a small room.

She walked down the carpet toward it. Her feet made no sound. She passed the doors, shut, with brass plates on them. *Deputy Registrar for Carriage. Audit.* She did not look at them. She looked at the open door at the end.

She came to it. She stood in it.

The room was small and square and had no window. There was a lamp on a bracket on the left-hand wall, turned low. On the floor, a red carpet. And against the far wall, the cabinet.

Walnut. Glass-fronted. Brass handles. Six trays, one above the other, pulling out like the drawers of a chest. Behind the glass, in the low lamplight, she could see the velvet. Dark red. And in the velvet, in rows, in little hollows pressed to fit them like eggs in a box, very small cores. Smaller than a thumbnail. Lilac. Faintly lit from inside, all of them, like coals banked for the night. Under each one, a tiny ivory tag.

There was a card in a brass frame on the front of the cabinet, at the height of her eyes. She did not need to read it. She read it anyway.

*Against the day.*

She opened the glass. She put her hand on the brass handle of the third tray and pulled. It slid out toward her, smooth and heavy and silent, on its runners.

---

Rows of little lilac cores in the dark red velvet. Twelve to a row, eight rows. Every one faintly lit. Every one with its ivory tag, hand-lettered.

She read the tags. She could not help it. She was a scribe; she read labels. Names she knew. Clerks from the Office. Proctors. Scribes who had come over from the Veil with her in 416, and taken the oath in the same hour, in the same room, and had a little fork put to the backs of their necks while they said it. She had not known. None of them had known.

Near the middle of the tray, in the fourth row, there was a hollow in the velvet with no core in it.

Just the hollow. The shape pressed into the dark red pile where something small and round had lain for three years. And under it, the ivory tag, still there, still lettered.

She read it.

It was a name. It was a woman's name, two words, written in a fair round hand, the hand of the clerk who had lettered all the tags. It was her name. She knew that it was, because Orrin had told her where it was, and because the hollow above it was empty, and because Hester had said *your tray*. She read it three times. She said it in her head, slowly, each syllable.

It meant nothing to her at all.

It was a name. A woman's name. Somebody's. She had read seventy-four thousand names in the plazas of Whisper-Grove and every one of them had meant more to her than this. She looked at it and waited for something to happen. Some recognition. Some door to open somewhere inside her, onto a room she had forgotten she had.

Nothing happened.

She had spent it. In the harvest-house at the Malting, five weeks ago, to put a woman's name back in a woman. The name itself. Not the word: the *thing*. The self that had answered to the word. She had paid it out, and it was gone, and the word was only a word, in a fair round hand, on a scrap of ivory under an empty hollow in a velvet tray.

And the copy, the little lilac core that would have held what the word had once meant, was in a man's pocket. In the basement. On a stool by the door of bin seven, with his hands in his lap.

*Against the day the scribe may need reminding of her duty.*

She stood in the small room at the end of the red corridor and understood, quite coldly, what the Registrar-General was waiting downstairs to do.

---

She did not take anything for herself. There was nothing to take.

But she pulled out the fourth tray.

She did it before she had decided to, the way she had once burned a dead Prior's letter. She slid the third tray shut and pulled out the fourth, and it came toward her heavy and silent on its runners. Rows of little lilac cores in the dark red velvet. And in the second row, at the end, a core that was different from the others.

It was not lilac. It was grey. A dull, dead, cloudy grey, like a pearl that has been in the ground. And it was not lit. Every other core in the cabinet glowed faintly from inside, banked coals, and this one did not. And it was not whole. There was a fine bright crack across it, from one side to the other, like the crack in a frozen pond.

The tag under it said *Hale, O. Tally, Receiving. 416.* And under that, in a different hand, red ink: *Incomplete. Retained as example.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she took it out of the velvet. It was cold. Much colder than it should have been, cold enough to ache in her fingers. She closed her hand round it. Through her palm, very faint, she felt something in it move. Not a voice. Not a name. Something smaller and harder and more urgent than a name. Something that wanted, very badly, to get away.

She put it in the inside pocket of her coat, against her breast, beside the Ledger.

She slid the fourth tray shut. She closed the glass.

And the Organum began to play.

---

It came up through the floor first, as a shudder in the soles of her boots, though that was not where it would take hold. A shudder, deep and slow, like a ship's hull when the engines take hold. Then through the walls. Then through the air of the small room, until the glass of the cabinet hummed in its walnut frame and the little lilac cores in their velvet hummed with it.

Then the sound.

Every pipe in the city at once. Every tower, every rank, from the hundred-footers in the lower city to the six-hundred-foot giants round the Choir-Hall. Not the hour. She knew the hour; she had heard it ten thousand times. This was not the hour. This was a chord she had heard only once in her life, in her first month at the Office, at a drill. It was the deepest the city could sound, and the widest, every note in every rank at full breath at once, so loud that it did not sound like sound. It sounded like the air turning solid. The lamp on its bracket on the wall flickered and nearly went out. Somewhere below her, through two floors of stone, glass broke.

The Lock.

She had heard the old clerks talk about it. *When the Synod speaks, the city plays.* And when the Synod wishes nobody to leave the Organum, the city plays the Lock. Every door in the city that is keyed to a Signature shuts and will not open. Every gate. Every mooring-ring on every mast. And anyone who is not of the Organum, whose own pitch is not tuned to the Keynote by years of living over it, feels it in the bone. Feels it go in at the ears, and down, and take hold, and hold them where they stand. Like a hand on the back of the neck.

She felt it take hold.

It was not pain. It was weight. As if the air had become water and she was standing at the bottom of it. She could move. Slowly. Every movement like moving through cold syrup. She could feel her own heart labouring against it.

She thought of Mags. Who could not hear it.

She thought of the man on the stool in the basement with her name in his pocket, who had waited all morning for the sound of the building's door, and had heard it, and had then waited while she went to the second floor and the third. Who had let her see the empty hollow before he rang for the Lock.

Because he wanted her to understand, before he used it, exactly what it was.

She turned round. Slowly, through the weight of the chord. She went out of the small room and down the long red corridor, through the dark and the honey and the sound. Toward the stair, and the basement. Toward the man on the stool, who had taught her to rule a page.

She did not hurry. She could not have hurried if she had tried.

She went down.

## CHAPTER SEVEN
### Sorting

> *Grade by ear. CLEAR: the name is given at once. FAIR: the name is given with hesitation. POOR: the name is not given, or is given wrongly. Poor cores are returned to stock.*
>
> — Office of the Catalogue, *Instructions to Sorters*, the second paragraph, framed on the wall at the foot of the basement stair

*The Hall of Carriage, the Organum. Stasisday the twenty-eighth of Ashfall.*

---

Seraphina Aubade had been taught that the soul has a sound. She had been taught it at six years old, in the Basilica school at Sol-Luminary, on a bench under the great east window, by a Cantor of the Pure Note with a tuning-rod and a voice like a bell. Every soul, the Cantor had said, holds one true note. Sin is the note going flat. Grace is the note held. And the Pure Note, sounded at the Altar every dawn by the Church's choirs, is the note that all souls were made to match: the note of God, which the faithful hold, and the faithless forget.

She had believed it. She had believed it with her whole body for seventeen years. She had carried the bone of the god at her back in a scabbard of white leather because she believed it. She had arrested people for singing the second note.

She went down the stone stair into the basement of the Hall of Carriage at the eighth hour of the morning in a Justiciary cloak over her plate, with the Sun-Blade wrapped in black canvas on her back. And at the foot of the stair she smelled the honey, and heard the souls.

---

It was a cellar. That was what she would remember first, afterward, when she tried to describe it to her sister and found she could not. It was not a dungeon or a pit or anything from a sermon about the punishment of the wicked. It was a cellar. Long and low and vaulted, brick, whitewashed, clean. It went back under the Hall for the whole length of the building, a hundred yards, two hundred, further than the lamplight reached. And it was full of racks.

Wine-racks. That was what they looked like. Racks of dark oiled wood from the floor to the curve of the vault, in rows, with aisles between them just wide enough for a person to walk. Each rack was a lattice of small square pigeonholes, hundreds of them. And in every pigeonhole, lying on a little pad of grey felt, a core.

Lilac. Faintly lit from inside. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, row after row, aisle after aisle, glowing softly in the dim like the windows of a city seen at night from a long way off.

And every one of them murmuring.

She heard it as she came off the last step. Not loud. Never loud. A sound like a congregation praying under its breath in a very large church. Thousands of voices, each one tiny, each one saying the same thing over and over, very fast, very quietly, so as not to forget it. A name. Its own name. Tens of thousands of names at once, overlapping, in the dim and the honey and the cold. They made a single low continuous whisper that filled the vaulted cellar from end to end, the way the sea fills a cave.

She stood at the foot of the stair and could not move.

"Knight," said Orrin Hale beside her. Low. Pleasant. "You're stopping."

"Yes," said Seraphina.

"Everybody does," said Orrin. "The first time. I did. I worked upstairs nineteen years and I came down here once, in '15, with a tally, and I stopped just there. Where you are." He looked down the long cellar, at the racks and the glow. "And then I went back upstairs and I never came down again. I sent the boy." He was quiet for a moment. "I'd like to say I couldn't bear it. I could. That's the thing. I could bear it fine. So I didn't come down, because if I'd come down every day I'd have stopped hearing it, and I didn't want to stop hearing it. That was the only thing I could do. Not come down." He shifted the tin box under his arm. "Come on. They're expecting us."

---

There were people working in Sorting. She had not expected that, though Orrin had told her. Somehow she had imagined it would be empty. Machines. A place without hands.

It was not. Along the near end of the cellar, under the lamps, there was a long table of scrubbed deal. On it, crates. Wooden crates like the ones in the *Kestrel*'s hold, with their lids prised off and leaning against them. And at the table, on stools, a dozen people in grey linen smocks with grey felt gloves on their hands, sorting.

She watched them do it.

A woman of fifty with her grey hair under a cap reached into an open crate. She took out a core in her gloved hand. She held it up to her ear, the way you hold a shell up to hear the sea. She listened, with her eyes half-closed and her head on one side, for perhaps three seconds. Then she took a stick of chalk from the table and made a mark on the felt pad, *C*, and laid the core on it. She passed it to the boy at her elbow, who carried it away down an aisle between the racks and put it in a pigeonhole. Then she reached into the crate for the next.

Held it up. Listened. *C.*

The next. Listened. Her brow creased. Listened again. *F.*

The next. Listened. Listened again. Put it down on the table and picked it up and listened a third time. She shook her head very slightly, the way you shake your head at a child who has got a sum wrong. She did not chalk the felt. She laid the core in a shallow tray at her elbow. The tray was stencilled on its side in neat black letters: *RETURN TO STOCK*. There were eleven cores in it already.

*Poor cores are returned to stock.*

Seraphina looked at the tray.

At the end of the table there was a machine. She saw it now. Small, brass, on a cast-iron stand, about the size of a coffee-mill. It had a hopper on top and a crank on the side and a little drawer underneath. A young man in a grey smock was turning the crank, slowly, steadily. The tray of poor cores was emptied into the hopper. And under the hopper, from inside the brass, came a sound. A small, dry, crunching sound, like a mill grinding sugar.

And from the little drawer underneath, as he turned, a fine bright lilac powder.

Seed. She understood it without anybody telling her. The press would not set a name in glass without something to set it round. The trench off Cape Dissonance sent forty bottles a month, and that was not enough. It had never been enough. So the Office used what it had. The cores that could not say their own names clearly enough to be worth assigning to a Legion or an engine went into the hopper, and the young man turned the crank. And what came out of the little drawer was put into the next press, at the next harvest-house, to give the next name something to set around.

The young man at the crank was humming. Very softly, under his breath, a tune. She knew it. It was Hymn 9.

---

She felt her hand go to the bundle on her back. She did not tell it to. She felt it close on the hilt of the Blade through the black canvas.

Orrin's hand came down on her wrist. Lightly. It was quite steady.

"Not yet," said Orrin Hale. "Look."

She looked where he was looking. Not at the table. Past it.

At the far end of the long cellar, beyond the racks, two hundred yards away in the dim, there was a wall. In the wall a door, iron, painted grey, with a heavy lock and a small square grille at the height of a face. A lamp hung on a bracket above it. Beside the door, at its right hand, a tall iron stand like a music-stand, and on it, slung horizontal from two hooks, a bar of dark metal as long as a man's arm.

And beside the bar, on a plain wooden stool, a man was sitting with his hands in his lap.

He was small. Not short; small, the way a sparrow is small. Neat. Sixty, perhaps, in an Office coat of very good grey cloth, with a white stock at his throat and spectacles on his nose. His hair was thin and grey and combed flat. His face was soft and pink and composed, the face of a man who has never in his life had to raise his voice to be obeyed. His hands lay folded in his lap. The fingers of the right hand, Seraphina saw even from that distance, were stained with ink to the second knuckle, the way Sable's were.

He was looking at them. He had been looking at them since they came off the stair. He did not get up.

"Mr Hale," he said.

He did not raise his voice. It carried anyway, the whole length of the cellar, over the murmur of the racks. Soft and dry and courteous, like a pen moving on paper.

"Good morning. You were in the fourth tray, I think." He tilted his head very slightly. "And you'll be the Aubade girl. The Consecrated. The one who put the Blade down." He looked at Mags, at the troopers. "And the deaf one. Yes. I see." He folded his hands a little tighter in his lap. "Do come down. We'll wait for her together, I think. She'll be along presently. She'll have gone upstairs first. She always did the second floor first."

---

They went down the aisle. There was nothing else to do.

Between the racks, in the dim, with the cores glowing on either side of them at the height of their faces. Seraphina could see the names on the little brass tags tacked under each pigeonhole. WG-40,118. WG-40,119. Hollin Ford, 416. Ebb Cross, 417. Grade C. Grade F. The murmur on either side so close now that she could hear individual voices in it. A man, saying a name she did not catch, over and over. A woman. A girl—

She did not let herself hear the girl. She looked straight ahead at the small neat man on the stool, and walked.

The three troopers walked behind her. Nineteen, a grey woman of fifty with a scar through one eyebrow. Sixty, a lean silent man with a face like a closed door. And Eighty-Eight, a boy of nineteen with a big nose and enormous hands, who had been sent to the Watch for stealing a horse. He had talked all the way from the Watch to the Organum about his mother's chickens. They were all talking now, very low, under their breath, the way the Watch talked. *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday.* It was the only sound in Sorting that was not a name.

Mags walked beside Orrin. She had her carbine in her good hand and her strapped arm across her chest, and her eyes on the small man's mouth.

The sorters at the long table did not look up as they passed. The woman with her grey hair under the cap held a core to her ear and listened and chalked it *C*. The young man at the end turned the crank of the brass mill, humming.

They stopped ten yards from the stool.

The Registrar-General of the Office of the Catalogue looked up at them with his soft pink face. Seraphina saw that his eyes behind the spectacles were pale blue, and very tired, and very kind.

"Pellam Quist," he said. "I don't think we've been introduced, Dame Seraphina. I knew your father. A great servant of the Church. He sent us a great many people." He nodded, very slightly, past her, back up the cellar toward the long table. "From the Basilica. For correction. You'll find the crates stencilled with the sun-disc, if you look. There are some on the table now. I believe you arrested several of them yourself."

She did not look.

She did not need to look. She had seen the crates on the table as she passed, and had not let herself read them, and she had read them anyway. White sun-discs, stencilled on the wood, the Church's mark. *Basilica of the Pure Note. For correction.*

She had arrested people for singing the second note. Seven of them, in her first year as a Consecrated. She had taken them to the Basilica, and they had been sent for correction. She had never asked where.

"Yes," said Pellam Quist gently, watching her face. "I thought you hadn't known. Very few of them do."

---

Orrin Hale set the tin box down on the stone floor at his feet.

"Registrar-General," he said. Pleasantly. "You'll know why we're here."

"Bin seven," said Quist. "The incomplete draws. Yes." He did not look at the iron door beside him. "I've been sitting by it all morning, Mr Hale. I came down at the seventh hour. I don't like it down here, you know. The honey gives me a headache. But I thought it was right that I should be here myself, for this. I didn't think it should be left to a clerk."

"For what?" said Orrin.

The Registrar-General looked at him with his pale tired kind eyes.

"Reconciliation," he said.

He unfolded his hands. He laid the right one, the one with the ink on it, very lightly on the iron bar slung on its stand beside him. Not gripping it. Resting on it. The way you rest your hand on the head of a dog.

"Incomplete draws are retained against need," said Pellam Quist. "That's the rule. I wrote it myself, long ago. The Office does not throw away a voice. Even a broken one. You never know when one might be wanted." He stroked the bar, very lightly, with one ink-stained finger. "But the need has changed. There's a writ. You'll have seen it; you've come from the Watch. When the residuals at the lip are disposed of, as the writ directs, the incomplete draws in bin seven will have nothing left to be reconciled *with*. Nobody to go home to. They become surplus." He took his hand off the bar and folded it back in his lap. "Surplus is reconciled. That's a rule too."

"You were going to do it in twelve days," said Orrin Hale. "When the writ fell."

"Yes."

"And now?"

"Now," said Pellam Quist, "you've come to take them home. You'll have the dockets, I expect. Mr Hale never threw anything away." He looked at the box at Orrin's feet. "Yes. There they are. And if you take them home, Mr Hale, and they go back into the people they came out of, then the writ cannot fall cleanly. Because there will be no residuals on that shelf of yours. There will be people. And a writ for the disposal of residuals cannot dispose of people." He spread his hands, a small apologetic gesture. "You see the difficulty."

---

Seraphina saw it. She saw all of it, at once, the way she had once seen a line of Flambeaux on the Pilgrim Chain and known what would happen next. The small kind man on the stool. The iron bar on its stand. The grey door beside it with its grille, and behind the grille, very faint, a sound. A murmur, like the racks. But not like the racks. Broken. Ragged. Voices that started a name and stopped half-way and started again. Hundreds of them. Saying half a name, over and over, in the dark, and never reaching the end.

*Bin seven.*

"The bar," said Orrin Hale. Not to Quist. To her, low, without turning his head. "It's a reconciling bar. Tuned. Cracked glass has a flaw, and a flaw has a pitch. Strike the bar and it rings at the pitch of the flaw. Every cracked core on the same iron breaks along the crack. It carries through the metal: the stand's bolted to the door, and the door's hung on the frame that holds the shelves." His voice did not change. "I've seen it done. Once, in '15, for a crate of incomplete draws from Ebb Cross; that was the tally they sent me down with. One stroke. It took about four seconds."

"Yes," said the Registrar-General. "Thank you, Mr Hale. That's quite right."

Seraphina's hand was on the Blade.

"I wouldn't," said Pellam Quist. He did not move. He did not look at her hand. "Dame Seraphina. I really wouldn't. Not yet." He lifted his own right hand from his lap. In it, between his ink-stained finger and thumb, there was a small hammer, the kind a jeweller uses, with a head of polished brass. He held it up to the lamp. "I'm sixty-three. My hand isn't what it was. But I can strike a bar from where I'm sitting before you can get that thing out of its canvas. And then you'll have killed an old man for nothing, and the bin will be gone anyway." He lowered the hammer to his knee. "Let's wait for her. Please. I've waited all morning. I'd like her to be here."

And as he spoke, from somewhere above them, through two floors of stone, the Organum began to play.

---

Seraphina had been under the Bleach. She had stood at the Altar on Mount Lumina with two hundred throats of the Pure Note round her, and the god's own light coming off the Spire. She thought she knew what it was to be held by a sound.

She had not known.

It came down through the vault of the cellar like water through a roof. Deep and wide and total, every note at once, the whole city playing at full breath. The floor shuddered with it under the soles of her feet, and the plates of her armour rang. But it was at her ears that it came in. It came in at her ears and went down, into her chest, her spine, her hips, her knees. It took hold. Not of her body, exactly. Of the thing in her body that moved it. As if the air in the cellar had turned to cold syrup and she was standing at the bottom of it.

She could move. Slowly. Every movement like moving underwater in full plate. Her hand on the hilt of the Blade, through the canvas, felt as if it weighed a hundredweight.

Around her, in the dim and the honey, everything had slowed. The three troopers had stopped talking. They were standing in the aisle with their mouths moving very slowly, the words coming out of them long and drawn, *Rooot-daaay*. Their faces were straining. Orrin Hale stood with his hands at his sides and his head a little bowed, as if he were walking into a wind. The sorters at the long table, back up the cellar, were sitting quite still on their stools with their gloved hands in their laps and their eyes closed. Not held. Waiting. They were of the Organum, they had lived over the Keynote all their lives, and the Lock did not hold them. It only told them to sit still.

And on the stool by the iron door, Pellam Quist sat with his hammer on his knee and his eyes on the foot of the stair, two hundred yards away, and did not seem to feel it at all.

Mags moved.

She was the only one. Seraphina saw her. The bosun took two steps forward down the aisle at an ordinary walking pace, as if the cellar were full of air and nothing else. She stopped, and looked round at the rest of them standing held in the syrup of the sound, and Seraphina saw her understand. Mags could not hear the city play. It went in at the ears and down and took hold, and Mags had no ears for it to go in at. She was standing in the middle of the Lock in the basement of the Hall of Carriage, and she was free.

She looked at the Registrar-General.

He looked back at her. For the first time something moved in the soft pink face. Not fear. Interest.

"Ah," said Pellam Quist softly. "Yes. The deaf one."

---

Sable came down the stair.

Seraphina saw her at the far end of the cellar, two hundred yards back, coming down off the last step into the lamplight. Slowly. So slowly. Every step a labour, through the weight of the chord. Her grey Office coat. The Ledger under her arm in its oilcloth. Her face white and set. Behind her, slower still, the two troopers who had gone up with her. They were carrying something between them, slung on a carbine, wrapped in a black coat, heavy. Behind them Thirty-Three, with her carbine, her mouth moving long and slow.

Sable did not stop at the foot of the stair. She did not stop at the long table, or at the crates with the sun-discs on them, or at the brass mill. She came down the aisle between the racks through the glow and the murmur and the Lock, one step and then the next. Her eyes were on the small man on the stool.

He watched her come. He did not get up.

When she was twenty yards off, he reached into his coat with his left hand. He took something out. He held it up to the lamp above the iron door between his finger and thumb.

A core. Very small. Smaller than a thumbnail. Lilac, faintly lit.

"Good morning, my dear," said Pellam Quist. "You've been to see the tray, I think. I'm sorry. I wanted you to know what this was before I used it. It didn't seem fair otherwise."

Sable stopped.

"I taught you to rule a page," said the Registrar-General. "Do you remember? Your first week. You'd come over from the Veil and your margins were all wrong. A Veil margin, an inch and a half, so much wasted paper. I sat beside you at Hester's desk and showed you. Three-quarters of an inch, and a double rule in red." He turned the tiny core in his fingers. "You were the best I ever had. The harvest schedule. The routing. F/3. *No further entry is required.* I've had it framed, you know. It's in my office. I show it to the new ones."

Sable did not say anything.

And Pellam Quist put the little lilac core to his lips, as if he were going to kiss it. He said, very softly, a name.

Two words.

Seraphina did not hear what they were. She would never know. She heard the shape of them, a woman's name, soft and dry and courteous. And she saw Sable stop.

Not stop walking. She had already stopped walking. *Stop.* All of her, at once, the way a clock stops. Her face went smooth. Her eyes went empty. Her hand on the Ledger under her arm went loose. She stood in the aisle between the racks in the glow and the honey with her mouth a little open and her eyes on nothing, and she did not breathe.

"I'm sorry, my dear," said Pellam Quist. "Against the day."

He lifted the jeweller's hammer.

"Reconcile," he said, and struck the bar.

---

It rang.

Seraphina felt it before she heard it. It was not loud, not against the Lock. A thin clear note, high, glassy, very pure, like a fingernail drawn round the rim of a wineglass. It went out from the bar on its stand, and through the iron door beside it, and into bin seven.

And bin seven broke.

She heard it through the grille. She would hear it for the rest of her life. A sound like ice breaking on a pond in the spring, a long spreading crackle, starting at the door and running back into the dark. And under it, *in* it, the voices. The broken voices that had been saying half a name over and over behind the grille. She heard them stop. Not all at once. In a wave. One after another after another, running back from the door into the dark of the bin the way a crack runs across ice. Each one starting its half-name, and getting a syllable out, and stopping. With a tiny bright sound like a thimble dropped on stone.

*Ma—* crack. *Hol—* crack. *Ed—* crack.

Back and back and back.

Orrin Hale made a sound.

She had never heard him make a sound. Not on the Shelf, not in the cutter, not walking along the lip of the Rift with his boots half over the drop. He made one now. It was not fear. He had none. It was something underneath fear, older, that the press had not reached. He took a step toward the iron door through the syrup of the Lock, and another.

The wave ran on into the dark of the bin. The bar on its stand went on ringing. Thin and high and pure, it hung in the air of the cellar and did not fade. A tuned bar, struck once, would ring for a long time.

*A minute*, Seraphina thought, with the coldness of the Order. *More. Long enough to reach the back of the bin. Long enough to—*

Mags put her arm round the bar.

---

Seraphina saw her do it. She saw it the way she had seen the knight on the Pilgrim Chain step under the cage. A thing so simple it did not look like a decision at all.

The bosun had been standing in the aisle, free in the Lock, with her eyes on Quist's mouth. She had watched him say the name and seen Sable stop. She had watched him lift the hammer. She could not hear the bar ring. She could not hear bin seven breaking.

But she could feel it. Seraphina understood that afterward. Mags had spent her whole life reading the world through her skin. The deck through her feet. The signal line through her palm. A ship's mood through the wood of the rail. And the stone floor of Sorting had begun to shiver under her boots, very fine and fast, the way a deck shivers when a line is about to part. She had looked at the bar on its stand, and seen it trembling, a blur at its edges. She had understood what it was doing the way she understood a line.

She dropped her carbine. She stepped up to the stand, past the Registrar-General on his stool, who did not move. She wrapped her one good arm round the ringing bar and pulled it in against her chest. Against her breastbone, against the strapped arm, against her whole body, as hard as she could. The way you put your hand flat on a bell to stop it.

Seraphina saw her face go white.

The bar was ringing at the pitch of a flaw. The bosun was holding it against her body with all the strength she had, and the ring went into her. Into her arm, her chest, her ribs, her teeth. Seraphina saw it go. She saw Mags's whole body shake, fine and fast, like a struck wire. She saw her eyes squeeze shut and her lips peel back from her teeth. She saw the blood come out of her nose, sudden and bright, and run down over her mouth and chin.

Mags held on.

And the ring began to die. Slower. Thinner. The thin high glassy note in the air of the cellar wavered, and broke, and was gone.

Behind the grille of the iron door, the long spreading crackle of ice stopped.

Somewhere in the dark of bin seven, beyond where the wave had reached, a broken voice began, very faintly, to say half a name again.

---

Pellam Quist looked at the bosun with his arms round his bar and her face covered in blood.

"Oh," he said. Quite softly. As if someone had told him a fact he had not known. "Oh, that's clever."

He lifted the hammer again.

Seraphina drew the Blade.

---

She would not remember drawing it. Not the movement of it. Her hand was on the hilt through the canvas, and the canvas was in the way. The Lock was in her arm and her shoulder like cold iron, and she had perhaps a second and a half before a jeweller's hammer struck a bar. She would remember only the thing she said, not aloud, to the bone of the god at her back. She had never said it before and did not know where it came from. *Please.* And then the canvas was gone, torn, and the Blade was in her hand, out, in the basement of the Hall of Carriage, and the light went to it.

All of it.

Every lamp in Sorting. Every lamp on every bracket, the whole length of the cellar, two hundred yards of them. The light came off them the way it had come off the Pilgrim Chain, the way mist comes off water, and streamed down the aisles between the racks toward her and the Blade in her hand. The lamps went dark. Not out; the flames still burned, she could see them, small blue points on their wicks. But dark. Their light drawn off them and pulled down the cellar into the god's bone in her fist.

And the cores.

She had not thought of the cores. Tens of thousands of them, glowing faintly in their pigeonholes, lilac, like the lights of a city at night. Their glow came off them too. All of it. Their faint inner light rose out of them like breath on a cold morning and streamed down the aisles, a river of lilac through the dark, into the Blade. The racks went black. The whole of Sorting went black, from end to end, two hundred yards of vaulted cellar, in a single instant. Utterly, absolutely black.

Except the Blade.

It blazed in her hand like a struck sun. Gold and white and lilac, all at once, every light in the room in it. It lit nothing. It lit only itself, and her hand on the hilt, and her face above it. It was so bright that she could not see anything else at all. Everything round her was a wall of black.

And in the black, the light went round her. It parted round her as it always had, at the Foundry and on the Chain. She stood in the middle of it untouched, holding a sun.

She heard Pellam Quist cry out. A short, sharp, startled sound, the sound of a man who has been looking at a lamp and has had it taken out of his eyes. She heard the jeweller's hammer strike something. Not the bar. Stone. The floor. A small bright ringing clink in the dark.

He had missed.

He could not see. Nobody could see. Nobody in Sorting could see anything but a blazing point of light in a wall of black that hurt to look at, and they could not look away from it.

Except one.

---

She felt Orrin Hale go past her in the dark.

She did not see him. She felt him. A big slow body moving past her at a steady walk through the black and the Lock, toward the stool, toward the voice that had cried out. He walked as he walked along the lip of the Rift: without hurry, without hesitation, as if the dark were a garden path. He had walked into the Hush for an old woman and lost a boot. He walked now into a blinding black cellar toward a man with a hammer, and Seraphina heard him reach the stool.

She heard a scuffle. Short. A soft dry cry. A small sound of something light hitting the stone floor and rolling. And then Orrin Hale's voice, quite close, quite calm, in the dark.

"He's down. I've got his hands. Knight. Take the light off. I can't see what he dropped."

She lowered the Blade.

She did not sheathe it. She lowered the point to the floor, slowly, through the weight of the Lock. And she did what she had never had the patience to do on the Pilgrim Chain. She did not drop the light. She let it go. Slowly, a little at a time, the way you pay out a line. She felt it go out of the bone and back down the aisles, to the lamps on their brackets and the cores in their pigeonholes. The lamps came back. Dim, then less dim. The racks came back, glowing lilac, row on row. The cellar came back out of the dark.

Pellam Quist was lying on his face on the stone floor beside his stool. Orrin Hale knelt on his back with one knee, holding both his wrists behind him in one big hand, as calmly as a man holding a parcel. The Registrar-General's spectacles had come off and lay on the floor a yard away. His face was turned sideways on the stone. His pale tired eyes were open, blinking, streaming with tears from the light.

Mags was on her knees by the stand with the bar still clutched against her chest, and her face covered in blood, and her eyes shut.

And Sable was lying in the aisle where she had stood.

She had fallen. When Quist went down and stopped speaking, the name had let go of her. She had dropped where she stood, on the stone floor between the racks, in her grey coat, with the Ledger fallen open beside her. She was on her side. She was breathing. Seraphina could see her breathe. Her eyes were open, and moving.

"Scribe," said Orrin Hale. "Scribe. Can you hear me?"

Sable's lips moved. Nothing came.

Then, very faintly: "*Where is it.*"

Orrin looked at the floor round the stool. At the stone. At the jeweller's hammer lying where it had fallen, and the spectacles. And at the foot of the nearest rack, three feet away, where the lowest row of pigeonholes came down to within an inch of the floor.

"He dropped it," said Orrin Hale. "When I took his hands. I heard it go. It rolled." He looked along the foot of the rack, along the row of pigeonholes. Every one of them held a little lilac core on a felt pad, glowing faintly, murmuring a name. Hundreds in that rack alone. Thousands in the aisle. Tens of thousands in Sorting. Every one the same size, the same colour, the same faint light.

Except that hers was smaller. Smaller than a thumbnail.

"It rolled under the rack," said Orrin Hale. "Or into it. I don't know."

---

They looked for it for three minutes. It was all the time they had.

Seraphina would remember those three minutes, afterward, more clearly than anything else in Sorting. Not the honey, not the racks, not the bar. Five people on their knees on the stone floor of a cellar at the bottom of the Hall of Carriage, through the weight of the Lock, with their faces to the floor. Peering under the foot of a rack. Running their hands along the dusty stone in the dark beneath it. Pulling cores out of the lowest pigeonholes one by one and holding them up to the lamp to see if one was smaller. The murmur of the racks going on and on above them, tens of thousands of names. And somewhere in it, very small, very faint, perhaps, one more. A woman's name. Two words. That nobody could hear, because it was saying itself in a voice the size of a thumbnail, among ten thousand others, in the dark.

Sable did not look. She sat on the floor of the aisle with her back against a rack and the Ledger in her lap and watched them. Her face was quite smooth. She did not help.

"Leave it," she said, after the third minute.

Orrin looked up at her from the floor.

"Leave it," said Sable. Her voice was very thin. "It's a unit. It's in Sorting. It's been sorted." She closed the Ledger. "Every name is a unit. He's always said so. He'll be very pleased."

On the floor under Orrin's knee, the Registrar-General of the Office of the Catalogue began, very softly, to laugh.

---

Then the proctors came.

Seraphina heard them before she saw them. Boots on the stair at the far end of the cellar, many of them, coming fast. Free in the Lock, because they were of the Organum and it did not hold them. She knew the sound of men coming down a stair to take somebody. She had been one of them, often enough.

"Proctors," said Orrin Hale. Pleasantly. "That'll be the Lock, ringing them in. Thirty of them, I'd think. Felted rods and hand-forks. They'll want the scribe. And him." He looked down at the man under his knee. "And they'll want us, after."

Seraphina looked at bin seven.

The iron door was still locked. Beyond the grille, very faint, in the dark at the back where the wave had not reached, broken voices saying half a name.

"Mr Hale," she said. "The door."

Orrin let go of Quist's wrists. He reached into the Registrar-General's coat, unhurried, and took out a key on a brass ring. He stood up. He went to the iron door and put the key in the lock and turned it. And the door swung open on the dark of bin seven, onto the sound of breaking.

---

She would remember bin seven as a room full of broken glass.

Shelves, floor to ceiling, like the racks outside, but narrower, closer. On them, on little felt pads, cores. Cracked cores, each one with its bright hairline flaw across it like ice. And the near half of the room, the half nearest the door, nearest the bar, was glass. Splinters. Lilac dust. Shards on every shelf and drifted on the floor, glittering faintly in the lamplight from the door, still faintly warm, smelling of honey. Hundreds of them. No voices there. The voices there had stopped in the middle.

And the back half of the room, where the wave had not reached, was whole. Cracked cores on their felt pads, in their rows, by docket number. Saying half a name in the dark.

Orrin Hale stood in the doorway with the tin box in his arms and looked at it.

"Docket numbers," he said. "Each shelf. By number. Low at the door, high at the back." His voice did not change at all. "The low numbers were the early ones. Ebb Cross, '13. '14. '15." He looked at the glass. "Fen was F-four. Her card only ever said *F*, but the Office gave her the number. Tessa was F-two-hundred-and-eleven. Bridie was F-three-hundred-and-two." He looked at the back of the room. "I don't know where it stopped."

Behind them, up the cellar, the boots reached the foot of the stair.

"Then we take what's whole," said Seraphina. "All of it. Now."

---

They took what was whole.

The three troopers and Thirty-Three and the two who had carried the book. Six of the Watch in dead men's coats, in the doorway of bin seven, sweeping cracked cores off the shelves into their arms, into their coats, into the canvas sacks they had brought rolled under their belts. They worked through the weight of the Lock as fast as men could move through water. They were still talking. *Rootday. Hearthday.* Orrin was among them, steady, unhurried, reading the numbers on the shelves aloud as he swept them. Mags, on her feet again, with blood on her face and her one good arm, holding a sack open.

And Seraphina in the aisle outside the door, facing the stair, with the Blade.

The proctors came down the cellar between the racks. Twenty. Thirty. Grey oilcloth coats, silver mesh over the faces, felted rods in one hand. In the other, the hand-forks: brass, tuned to seven hundred and forty-one, long as a forearm. A proctor with a hand-fork could take a name in eight seconds of contact. She had seen what that looked like now, in the cellar, in the racks.

She did not draw the light. Not again. She could feel what it had cost her the first time, a heaviness in her arms that was not the Lock, a whiteness creeping in at the edges of her eyes. And she did not need it. They were coming down a narrow aisle between two racks, one or two at a time. She was a knight of the Choir-Church of the Pure Note, the best of her year, and she stood at the end of the aisle with the bone of the god in her hands.

The first proctor came at her with his rod up and his fork low, the way they were trained. She took his fork-hand off at the wrist. The fork rang on the stone. He screamed through his mesh, and she put him down with the pommel.

The second, she killed.

She did not mean to. He came in fast and low, and she was slow, the Lock in her shoulder, and the Blade went where she had not meant it to go. It went in under the mesh at his throat and out again. He fell against the rack beside him and slid down it, and the cores in their pigeonholes glowed lilac on either side of his face, murmuring their names. He lay at the foot of the rack with his blood running out across the stone floor of Sorting. It ran into the dust under the lowest pigeonholes, where somewhere, perhaps, a little core smaller than a thumbnail was lying in the dark, saying a woman's name.

The third stepped over him.

---

She did not count them. She was not Vane. She held the end of the aisle, and they came, and she put them down. The Blade was heavy in the Lock, and she was slow, and they had forks. She knew that if one fork touched her neck for eight seconds she would be a core on a felt pad in a rack, and someone would hold her to an ear and chalk her *C* or *F*. And she held the aisle.

Behind her, from the door of bin seven, the troopers talking. *Massday. Stasisday.* Sweeping glass. Orrin's voice reading numbers.

And then, behind her, one voice stopped.

---

She heard it the way the Watch would have heard it. She had been on the Watch only five days, and she heard it anyway. A voice that had been talking, steadily, about chickens, all the way from the Rift to the Organum, under everything. It stopped in the middle of a word.

"*Eighty-Eight!*"

That was Nineteen. The grey woman with the scar through her eyebrow. Not talking. *Shouting.* The way the Watch was sworn to shout. "*Eighty-Eight's gone quiet! Eighty-Eight's gone—*"

Seraphina turned her head.

The boy with the big nose and the enormous hands was standing at the end of the shelves in bin seven, by a gap in the wall. Seraphina had not seen it. A low arch at the back of the bin, into the dark beyond. A proctor had come through it. A thin man in grey oilcloth, who must have known the other way in. And he had come up behind Eighty-Eight in the dark at the back of the bin. Eighty-Eight was standing with his arms full of cracked cores and his mouth open. And the proctor was standing behind him with his hand-fork pressed to the back of the boy's neck, under the skull, where it went in.

Eight seconds.

She did not know how many had gone. She could not reach him. She was at the end of the aisle with a proctor in front of her and a fork coming at her face. And the boy was thirty feet away at the back of bin seven with the glass on the floor and the cores in his arms.

She watched it take him.

She watched his face. The big nose. The enormous hands, holding the cores against his chest. She watched his eyes go wide, and then go soft. She watched his mouth, which had been open in the middle of a word about chickens, close. Slowly. Peacefully. She watched him stop.

Nineteen was there before Seraphina could move. The grey woman went across bin seven through the glass and the Lock like a ship through a heavy sea. She came at the proctor from the side with her carbine reversed, and swung it, and the butt took him across the side of the head through the mesh. He went down among the glass with his fork still in his hand. On the end of the fork, in the little brass cup where the glass went, something small and lilac and new, faintly lit, saying a name.

Eighty-Eight stood where he had been standing.

He did not fall. He stood quite still at the back of bin seven with his arms full of cracked cores and his face smooth and his eyes on nothing. He was breathing. He was a big boy of nineteen with a big nose and enormous hands, standing in a room full of broken glass, and he was not anybody at all.

Nineteen stood in front of him with the carbine in her hands.

"*Eighty-Eight*," she said. Not shouting now. "*Eighty-Eight. Talk. Talk to me. Your mother's chickens. Tell me about the chickens. The brown one. The one that got in the house.*"

The boy looked at her. His eyes were quite empty.

He did not say anything.

---

Afterward, Seraphina would think that this was the moment Orrin Hale did the bravest thing she had ever seen anyone do. And he did it without any courage at all, because he had none.

He came across bin seven to the proctor lying among the glass. He knelt down. He took the hand-fork out of the man's limp fingers. Very carefully, the way you take an egg from under a hen, he took the little new lilac core out of the brass cup on the end of it. He held it up to the lamplight from the door.

"Eighty-Eight," said Orrin Hale. Quite pleasantly. Reading it. "No. Not that. That's his number." He listened to it, his head on one side, the way the sorters did. "*Davey Pruett.* Of Coldharrow, in the Peaks. He's saying it very clearly. Grade C." He looked at the boy standing in the glass with his arms full. "Hello, Davey."

Then he put the core in the inside of his own coat, against his chest. He took the boy by the arm, gently, and turned him toward the door.

"Come on, Davey," he said. "We're going home. Bring those. Don't drop them."

And the boy who had been Eighty-Eight, who had been Davey Pruett, who had stolen a horse and talked about chickens all the way from the edge of the world, walked out of bin seven beside him. Steadily. With his arms full of cracked cores. Saying nothing at all.

---

The goods-hoist was at the north end of Sorting, behind the last rack, where Orrin had said it would be. A square iron cage on a chain, for bringing crates down from Receiving. It was not keyed to a Signature, because it was a winch, and winches did not care who turned them. The Lock had not touched it.

They went up in it with everything they had. The six of the Watch, five now, and one who did not talk. Orrin. Mags, white-faced, with blood on her lip and her good arm shaking. Sable, on her feet, holding the Ledger, with her face smooth and her eyes on nothing in particular. The Concordance in its black coat. The sacks.

And Pellam Quist, with his hands tied behind him with his own white stock and his spectacles in Orrin's pocket. Because Orrin had looked down at him on the floor and said, "I think we'd better have him. I don't think we want to leave him here with the bar." And nobody had argued.

Seraphina went up last. She stood at the gate of the hoist with the Blade bare in her hand. As the chain took the cage up out of the cellar, she looked back once.

Down the length of Sorting. The racks, row on row, glowing lilac. The long scrubbed table with its crates, the sun-discs stencilled on the wood. The sorters on their stools, sitting quite still in the Lock with their gloved hands in their laps and their eyes closed. The brass mill at the end of the table. The young man beside it, who had stopped turning the crank. The murmur of the names, tens of thousands, filling the vaulted dark from end to end like the sea in a cave.

And at the foot of the nearest rack, in the dust under the lowest pigeonholes, in the dark, too small to see: a woman's name. Two words. Saying itself to nobody.

The cage rose, and the cellar went down out of sight beneath her feet.

---

She counted them on the *Equity*'s deck an hour later, because there was nobody else to do it. Vane was at the wheel with the city behind him and the Arbiterium's guns on the rooftops still firing. Sable was sitting against the deckhouse with her face smooth and the Ledger in her lap and did not seem to see anything.

They had come out of the hoist into the Receiving yard and across the end of Registry Square in the Lock, slowly. Mags had been in front, the only one of them walking at an ordinary pace, holding the others by their sleeves and pulling. The pipes were playing over their heads like the end of the world. Ambrose had been standing behind his copper urn under the faded awning. He had watched them go past, a party of Justiciary troopers and two Office officers and a man in a ruined clerk's coat, with sacks on their backs and the Registrar-General of the Office tied up among them. He had not said anything. Seraphina did not think he had noticed. He had been looking at his urn.

And the mooring-ring at the head of the Arbiterium's mast had been locked, as every ring in the city was locked, and the *Equity* chained to it. Vane had stood at the foot of the mast in the Lock and looked at the ring for a moment. Then he had climbed the sixty feet of iron rungs, slowly, through the weight of the city's chord, with his cracked ribs. He had not cut the ring. He had cut the cutter's own bow-hawser above the ring with the dead sergeant's axe. They had gone up off the roof of the Arbiterium with forty feet of hawser left on the ring and the guns on the rooftops opening up on them.

She counted.

Vane. Mags, with a cloth to her face and her good arm in her lap, shaking, and blood in her ear. She would never hear again on that side, Seraphina thought, if she had ever heard on either. Thirty-Three, Nineteen, Sixty, and the two who had carried the book. Orrin Hale. Sable. Herself.

Davey Pruett, sitting against the rail with his big hands in his lap and his eyes on nothing. Nineteen sat beside him, talking to him steadily about chickens.

Pellam Quist, tied to the foot of the mast with his own stock, without his spectacles. Blinking, very calm, as if he were waiting for a meeting to begin.

And the sacks.

Orrin had them open on the deck. He was going through them by the light of the binnacle-lamp, one core at a time. Holding each one up to his ear and listening to it say half its name, reading the number on its little brass tag, and finding the docket in the tin. He was moving them, card by card, from one side of the tin to the other, the way he had moved the dockets of the dead on the Shelf.

When he had finished, he sat back on his heels and looked at the two piles of cards.

"A hundred and thirty-one," said Orrin Hale.

Seraphina looked at him.

"A hundred and thirty-one of ours," said Orrin. "Whole. Cracked, but whole. Low numbers broke. High numbers didn't." He looked at the larger pile of cards, the ones he had not moved. "Two hundred and thirty-two, gone. Fen's gone. F-four. She was near the door." He put the tin down on the deck very carefully. "Tessa's here. F-two-hundred-and-eleven. Except Tessa's already got hers. So it's an empty space on the shelf, hers." He looked at the cards. "Bridie's here."

He sat there for a long time on the deck of the cutter, with the tin box and the cards and the sacks, in the binnacle-light. The Organum fell away behind them in the grey, and its pipes went on playing the Lock to nobody.

"I'll have to tell them," said Orrin Hale. "On the Shelf. Two hundred and thirty-two of them. Which ones." He looked at his hands. "I'll have to tell Fen."

And Seraphina Aubade, who had been taught at six years old that the soul has a sound, sat down on the deck of the *Equity* beside a man who had no fear. She found that she was weeping. She could not stop. She did not try.

Orrin Hale looked at her for a while.

"I'd like to do that," he said, after a time. "I can't. I know I ought to. I'd like to." He reached into his coat and took out the little core with Davey Pruett's name in it, and held it in his palm in the lamplight, and looked at it. "I'll tell Fen tomorrow. You'll have to do it for me today."

So she did.

## CHAPTER EIGHT
### Here

> *A Cantor's instrument is the breath. The voice is only the most common way of spending it.*
>
> — Cantor-Master Ilse Brennan, to her students at the Reedside Conservatory, Oakhaven, in a note she wrote on a slate in 408, after her own voice went

*The edge of the Spectral Woods. Nullday the twenty-fourth of Ashfall, to Stasisday the twenty-eighth.*

---

They came into the mist at the edge of the Woods on the evening of Massday, three days out from the Watch, and hid the *Kestrel* there to wait. And on that first night in the mist Lyra found out she could still whistle.

She had not tried. It had not occurred to her. For two weeks she had thought of herself as a woman with no instrument. She had written on a slate, and signed with her hands, and beaten an iron pot with a ladle at the edge of the world because it was the only sound she could make. She had not thought of her lips. She was sitting on the foredeck that night with her knees drawn up and the lilac mist all round the ship. The silver willows were ghostly in it, and the phosphorescent pools glowed under them. And she put her lips together without thinking and blew. The way you do when you are tired and alone and there is nobody to hear.

A note came out.

Thin. Breathy. Wavering. A hundred and seventy-four, the Root, the tending note, but whistled, not sung. It went out from the bow into the mist and hung there.

She stopped. She sat quite still.

Then she tried again, carefully, and it came again. And this time it hurt. Not in the lips. Lower. In the throat, where the breath went up past the torn place on its way out. A long hot raw pull, like breathing through a burn. She felt something warm at the back of her tongue, and swallowed, and tasted iron.

She did it anyway. Four notes and a falling fifth. The tending note of the Reach, whistled, into the mist at the edge of the Spectral Woods. Then she sat with her hand over her mouth and her eyes streaming and the taste of blood in her mouth, and could not have said whether she was weeping for the pain or for the other thing.

From the foot of the press, in the dark, Kaelen said, "*Lyra.*"

He had heard it. Of course he had heard it. Vesper had gone aft at the dead hour to walk the deck, as she did every night, and taken the quiet with her. For that hour he sat alone against the press with the old man at his back, and he had heard.

She looked at him. He was looking back at her through the mist, with the sack on his arm and his face grey and something in it she had not seen there in two weeks.

She took her hand away from her mouth. She did not have the slate. She made the sign instead, with two fingers, the *Kestrel*'s hand-speech.

*Here.*

---

The quiet had been Kaelen's idea. He had written it on a slate on the first night out from the Watch and handed it to Vesper without a word.

*AT THE EDGE HE COULDN'T HEAR. THE QUIET THERE IS THE SAME THING AS YOURS. ISN'T IT. A LITTLE OF THE TRENCH. SIT WITH ME. LET'S SEE.*

Vesper had read it twice, standing at the bow in the voiceless wind with her coat drawn round her and the thing in it straining east. She had looked at him for a long time with her violet eyes.

Then she had sat down beside him against the press.

Lyra had watched it from the hatch. She had watched the quiet settle round the two of them like a cloak. The edge came off the creak of the rigging. The hum of the tanks went far away. The forward third of the ship turned into the place where nothing could be heard from outside. She had watched Kaelen sit inside it with his eyes shut and his whole body listening. Not with his ears. With the back of his neck, where he said the old man stood.

After an hour he had opened his eyes and written on the slate.

*FAINTER. NOT GONE. LIKE A VOICE THROUGH A WALL. HE CAN HEAR SOMETHING. NOT WORDS.*

And under it, after a moment:

*HE KNOWS. I CAN FEEL HIM PRESSING. LIKE A MAN WITH HIS EAR TO A DOOR.*

So they had a wall now. Not a room. A wall, with a man on the other side of it with his ear pressed to the plaster. It was more than they had had. Kaelen had sat in Vesper's quiet for four days, every hour of them, sleeping there, eating there, his back against the press and Vesper's shoulder against his. And the thing in Vesper's coat had leaned, not east now, but toward him.

Lyra had seen it lean. She had seen Vesper see it too, and put her hand on it, and hold it.

---

The mist at the edge of the Woods was not like any mist Lyra had known. It did not lift with the sun. It did not thin at noon. It lay in the hollows between the silver willows at the height of a man's chest, lilac, faintly luminous, and the *Kestrel* lay in it with her keel a yard above the phosphorescent water of a pool. They had tied her to a willow with three lines, and her bag was black against the white sky and her deck was lost in the mist. If you stood on the deck you could see nothing past the rail but lilac and the grey shapes of trees.

And you could hear the Woods.

Not near. Far off. Miles. From somewhere deeper in, toward the heart of the Woods, where Whisper-Grove lay sunk in its marble plazas: voices. Very faint, at the edge of hearing, on the still air. Hundreds of them. Each one saying three or four words, over and over, at conversational volume, all at once, overlapping. The loops. Seventy-four thousand Hollows in the plazas of Whisper-Grove, repeating the last thing they had said before the Silencing. Lyra had heard them in Harvestmonth, walking through the Grove with Kaelen and Sable. She had seen a man set a table for four. She had heard them since the moment they came into the mist, at the edge of hearing, and she did not expect to stop.

Nobody on the *Kestrel* talked about it. Hetty Bram sang in the galley to cover it. Old songs, tavern songs from the Boar & Dulcimer, in a voice like a cart going over cobbles. Bettany sat in the hold with the Aegis and the crates and read aloud from the one book she had brought from Oakhaven, a herbal, the same pages over and over. Tobiah talked to the tanks. He had taken it up from Pennick without anybody noticing, and he did it now all the time, under his breath, in the cradle. *There's my girl. Steady now. Don't rush her.*

Solenne sat in the lee of the tank-cradle with her broken wings folded wrong round her, and the priest sat beside her. Sometimes she sang. Two voices, copper and void. The copper one was Hymn 9, the soprano's line. The void one was nothing; it was the sound of a door opening onto nothing, under the copper, and Lyra could not listen to it for long.

The priest did not sing. Ignatius Crane had not said a word, as far as Lyra knew, since Lumina. He sat beside Solenne with his white hand in his lap and his eyes open. When she sang the void, he flinched. When she stopped, he looked at her. She did not look back.

And the Aegis in the hold, with its thinned white back against the bulkhead and Bettany's herbal beside it, hummed the alto line of Hymn 9. Under everything. All day. All night.

---

On the morning after they moored, Stasisday, the Office came.

Lyra heard them before anyone. It was the only craft she had left, and it had never failed her. She was sitting at the bow in the grey before dawn, with the mist lilac and still round the ship and the loops at the edge of hearing. And she heard, under the loops, a new sound. Far off. Coming nearer. A low steady hum, very faint, like a bee in a jar. And under the hum, rhythmic, a soft heavy *whuff*, *whuff*, *whuff*.

Airscrews. A steam-cutter, running slow.

More than one.

She was on her feet before she knew it. She had no voice and no slate. She ran aft along the deck through the mist to the tank-cradle, where Tobiah was asleep sitting up with his hand on the Dawnday's valve. She shook him. He woke and saw her face and stopped breathing.

She pointed. South-east. Toward the sound. She held up three fingers.

He listened. She watched him hear it.

"*Oh, Light*," said Tobiah Marsh.

---

Three cutters. They came in over the willows out of the south-east in the grey before dawn, low, slow, in line abreast, a quarter of a mile apart. Office cutters, grey-hulled, under grey envelopes, with the key-and-quill of the Catalogue painted on their flanks inside a ring. Lyra saw them through the mist as grey shapes moving over the treetops. On the bow of each, under the envelope, a horn.

Listening-horns. Long brass trumpets, ten feet from mouth to bell, swivelling slowly on their mounts. On each horn a man sat with his ear to the narrow end and his eyes shut, turning it. Left. Right. Left. Sweeping the mist the way a lighthouse sweeps the sea, but backward. Not throwing light out. Drawing sound in.

They were listening for the ship.

Lyra had heard about the horns. Sable had told the story on the *Kestrel* in Harvestmonth, of the night after Whisper-Grove when an Office cutter had hunted them by sound. *They can hear a man breathing at a quarter of a mile*, Sable had said. *They can hear four hundred and thirty-two cores saying their names in a hold at two miles. They found us by the hold.*

The hold. Three hundred and ninety-six names, murmuring in the dark, under her feet.

And the arm.

She looked forward. At the press, and Kaelen against it in Vesper's quiet, and the faint cyan of the glass through the canvas. Ringing. It always rang. Five hundred and twenty-eight, high and faint and clear, and six hundred and thirty-nine under it. She had lived beside it for two months and stopped hearing it. A horn would not stop hearing it. A horn would hear it at two miles, if it were not inside the quiet.

It was inside the quiet. That was why he had been sitting there since the Watch.

But the hold was not.

---

They had perhaps five minutes. She knew it by the sound of the airscrews, getting louder. Everyone on the ship was awake now, on deck, in the mist. Tobiah at the valves. Hetty in the galley hatch with her strapped wrist and a cleaver in her good hand. Bettany at the forward hatch. Solenne on her feet, tall in the lilac, with her broken wings half-spread. The priest beside her. Vesper and Kaelen at the bow.

Nobody spoke. Lyra could not. But she could hear what they were all thinking, the way she could hear breath.

*The hold.* Three hundred and ninety-six people in crates, saying their names in the dark. You could not tell them to be quiet. Sable had tried, once, on the Rust Coast, and they had not understood. They were saying their names so as not to forget them. It was the only thing they had.

The horns would hear the hold.

She looked at Vesper.

Vesper was already looking at her. The violet eyes, shot through with very small cold points of light, steady in the mist. Lyra saw her understand. She saw Vesper look at Kaelen beside her against the press, inside the quiet. Then aft, along the deck. At the after hatch, and the after hold beneath it, where the crates were lashed.

The quiet covered the bow and the foredeck and the man against the press. The crates were lashed in the after hold, aft of the mast, forty feet beyond its reach. It could not cover both.

Vesper looked at Kaelen for one long moment. Then she stood up.

Lyra saw his face as the quiet went off him. As Vesper got up from beside him against the press and walked aft along the deck to the after hatch, and went down the ladder into the hold, and took the quiet with her. Lyra saw it go off Kaelen like a blanket pulled off a sleeper in a cold room. She saw his hand go to the back of his neck.

She saw the old man come back.

Kaelen sat against the press with the sack on his arm and his hand at his neck, out of the quiet, ringing. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Six hundred and thirty-nine. Clear and high and faint into the still lilac air, like a struck glass. He looked aft along the deck at Lyra. His face was quite calm.

The horn on the nearest cutter stopped swivelling.

It swung. Slowly. Toward the *Kestrel*. And stopped.

---

They came down out of the grey on the *Kestrel* like hawks on a hen-coop.

The near cutter dropped through the mist on her airscrews with her grapnels already swinging. Iron hooks on chains, thrown from her rail, coming down out of the lilac onto the *Kestrel*'s deck. One caught the rail by the tank-cradle. One caught the foremast shrouds. One went through the patched sailcloth of the bag over their heads with a sound like tearing silk. The chains came taut, and the cutter came down alongside, grey hull against brown, and the *Kestrel* heeled under the weight.

And they came over the rail.

Proctors. Lyra knew them from the plaza at Whisper-Grove. Grey oilcloth, silver mesh over the face, felted rods. Hand-forks, brass, long as a forearm, tuned to seven hundred and forty-one. And behind them men she did not know. Men in grey with the key-and-quill on their breasts and carbines in their hands. Office marines, who did not take names. They took everything else.

Ten. Fifteen. Coming over the rail in the mist.

And from the other two cutters, still circling overhead, more grapnels coming down.

---

The Aegis came up out of the hold.

Lyra heard it come. A great grinding of iron at the after hatch, and steam hissing out of the thinned plates of its back. The hatch-coaming splintered as nine feet of riveted iron came up through a hatch built for a man, and stood on the deck in the lilac mist with its helm turned toward the rail. Its chest was not chiming the alto line now. It was not chiming anything. It was silent. And across its chest, in old stencilled letters, half rubbed away: *DEFEND THE SINGERS.*

It went forward into the proctors like a wall falling down a hill.

Lyra had seen it fight once, in the Cloister of the Lost Choir, in Harvestmonth. When it stood up out of its alcove and broke Mags's collarbone. She had thought then that it was the most terrible thing she had ever seen. She had not seen it fight with somebody in it.

It fought like a child defending its mother. Not skilfully. Not well. With its whole iron body, without thought for itself, wildly, terribly. It took the first proctor by the mesh over his face and threw him over the rail into the mist. It took the second by the arm and the arm came off. It walked into a volley of carbine-fire from the marines and the balls rang off its chest like hail off a roof. It kept walking, with the steam screaming out of its back. It caught two marines in its great iron hands and brought them together, and Lyra looked away.

A proctor came in under its arm with a felted rod and struck it across the back. On the white, on the thinned plates that had held the Bleach off a Cantor for twenty minutes on the Pilgrim Chain.

The plate gave. Lyra heard it give. A sound like a cracked bell.

The Aegis turned round. Slowly. And through the gap in the white plate on its back, faint and rosy, like a hand held over a candle, Lyra saw the light of the core.

---

She did not see Kaelen get up. She saw him in the middle of the deck.

He had come off the press without the sack. The arm was bare, cyan and blazing, the green vein in it bright as a river under ice. A proctor came at him with a hand-fork, the way the proctors in the plaza had come. Not at his face. At his arm. The fork struck the glass at the elbow.

Seven hundred and forty-one. The Office's forks had locked it once before, in the plaza at Whisper-Grove, and they had learned. The arm went rigid. Lyra saw it. She saw it lock out straight from his shoulder, like a bar of glass bolted to his body. She saw his face go white with the pain of it, the joint where glass met flesh shrieking. She saw him go down on one knee on the deck with his arm stuck out stiff in front of him, ringing, flat, and useless.

The proctor lifted his fork again. Not at the arm. At the back of Kaelen's neck.

Lyra was twenty feet away. She had no voice. She had no weapon. She had her breath, and her lips, and the craft her father had taught her in the wind-reed orchards before the reeds died. And she had the one thing Sable had told her that she had never forgotten: *breaking a unison. A note a hair flat makes them beat.*

She put her lips together and whistled.

---

Seven hundred and forty-one. The fork's own note. But a hair flat.

It came out of her thin and high and wavering, and it tore her. She felt it tear. The breath went up past the raw place in her throat like a blade going up a sheath, and she tasted the blood come. She whistled anyway. She held it. Thin and high and a hair flat against the ringing of the fork in the glass of Kaelen's arm. And the two notes met in the air over the deck and began to *beat*.

She heard it. A slow throb in the ringing, like a pulse. *Wah. Wah. Wah.* The lock in the arm and the note from her lips, fighting each other, a hair apart. Neither one could hold the other.

The arm moved.

She saw it. The rigid glass at Kaelen's elbow shivered, and bent, a degree. Two degrees. The lock was breaking, the way a unison breaks when someone sings a hair flat underneath it. It was coming apart from the inside.

The proctor turned his head toward her.

She held the note. Blood was in her mouth now, and running over her lip, and she held it. She saw the proctor take a step toward her across the deck through the mist, with his fork. And she held it. She could not stop. If she stopped, the lock would close again. She stood on the deck of the *Kestrel* with blood running down her chin and whistled a hair flat at the man coming to take her name.

And Hetty Bram stepped between them.

---

Lyra saw her do it. She would see it every night for the rest of her life, the way Vane saw the hawser.

Hetty came out of the galley hatch with the cleaver in her good hand and her broken wrist strapped against her chest. She was sixty, and broad, and grey, and she had kept the Boar & Dulcimer in Oakhaven for thirty years. She had fed the whole of Reedside through the Greening and never once let the hearth go out. She came across the deck in the mist at a stumping, heavy run, the way a woman runs who has not run in twenty years. And she put herself between the proctor and Lyra and swung the cleaver.

It took him in the side of the neck under the mesh.

He went down. He went down onto the deck with his fork ringing on the planks. Hetty stood over him with the cleaver, breathing hard, and turned round and looked at Lyra. Her broad grey face was set like a woman carrying water up a hill.

"*Keep on, love*," said Hetty Bram. "*Keep on. Whatever that is. Keep on.*"

The marine behind her fired.

---

Lyra saw it hit her. In the back, low, through the apron. She saw Hetty's face change. Not pain. Surprise. As if somebody had tapped her on the shoulder in a crowded room.

Hetty took one more step toward her. Then her knees went.

Lyra did not stop whistling.

She would not forgive herself for that. She would understand it, afterward, and know that it was right, and she would never forgive herself. Hetty Bram went down on the deck at her feet in the lilac mist with a ball in her back. And Lyra stood over her and did not stop. She did not drop to her knees. She did not take Hetty's head in her lap. She held the note, a hair flat, with the blood running down her chin, because Kaelen's arm was still locked and the lock was still breaking and if she stopped it would close.

Hetty looked up at her from the deck. Her lips moved.

Lyra could not hear what she said. The note was in her ears and the blood was in her mouth. She saw the lips move, and she read them, the way Mags had taught her on the long nights in the hold.

*Good girl.*

And behind her, from the middle of the deck, Lyra heard the arm break free.

---

It was not a crack. It was a bell.

Six hundred and thirty-nine. Deep. The drowned tenor of Wendmere, rung from the inside. It came up out of the arm all at once, and the lock went to pieces in it like ice in a flood. Lyra heard the fork's note go out of the glass. She heard the beat stop. She stopped whistling, and fell to her knees on the deck beside Hetty Bram, with the blood pouring out of her mouth.

And Kaelen stood up.

She did not see what he did then. She was on her knees with her hands on Hetty's back, on the wound, pressing, the way she had pressed a hundred wounds in a hundred fever-rooms, and there was too much blood. She heard it. She would hear it afterward when she closed her eyes.

She heard the green.

There was no other word for it. A sound like the sea. Like the Drift, the endless Velocity wind of the Rust Coast that never stopped. Deep and green and moving, coming out of the glass of his arm. And under it, the other sound. The one she had heard once, in the Valley, from the hold. When he had caught a twelve-pound ball out of the air and the green had drunk its speed.

He was letting it out.

All of it. Everything the green had drunk. The speed of the ball in the Valley. Every small start of motion the *Equity* had made for an hour at the Watch, straining on the end of a hawser, drunk a jerk at a time. It had all gone into the glass and stayed there, held, stored, the way a bell holds a note after it is struck. And he was letting it out.

She heard the near cutter go.

---

She looked up. She could not help it.

He was standing at the rail where the cutter's grapnels came over. Bare-armed, blazing green, his feet apart on the deck, his face white. He had his glass hand flat against the grey hull of the cutter where it lay alongside, the way he had laid it flat on the pier under the thirtieth arch. And the cutter was moving.

Away from the *Kestrel*. Sideways. Off the rail, off the grapnels, which snapped one after another with a sound like gunshots, out into the mist. At the speed of a thrown stone. At the speed of a ball out of a gun. The grey hull went away from the *Kestrel* across the pool and the willows in the time it took Lyra to draw breath, with its crew on its deck and its envelope full. It went sideways through the lilac like a plate thrown across a room.

It hit the trees.

The silver willows at the edge of the pool, a hundred yards off. The cutter went into them broadside at the speed of a ball out of a gun, and Lyra heard it break. The hull. The envelope, tearing. The lift-cells going, one after another, with a sound like a man coughing. The men on the deck. And then a long slow grinding crash as forty tons of skyship came down through the willows into the phosphorescent water of the pool. It lay there on its side with its bag draped over the trees like a shroud, burning a little, in the mist.

Nobody came out of it.

Kaelen stood at the rail with his hand still flat on the air where the hull had been. The green was going out of his arm. Slowly. Back into the vein.

She saw the vein.

She saw it from where she knelt, twenty feet away, through the mist, and she would never forget it. The green thread came up out of his shoulder along the collarbone, under the edge of the glass, toward the hollow of his throat. And at the top, where the glass ended, where for two weeks the green had run right up to the edge as if it were looking for a way out—

It had found one.

There was green under the skin of his throat. A hair's breadth. Above the edge of the glass, in the living flesh, in the hollow under the jaw. A fine thread of sea-green, like a vein of copper in a rock, showing through the skin.

---

The other two cutters went away.

They had seen what happened to the first. They had been circling overhead with their grapnels ready. Lyra heard their airscrews change pitch and their horns stop swivelling, and they went up into the white sky and away to the south-east, fast. Back toward the Organum. To tell somebody.

The marines and proctors still on the *Kestrel*'s deck went over the rail after their cutter, or tried to. The Aegis caught two. Solenne caught one. Lyra saw her do it. The tall woman with the broken wings had stood in the lee of the tank-cradle through the whole fight with the priest behind her. She had not moved and she had not sung, and she stepped out now into the path of a marine running for the rail. She sang the void at him. Not the copper. Only the void. The dark hollow pitchless sound that was the far side of a door opening onto nothing.

He stopped.

Lyra saw his face. She had seen it before, on the Shelf, on a man at the far end of the ledge as the ground went quiet under him. Startled. Then not startled.

Solenne stopped singing. He stood there on the deck with his carbine in his hands and his face soft and his eyes on nothing.

"Sit down," said Solenne. Two voices. Copper and dark.

He sat down.

The priest stood behind her and looked at the marine sitting on the deck with his face soft, and then at Solenne. His face did something Lyra had not seen it do since Lumina.

Solenne did not look at him.

---

Hetty Bram died at the ninth hour of the morning, on the deck, with her head in Lyra's lap.

She did not say anything else. She had said *good girl*, and that was all. She lay on her side on the planks with Lyra's hands on her back and Bettany's hands on her front and the blood coming out between their fingers. She looked at the lilac mist going white with the morning, and breathed. And then she breathed more slowly. And Lyra knelt over her and could not say anything to her at all.

She wanted to sing. More than she had ever wanted anything. The Last Note. The family holds the dying person's pitch until the body lets it go, so the note is never alone in the air. Hetty's pitch was low and rough, a cart going over cobbles, and Lyra knew it. She had heard it singing tavern songs in the galley every night since the Watch to cover the loops. She could have held it. She had held a hundred.

She had no voice.

She put her lips together, bloody, and tried to whistle it.

It would not come. The breath went up past the torn place in her throat and nothing came out but air and blood. She had spent the whistle on the arm. She had spent it holding a note a hair flat while Hetty Bram took a ball for her. There was nothing left.

So she put her face down close to Hetty's ear and breathed. Just breathed. Slow and steady, in and out, the way you breathe beside a sleeping child so it knows you are there. She did not know if Hetty could hear it. She breathed anyway, until Hetty stopped.

Bettany sat back on her heels with her hands red to the wrist and looked at Hetty's face for a long time.

"Thirty years," said Bettany. "Thirty years she kept that fire in. At the Boar. Never once let it go out. Not in the Greening. Not when the hounds came up the Ladder." She wiped her face with the back of a bloody hand and left a smear. "Who's going to keep the fire in?"

---

The galley stove had gone out in the fight. Lyra did not notice until the noon, when she went below and found the galley cold and dark, with Hetty's pots on the hob and Hetty's apron on its hook.

And the priest on his knees in front of the stove.

Ignatius Crane knelt on the galley floor with his white hand and his good hand, and a flint and steel, and a handful of tow from Hetty's tinder-box. He was trying to light the stove. He had been trying for some time, Lyra thought. There were spent sparks all over the hearthstone and the tow was black at the edges and had not caught. His hands were shaking.

He had been the brightest flame in the Church. Lyra had seen his fire at Oakhaven, in the Root-vault, white-gold and roaring, the glass-fire of the Flambeaux that burned as long as faith did. It had gone out on Lumina when Solenne looked at him, and it had not come back. He had no fire. He was a man on his knees in a dead woman's galley, trying to light a stove with a flint and steel like anybody else, and he could not get the tow to catch.

He struck again. A spark. It fell on the tow and died.

He struck again.

Lyra stood in the galley door and watched him. He did not know she was there. She did not move.

On the ninth strike, the tow caught. A small flame, yellow, ordinary, the colour of any fire in any kitchen in the world. He bent over it with his face close and blew on it, very gently, the way you blow on a fire you are afraid of losing. And it took. It went up into the kindling in the firebox and crackled, and the galley filled with the smell of woodsmoke.

Ignatius Crane knelt in front of the stove with his hands in his lap and looked at the fire. It was not white-gold. It was not holy. It would burn whether anybody believed in it or not.

He did not see Lyra. He did not see anything but the fire. After a while he got up off his knees. He took Hetty's apron down from its hook and stood holding it, looking at it. Then he put it on. He tied it at the back, clumsily, the way a man ties an apron who has never worn one. And he took Hetty's big iron pot off the hob and went to the water-butt to fill it.

Lyra went away and left him there.

---

Kaelen was on the foredeck, against the press, where he always was. Vesper had come up out of the hold and was sitting beside him again with the quiet round them both. Lyra went forward and sat down on his other side, inside it, where the old man could only press his ear to the wall.

He did not look at her. He was looking at his own hand.

His right hand. The flesh one. It was lying in his lap, and he was looking at it as if he had never seen it before. And Lyra saw, with a cold slow sinking in her stomach, that the thing in Vesper's coat was not in Vesper's coat.

It was in his lap. Curled against his belly under his right hand. Small and pale and soft, the size of a cat, lit from inside with a night sky. Warm as a bath. Purring.

She looked at Vesper.

Vesper's face was white. Whiter than the white side of it. She was looking at Mote in Kaelen's lap with her violet eyes very wide.

"I was in the hold," said Vesper. Very low. "With the quiet. With the cores. For the horns. I took it down with me. In the coat. And when the fighting started I put the coat down to—" She stopped. "It got out. While I was in the hold. It came up the ladder. It came here." She looked at Kaelen. "I didn't see. Nobody saw. Everybody was looking at the cutter. And at Hetty." Her hands were clenched in her lap. "When I came up it was already on him."

Lyra looked at Kaelen.

He looked back at her. His face was quite calm. Calmer than she had seen it in seven years, since the morning of the Foundry, since before. The grey was gone out of it. The set was gone out of the jaw. He looked, she thought, like a man who has just woken up from a long sleep in a warm room and does not yet remember where he is.

"Lyra," he said. His voice came out ringing, double, the Light and the bell. And under them now, so faint she was not sure she heard it, a third thing. A whisper of green. "I'm all right."

She did not have the slate. She made the sign with shaking fingers.

*What did it take?*

He looked at the sign. He thought about it. She watched him think about it, slowly, carefully, the way an engineer thinks about a reading he does not understand.

"I don't know," he said. "Nothing. I don't feel like anything's gone." He looked down at Mote in his lap, purring under his hand. "It just doesn't hurt."

Lyra went cold.

"The throat," said Kaelen. Quite calmly. He lifted his right hand off the purring thing in his lap and touched the side of his neck. He touched the hollow under the jaw, where the fine thread of sea-green showed through the skin above the edge of the glass. He touched it the way you touch a bruise to see if it is still sore. "It's through. I can feel it. Under the skin. It's in my throat now." He took his hand away and looked at his fingers. "It doesn't frighten me."

He said it with mild surprise, as if he had found a coin in his pocket.

"It should," said Kaelen. "I know it should. I've been afraid of this for seven years. Every morning I measured it. Every morning. A finger. Half a finger. I used to wake up in the night and feel for it." He looked at her with his calm clear eyes. "And now it's through, and it's in my throat, and one day, not far off, it'll have my voice. And whatever's in the glass will start to speak through me. And I'm—" He stopped. He seemed to search for the word. "I'm not afraid. I'm not anything. I know I ought to be. I'd like to be. For you." He looked at the purring thing in his lap. "I can't find it."

Vesper reached across and took Mote out of his lap.

She did it very gently, the way you take a sleeping child out of somebody's arms. Mote did not resist. It curled against her and purred, and she put it inside her coat and held it there with both hands. Lyra saw her face. It was the face of a woman holding something she loves, that has just done a thing she cannot forgive.

Kaelen watched it go. Lyra saw nothing in his face at all. No regret. No relief. Only the mild calm interest of a man watching weather.

---

The *Equity* came in out of the east at dusk, shot to pieces, with her bow-hawser trailing and her bag holed in nine places and her deck full of people who did not speak.

Lyra saw her come. She was at the bow with Kaelen and Vesper. She heard the airscrews first, and then saw the black envelope with the silver scale come over the willows in the last of the light, low and lurching, losing lift. And she knew the man at the wheel by the way he held her.

Vane brought the cutter down alongside the *Kestrel* in the lilac mist. He came across the gap on a line, one-handed, with his ribs strapped. He stood on the *Kestrel*'s deck and looked at it. At the splintered hatch where the Aegis had come up, and the white plate gone on its back. At the burned wreck in the willows across the pool. At Ignatius Crane in Hetty's apron at the galley hatch. At the long shape on the foredeck under a black blanket.

He looked at the blanket for a long time.

Then he looked at Lyra. At the blood dried on her chin and down the front of her shift. At her hands.

"Who?" he said.

She could not tell him. She had no voice, no whistle, no slate. She stood on the deck in front of him and did the only thing she could. She went to the blanket and knelt and lifted the edge of it, so that he could see Hetty Bram's broad grey face, calm, with her eyes closed.

Vane looked at her.

He did not say anything. He took his cap off. Lyra had never seen him wear a cap, and did not know where it had come from. Some dead Justiciar's, on the cutter. He took it off and stood holding it in his good hand, in the mist. Behind him, across the gap, the people on the *Equity*'s deck came over one by one.

Mags, with dried blood on her lip and in her ear, and her good arm shaking so badly she could not hold the line.

Orrin Hale, with a tin box under his arm, and beside him a big boy with a big nose and enormous hands. His face was smooth and empty and he did not look at anything. A grey woman with a scar through her eyebrow had him by the elbow and was talking to him, steadily, about chickens.

Seraphina, with the Sun-Blade bare on her back and her face swollen with weeping.

A small neat man of sixty with his hands tied behind him with his own white stock, and no spectacles, blinking in the lilac mist. He looked at the *Kestrel*'s deck with mild interest, as if it were a building he had been sent to inspect.

And Sable.

She came over last, with the Ledger under her arm. She stood on the deck in her grey Office coat and looked at Lyra. Lyra saw her face, and something in her went cold and still. Sable's face was smooth. Not calm. Not set. *Smooth.* The way Kaelen's was smooth. The way the face of a Hollow is smooth, in the plazas of Whisper-Grove, setting a table for four.

Not quite. Not yet. There was something still behind it. But less than there had been.

Sable looked at the blanket, and at Hetty's face, and at Lyra kneeling beside her. She did not say anything for a long time.

Then she came and knelt on Hetty's other side. She opened the Ledger on her knees, and took out her pen, and wrote, in her small clear hand, by the light of the binnacle-lamp. Lyra watched her write it.

*Henrietta Bram. Of the Boar & Dulcimer, Reedside, Oakhaven. Keeper of the hearth. Killed on the deck of the* Drift-Kestrel *at the edge of the Woods, the twenty-eighth of Ashfall, standing between the Cantor and a proctor.*

She stopped. She looked at it.

Then, under it, in the same small clear hand, she wrote one more line. And Lyra, who read everything upside-down, read it.

*She kept the fire in.*

Sable closed the Ledger. She looked across Hetty Bram at Lyra. And she lifted two ink-stained fingers, slowly, badly, the way she had learned it by writing it down.

*Here.*

Lyra made it back.

---

Down in the galley, the stove was lit. The smell of woodsmoke came up through the hatch into the mist. A man in a dead woman's apron was stirring a pot. He did not know what he was making. He was making it anyway.

Kaelen sat against the press with his hand at his throat, on the green, quite calm. He was looking at the people coming over the rail from the cutter: the boy who did not speak, the man with no fear, the scribe with a smooth face, the Registrar-General with his hands tied. And Lyra, kneeling by Hetty Bram with blood on her chin, watched him look. She saw that he was counting them, the way Vane counted. And she saw that it did not hurt him to count them. Not any of them. Not even her.

She looked away into the mist.

Somewhere far off, at the edge of hearing, the loops of Whisper-Grove went on. Seventy-four thousand people saying the last thing they had said, over and over, so as not to forget it. And on the *Kestrel*'s deck, in the lilac dark, Nineteen sat beside the boy who had been Davey Pruett. She went on talking to him about chickens, steadily, the way the Watch talked. As if he could still answer.

As if, if she kept talking long enough, he would.

*End of Part Two.*
