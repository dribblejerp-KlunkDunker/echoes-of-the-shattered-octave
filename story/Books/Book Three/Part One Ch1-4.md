# Echoes of the Shattered Octave — Book Three: The Hall of Carriage
## Part One: The Shelf (Chapters 1–4)

## PART ONE: THE SHELF

## CHAPTER ONE
### The Tramway

> *VELOCITY CORDON. In the Valley, what is still stays still, and what is moving goes on getting faster. Nobody can tell which a thing is until it is one or the other. Do not throw anything. Do not stand in front of anything you have thrown. Do not fire.*
>
> — Notice on the old Tramway gate at Hob's Cutting, painted over three times and still legible

*The Valley of Velocity. Dawnday the thirteenth of Ashfall, to Memoryday the fifteenth.*

---

He would remember the sentence for the rest of his life. He could have told you which word he had leaned on.

He had said it at the wheel on the night of Dawnday, at the end of the second watch, with the stars out over the foothills and the Heights gone down into the dark behind them. He was tired past the point where tiredness is a feeling, and his face throbbed under the stitches. Pennick had come aft from the tank-cradle with his chart and his lamp and asked where they were crossing the Tramway, because he had to know the height and the hour to work out the lift.

And Vane had told him.

"The Hanging Water. Where the line goes over the Valley on the long viaduct, the forty arches. There's a stretch at the east end where the rain's been standing since the year the Valley broke, a mile of it, like a curtain. Nobody watches it. Nobody can get a gun in there without killing himself, and nobody flies through it but a fool. We go over it tomorrow at the dead hour, five hundred feet, slow, with the wind dead behind." He remembered the short laugh. "It'll be wet."

Pennick wrote it on the chart. *Hanging Water. Dead hour. 500.*

Thirty feet forward of the wheel, on the foredeck, with his back against the press under its tarpaulin, the engineer was asleep. He had been asleep for the first time in three days, and nobody had wanted to wake him. His left arm lay bare across his chest. The black canvas sack was still in the hold, folded over the Cantor where the Aegis had laid it that morning, and the glass burned a soft steady cyan in the dark, rising and falling with his breath.

He was asleep. That was the thing Vane would turn over afterward, again and again, the way you turn a stone you have pulled out of a wound. *He was asleep.* As if that made any difference to the glass. As if the old man in his white room at the Organum needed the engineer to be awake to hear.

Thirty feet. Within thirty feet of any Dawning glass on the continent, anything said, for seven years.

He had known that for two months. He had sent the engineer out of a hundred rooms. He had planned in grease-pencil on the walls of the hold and on Pennick's charts and on the Cantor's slate, and watched Kaelen get up and go and see about the bilge pump every time he walked into a room where people were not talking. He had watched what that did to him.

And on the first night out from Sol-Luminary, at the end of the second watch, tired, with the engineer asleep, he had forgotten.

---

The Valley of Velocity is not wide. That was the first thing anybody noticed about it who had only ever heard it talked about. You came down off the foothills of the Heights onto the long ridge called the Combe, and there it was below you, a cleft in the country eleven miles long, two across at its widest and less than one at its eastern end, running east and west. Its floor was green and pleasant-looking at a distance, and across its eastern end, from wall to wall on forty arches of grey stone, ran the Iron Tramway.

The rails were still there. Nothing had run on them since 398, twenty-one years ago, when the Synod drilled the first spire-root at the Valley's western head and the law of motion came loose in it like a tooth. The stories said there was still a tram on the line, a single car of the Inland Express with its passengers, standing on the viaduct where it had stopped dead on the morning the law went, and that it was going to start again some day, without warning, and not stop. The Tramway Company had put up the notice at Hob's Cutting a week later. Nobody had taken it down.

Vane brought the *Kestrel* down off the Combe at the eleventh hour of Driftday night, in the dark, with no lamps lit. The Valley opened under her.

He had been through it once before, five years ago, in the spring of '14, in daylight, on foot along the old trackbed, on a dare, with Vesper and four of the *Wave-Cutter*'s hands. It had taken them five hours to walk a mile, because nobody hurried in the Valley, and a man called Doyle had kicked a stone off the embankment as they went in. It had gone down the slope and out across the Valley floor, rolling, getting faster. They had watched the line of dust it drew all the way to the far wall, and then a hole had appeared in the far wall, and a minute later they had heard it. Nobody had kicked anything after that.

Tonight the Valley was full of rain.

He could see it as they came over the lip. Not falling. Standing. A wall of it across the Valley's eastern end, silver-grey under the stars, from the floor to perhaps a thousand feet. Every drop of a squall that had come up the Valley on the morning the law went, twenty-one years ago, and stopped. It hung there. In the starlight it looked like a curtain of glass beads that somebody had forgotten to hang a room behind.

Through the curtain, black on the grey, the viaduct ran from wall to wall on its forty arches.

"There's a train on it," said Mags.

She said it aloud, in the flat over-careful voice of the deaf, and pointed. Vane put the glass up. Half-way across the viaduct, in the curtain, there was a long dark shape on the rails. A single car. It had lamps along its side, and they were lit. Or they were not lit; they were still lit. Twenty-one years on the same morning, with the oil unburned, because the flame had stood still.

"That's the Express," he said, so that she could read it. "It doesn't go anywhere."

She looked at him for a moment and then looked back at it, and he saw her decide she did not like it.

He did not like it either. He did not like any of it. He had not liked it when he planned it. But the Arbiterium's column was on the Tramway road, coming down out of the Reach toward the Heights with a writ for the engineer and everybody who had sailed with him. Every other crossing between here and the coast had a Justiciar's picket on it by now, or soon would have. This was the one place on the line where no column would set a picket, because no sane man would fire a gun here. The ball would never stop. It would go through what you aimed at and on through whatever was behind it, getting faster, through the far wall of the Valley and the hill behind that, for ever. A gunner who fired in the Valley could not know what he would kill a mile away, or next week.

The Arbiterium was law. Law did not do things it could not account for.

That was what he had told himself.

---

He took her down to five hundred feet at the east end of the Valley and let the wind have her.

There was always a little wind on the Combe. It came over the ridge and down into the Valley and died there, and what was left of it carried them, very slowly, toward the curtain. Pennick had trimmed the tanks to a hair. The *Kestrel* went into the Valley at the speed of a man walking, with her envelope black against the stars and her deck dark, and nobody spoke.

He had them where he wanted them. He always did, before; it was afterward that the arrangement came apart.

Vesper at the bow, on the anchor-bitts, with her quiet round her. It took the edge off the creak of the rigging and the hum of the tanks, and turned the whole forward third of the ship into a place where nothing could be heard from outside. Mote was in her coat. It had been leaning east since the Heights, the way it had leaned south-east for a month, and it was leaning east now, at the far end of the Valley, and Vesper had her hand on it.

Mags at the windlass with a line across her knees, watching. Mags could not hear the Valley. She would see it.

Pennick in the tank-cradle amidships, among his four great copper tanks in their iron frame: the new one from the *Gale*, which he called the Dawnday tank and spoke to by name, and the three old ones, which he spoke to the way you speak to old horses. He had his hand on the master-valve and his lamp hooded at his knee.

Tobiah Marsh in the hatchway, propped on his elbows, with his side bound. He was not supposed to be up. He had heard where they were going and asked to see the Express, and Bettany had said no and Tobiah had said please, and here he was, white-faced in the hatch, staring at the lit car on the viaduct with the eyes of a boy who had learned the Vance Sequence at fourteen.

Sable in the hold, with the Ledger, among the crates. Lyra on her cot, below. Bettany with her. Hetty Bram in the galley with the stove out. The knight at the stern rail, in the dark, with the bare Blade across her knees. It was useless at night: there was no light for it to draw. The woman with the wings in the lee of the tank-cradle with her priest.

The Aegis in the hold, folded down against the bulkhead beside Sable, humming the alto line so softly you felt it in the deck rather than heard it.

And the engineer on the foredeck, awake now, sitting against the press with the arm in its sack across his knees. He had gone down into the hold that morning and taken the sack back off the Cantor's cot without a word, and put it on, and tied it off at the neck. He was looking at the curtain of standing rain.

Vane brought her in.

---

The rain was cold. That was the first thing.

They went into the curtain at the speed of a walking man and the rain came onto the deck: not falling, but *met*. The ship moved into the standing drops and they burst on her like walking into a fine wet net hung across a lane. It came onto his face and the stitches stung. It came through his coat. It beaded on the envelope and the rigging and ran, and where it ran it moved, and where it moved it began, very slightly, to go faster. He saw it on the rail in front of him: a drop that had burst on the brass and run down it, running faster than water should, faster still. It reached the bottom of the rail and went off the edge of the deck into the dark like a struck spark, with a tiny thin whine, and was gone. He did not hear it hit anything.

All over the ship, the rain was doing that. Running off the rigging and the envelope and the rail in little streaks and whining away into the Valley in every direction, faster and faster, gone. It sounded like a thousand mosquitoes. It sounded like a ship full of wasps.

"Keep her slow," he said to Pennick, very quietly.

"She's not listening, Captain," said Pennick. "She's creeping. A hair faster every minute. It's the Valley. She'll be at a trot by the far side."

Vane had known that. It was in the arithmetic. Eleven minutes across the curtain, and a trot at the end of it, and then out over the north wall into ordinary air, where she would slow as things should. He had accepted it. He had accepted a great many things.

They went on into the curtain.

He could see the viaduct now, close, black and huge: forty grey arches marching across the Valley floor three hundred feet below, wet stone shining under the stars, and on top of it the rails, and the Express. It was a long dark car of green-lacquered wood and brass with the Tramway Company's crest on its flank, and it was lit all down its side. Through the windows, as they came abreast of it, he saw the passengers.

They were sitting in their seats. They had their hats on. A woman had a child on her knee. A man was half-way out of his seat with his hand up to the luggage rack. Nobody moved, because nothing in them moved. The light in the lamps had stood still, and the air in the car had stood still, and the passengers in their seats had stood still in the middle of the morning, and they were all looking out of the windows at the rain.

He felt Tobiah, in the hatch behind him, stop breathing.

"Don't," said Vane. "Don't look at them. It's the Valley. They're not looking at you."

He did not know if that was true.

---

It was Mags who saw it.

He would think afterward that that was right; that it could only have been Mags, who could not hear the wasp-whine of the running rain or the hum of the tanks or her own heart, and who therefore watched the dark the way nobody else on the ship could. She had been watching the Valley floor. She came up off the windlass and her good hand slapped the rail twice, hard, and he turned his head and saw her face, and then he saw where she was looking.

On the embankment at the north end of the viaduct, where the Tramway came off the last arch onto the solid ground of the Valley's wall, a light.

Not a lamp. A slow match. A red point the size of a coal. And beside it another. And another. Eleven of them, in a line along the top of the embankment, glowing and going dim and glowing as men blew on them. And behind them, very faintly, by their own red light, the shapes of guns.

Eleven guns on the embankment, in the dark, pointing up.

Not at the viaduct. Not at the Valley. *Up*. Straight up into the curtain, into the sky over the east end of the Hanging Water, at a single place in the air five hundred feet over the Valley floor. Laid there, and waiting. Laid hours ago, by men with chains and quadrants and tables, working out where a skyship coming down off the Combe on the dying wind would be at the dead hour, at five hundred feet, slow.

He understood it all at once, the way you understand a fall when you are half-way down.

*They knew.* Not *they guessed*, not *they picketed every crossing*. They knew the place and the height and the hour. They knew because he had said it. At the wheel, on Dawnday night, thirty feet from the press.

He turned his head and looked at the engineer.

Kaelen was looking at the guns. And then, slowly, Kaelen turned his head and looked back at Vane, down the length of the dark deck, and Vane watched him understand it too.

"*Pennick*," said Vane. "Dump—"

The first gun fired.

---

He saw the flash. He did not hear the gun, not then. In the Valley the sound came after, a long way after, as if it had stood still for a moment before it remembered to travel. What he saw was the red flower of the flash on the embankment, and then, coming up out of it into the rain, a line.

The ball was too fast to see, but the rain showed where it went. It went up through the standing drops, and every drop it touched burst and ran and went, so that the ball drew a line of white straight up the curtain like a needle drawn through wet silk. It came up the line toward them. It came *faster*. He saw it happen. The line went up from the embankment, quick and then quicker and then so quick that the top of it was there before he had seen the middle—

It went through the envelope.

Not through the ship. Through the great patched bag of the *Kestrel*'s envelope, twenty feet over his head. He heard that: a sound like a sail being torn by hand, very short, and then a long high scream going up into the dark above them, fading, and still fading, and never, as far as he could tell, stopping. Somewhere above the world tonight there was a twelve-pound ball still going up.

Wind came down out of the envelope onto the deck where the lift-cell had opened. A cold wind, smelling of the cell-gas, like wet iron.

"*Cell four's holed*," said Pennick in a high flat voice. "Four's holed, she's going, I've got her on the—"

The other ten fired together.

---

He did not remember the next part as a sequence. He remembered it as a number of things that were all true at once.

Ten white lines coming up the curtain from the embankment like the strings of a harp being drawn.

The deck under his feet jumping, all of it at once, as if the ship had been kicked from below.

The noise: not of the guns, which had not come yet, but of the ship being hit. A crack and a wrench and a scream of metal in the tank-cradle amidships. A sound in the hold like a box of crockery thrown down a stair.

Something going past his face so close and so fast that it took the rain off his cheek in a line and left the skin stinging, and going on, off the stern, into the dark, screaming, and the scream going up and up and away.

The knight at the stern rail, on her feet. The Blade dark in her hands. Nothing for it to draw.

And Pennick's voice from the tank-cradle, not high now, not flat, but very calm, the way Pennick only ever was when something was too bad to be frightened of:

"Captain. Number two's hit. She's venting."

---

He looked.

The tank-cradle was an iron frame amidships the size of a cart, bolted to the deck through the keelson, with the four copper tanks slung in it on straps and pipes. The Dawnday tank, the new one, was forward. Number two was aft of it on the starboard side, the oldest, the one Pennick called the Grandmother. There was a hole in the top of it the size of a fist, the copper petalled outward, and out of the hole, with a sound like a kettle the size of a house, the lift-gas was coming.

He could see it, faintly, by the starlight: a pale shimmer in the air over the tank. Going up. And he watched it begin, as it went, to go *faster*.

It was the Valley. Gas coming out of a hole was gas that was moving, and in the Valley what was moving went on getting faster. The jet came out of the Grandmother at the speed of a kettle's steam and a foot above the hole it was going faster than that, and two feet above it faster again. Within a second it was a column of shrieking air going straight up out of the tank-cradle into the envelope, into the hole the first ball had made, out of it, up into the night, and the noise of it climbed and climbed—

And the ship began to turn.

The jet was not quite straight. The hole was on the side of the tank's crown, not the top. It pushed. Gently at first, the way a man leans on a door. And the push was moving, and in the Valley what was moving went on getting faster, and the *Kestrel* began to swing round on her own centre, slowly, to starboard, the stars wheeling over the envelope, the viaduct sliding across the bow, the lit car of the Express going past like a shop window in a street seen from a carriage.

And going down. The Grandmother was the oldest tank and it was emptying into the sky.

"*Shut it off*," said Vane.

"I can't shut it off. The valve's on the crown, the ball's taken the crown." Pennick was out of the cradle's well and up on the frame already, on top of the tanks, in the scream of the jet, holding on to the straps. "I can cut her loose. If I cut her loose she goes. She takes the push with her."

"Then cut her loose."

"I'm cutting her loose." Pennick was hanging off the frame with one hand and working at something with the other: the big iron release-pin, the slip-hook that held the Grandmother's forward strap to the frame, that you knocked out with a maul to drop a tank in an emergency. He had the maul in his hand. He swung. Vane heard the iron ring. "She's — the ball's bent the frame, the pin won't — she won't—"

The ship came round. The guns on the embankment came across the bow, a line of red slow-matches in the dark, and he saw men moving at them. Swabbing. Loading. *A minute to reload a twelve-pounder for a good crew*, said the clerk in the back of his head, who never stopped. *They are a very good crew.*

"Captain." Sable, at the hatch. Her voice was quite steady. Her hands were not. "One of them came through the hold."

He looked at her.

"The aft crate on the starboard side. WG-forty thousand eight hundred and eighty to forty thousand nine hundred and fifteen." She said it the way she always said entries. "Thirty-six. It went through the crate. They were saying their names. They stopped."

He did not say anything.

"All at once," said Sable. "In the middle."

---

"*Down*," said Kaelen.

Vane heard it over everything. The engineer was not at the press any more. He was in the middle of the foredeck, on his feet, and he had pulled the black canvas sack off his left arm and thrown it down, and the arm was bare: nine inches too long, cyan, lit, and through the cyan, like a river under ice, the vein of sea-green that had come into it out of the drowned bell of Wendmere. The green vein was moving. Vane could see it from the wheel.

And Kaelen was looking at the embankment.

Vane looked too. And saw one of the eleven red points go bright, as a man put a match to the touch-hole.

They had not waited for the ship to come round. One crew had been faster than the rest. One gun, reloaded, re-laid, quick, and the ship was coming round slowly in front of it like a duck on a pond, beam-on.

The flash. The line coming up the curtain. Coming straight at the waist of the ship, at the tank-cradle, at Pennick on the frame in the scream of the jet, getting faster.

And Kaelen stepped into it.

He did not throw himself. Vane would remember that. He did not leap or shout. He took two steps to the starboard rail by the cradle the way a man steps to a door he has heard knock. He put his left arm out over the rail into the dark, open-handed, palm out. The line came up the curtain at him faster than anything Vane had ever seen move—

And the glass took it.

There was no noise at first. There was a light. The whole arm went green. Not cyan: green, the deep cold green of the sea at forty feet, from the palm to the shoulder, all at once, as if the vein had flooded. And there was a jolt that Vane felt through the deck and through the wheel and up his arms. Kaelen's whole body went back a foot along the rail, boots sliding on the wet deck, and stopped.

He had it in his hand.

A twelve-pound iron ball, black, smoking, in the palm of the glass hand. Not moving. *Stopped*. The arm round it blazing green, and the green running up from the palm, up the forearm, up past the elbow, into the shoulder, toward the collar of his shirt, where the edge of the glass came up along the bone—

Then the noise came. Not the gun. The *arm*. A note, very deep, deeper than any note Vane had heard come out of it: the note of a tenor bell forty feet under the sea, rung once, from the inside. Six hundred and thirty-nine. It went out through the ship and through Vane's teeth and out across the Valley, and the standing rain round the *Kestrel* shivered, all of it at once, like a curtain in a draught.

Kaelen opened his hand. The ball dropped onto the deck with a thud and lay there, steaming in the rain, quite still.

He turned round. His face was the colour of paper. He looked at Vane down the length of the deck, and his mouth moved, and nothing came out. He tried again.

"It drank it," he said. His voice came out ringing, two notes now, the Light and the drowned bell under it. "The speed. The green took it. Velocity glass. It's a — it's a Velocity bell, it *drank* it—" He put his right hand up to his throat.

Then his knees went, and he sat down on the wet deck against the rail with the green fading back out of his arm into the vein, slowly, and Vane saw the vein had moved. He could see it from the wheel. It had moved a long way.

---

There was no time to think about that. There was never time. That was the thing about action, the thing he had tried to tell Tobiah once and failed: it does not leave room for you to feel anything until afterward, and then it leaves nothing else.

The ship was still turning. The Grandmother was still screaming. The guns on the embankment were coming round across the stern as the *Kestrel* swung, and in a minute — less — ten fresh crews would put matches to ten touch-holes, and there was no engineer in the world who could catch ten.

And they were going down. He could feel it in his feet and see it on the viaduct: the rails coming up, the arches coming up. Three hundred feet. Two-eighty.

"*Pennick.*"

"I've got her." Pennick's voice from the top of the cradle, in the jet. "I've got her, Captain. I've got the pin. It's the strap, the after strap, the frame's bent on to it, the pin's out but the strap's — she's held by the strap—"

"Cut the strap."

"I'm cutting the strap." Vane saw him. Pennick was lying along the top of the Grandmother on his belly in the scream of the gas, with one arm hooked through the forward strap and his knife in the other, sawing at the after strap where it ran into the bent frame. A broad band of tarred hemp as thick as a man's wrist. It had held the Grandmother for eleven years. The knife was not going through it quickly.

Two-forty.

"Pennick," said Vane. "Come off it. I'll take her down. I'll put her on the viaduct, we'll—"

"You'll put her on the viaduct in front of eleven guns and a column of the Arbiterium," said Pennick, sawing. "With an engineer they want and a hold full of cores they want, and the Cantor. No, Captain. I've got her." The knife went in and out. "Nearly. She's nearly."

On the embankment, a slow-match went bright.

"Nearly," said Pennick.

---

He would not be able to say afterward what he saw clearly and what he made up later to fill the gap. He would know that the strap parted, because he heard it: a deep twanging crack, like a bowstring the size of a mast. He would know the Grandmother came free. He would know what the Valley did then, because he knew the Valley.

The Grandmother was a copper tank the size of a cow with a hole in its crown and a jet of shrieking gas coming out of it that had been getting faster for a minute and a half. Held in the cradle, the jet had turned the ship. Free, it took the tank.

It went up out of the cradle the way a cork goes out of a bottle, and on. Slowly for the first foot. Then quicker. Then up past the envelope and through the shredded rent of cell four as if the rent had been cut for it, up into the curtain, drawing a line of white through the standing rain, going faster, the scream of the jet rising and rising—

And Pennick went with it.

His arm was through the forward strap. He had hooked it there to hold on while he sawed. When the after strap parted, the Grandmother went up out of the cradle and the forward strap went with it and his arm was through the forward strap.

Vane saw that. He would always see it. He saw Pennick come off the top of the cradle in one movement, as if he had been called, his boots going up past the rail, his lamp falling away from him, his face turned down toward the deck for one instant. Vane would swear for the rest of his life that it was not frightened. He would swear that Pennick was saying something. He would never know what. And then he was going up with the tank, hanging off it by his arm through the strap, past the envelope, into the white line it drew through the rain. Getting faster.

"*Pennick!*"

That was Tobiah. In the hatch. With his side bound.

The tank went up into the dark over the Hanging Water, screaming, with a man hanging off it, and the scream went up and up and grew thin, and thinner, and became a whine, and became nothing anybody on the *Kestrel* could hear, and did not stop. It never stopped. Vane knew that. Somewhere over the Valley of Velocity tonight the Grandmother was still going up, faster than she had been a moment ago, with Pennick's arm through her strap. She would be going up tomorrow, and next year, past the clouds, past the place where the air ran out, for ever.

It was the Valley. What was moving went on getting faster. Nobody could tell which a thing would be until it was.

The ship lurched, hard, back the other way as the tank tore out of her, and the swing checked, and held.

---

He could not have said how long he stood at the wheel. A second. Less. Then the arithmetic came back, the way it always came back, coldly, the clerk in the back room totting up a column while the house burned.

*Three tanks. Cell four holed. Two hundred and twenty feet and falling, and falling faster, because in the Valley everything that moved went faster. The push gone. The swing checked. Ten guns on the embankment reloading. Thirty seconds, perhaps forty. The viaduct below, forty arches, the Express on it. The column on the road behind the embankment. Every man of it would be on the viaduct inside ten minutes.*

The viaduct.

He looked at it. The great grey arches marching across the Valley floor under the curtain. Each one a hundred and forty feet high at the crown and eighty wide between the piers.

And the guns on the embankment at the east end, pointing up.

He turned the wheel.

"Tobiah," he said. "The tanks."

The boy in the hatch looked at him. White as milk, his eyes enormous, his hand pressed to his bound side.

"I can't get up," said Tobiah.

"I know. Get up anyway. The tanks. Full lift on the Dawnday, hold the others. Stop her falling. I'm going to put her under the bridge."

Tobiah stared at him.

Then he put his hands on the coaming of the hatch and pulled himself up out of it. Vane saw his face go grey, and saw the bandage over his ribs go dark at the edge. He got one knee onto the deck, and the other. He crawled across the wet planks to the tank-cradle on his hands and knees, past the black twelve-pound ball lying steaming on the deck, past the engineer sitting against the rail. He pulled himself up the cradle's frame by the pipes into the well where Pennick had stood, among the three tanks that were left.

He put his hand on the Dawnday's master-valve. His hand was shaking so badly Vane could see it from the wheel.

"Full lift," said Tobiah. His voice cracked in half. "Full lift on the Dawnday."

The Dawnday groaned. The fall slowed, and slowed, and became a long gliding slant down through the rain toward the arches, and Vane put the wheel over and took her down it.

---

He took her in under the viaduct.

It was the only roof in the Valley. He had seen it the way the engineer had seen the cage on the Pilgrim Chain: the only thing between the guns and the sky. Eleven guns on the embankment at the north end, laid straight up. To fire at a ship under the viaduct they would have to lower their barrels and fire flat along the line of the viaduct itself, through its own piers. Every ball would bring a span of the Tramway down on their own column, or go on through pier after pier and out over the Valley's south wall and the country beyond it, getting faster, into whatever was there, for ever.

He did not think they would. He thought there was a gunnery officer on that embankment who had been given a table and a time and a height and a place, and had laid his guns exactly, and was now looking at a ship going down under a bridge and doing a new sum, and not liking the answer.

The *Kestrel* went down through the curtain. The standing rain burst on her and ran off her and whined away in every direction. The arches came up round her. Grey stone, wet, enormous, eighty feet between the piers. He took her in between the twenty-ninth and thirtieth piers, under the crown of the arch, with the stone a yard over the envelope and the Valley floor forty feet below the keel. There was a little river down there, which had stood still for twenty-one years in a long silver ribbon with its ripples on it like a drawing of a river.

And he stopped her.

Not on the wind. There was no wind under the arch, and in the Valley a ship did not slow because you asked her to. He held her fall with the Dawnday, a breath at a time, Tobiah at the valve. Then he brought her starboard bow in against the thirtieth pier the way you bring a boat alongside a quay, and felt the stem kiss the wet stone, and grind, and hold. The *Kestrel* hung under the thirtieth arch of the Hanging Water with her nose to the pier and her keel over the stopped river, and did not move.

What was still stayed still.

---

They heard the column come onto the viaduct eight minutes later.

Boots. Hundreds of them, on the old sleepers, overhead, coming down through the stone of the arch. A marching column of the Arbiterium coming at the double out along the Tramway from the embankment, a long rolling thunder of feet that came down through the arch into the envelope and into the deck. Every man aboard felt it in his teeth.

They did not move. Nobody moved.

He had them where they had stopped. Vesper at the bow, with her quiet thrown round the forward half of the ship like a cloak. Mags at the windlass, watching the arch. Tobiah in the cradle with his hand on the valve and his face grey and his lips moving. The knight at the stern rail with the dark Blade. The woman with the wings in the lee of the cradle, very still, with her hand over the priest's mouth. Sable in the hatch.

And the engineer, sitting against the starboard rail where he had sat down.

His bare arm lay across his knees. It was cyan again, the green gone back into the vein. But the vein had moved, and Vane could see where it had gone. It came up out of the shoulder now, along the line of the collarbone, under the edge of the glass at his throat, a long cold thread of sea-green. And at the very top, where the glass came up toward the hollow of his throat, the green had reached the edge.

The engineer was looking at Vane. He did not say anything.

He did not need to. Vane knew what he was thinking. He had been thinking it himself for eight minutes.

*He's listening.*

The old man. In his white room. Through the glass. Every word said within thirty feet of it. If anybody on this deck said *viaduct* or *thirtieth arch*, or even *we're under the bridge*, the old man would hear it. And the old man would say it to somebody, and that somebody had a chime or a runner or a semaphore, and the column overhead would know.

So nobody said anything.

Kaelen sat against the rail with his arm across his knees, and very slowly, without a sound, he reached out with his right hand and picked up the black canvas sack from the deck where he had thrown it. He pulled it back over the glass arm, to the shoulder, and tied it off at the neck. Then he put his right hand over his own mouth, and held it there, and looked at Vane over it.

Vane looked back.

Overhead, the column stopped.

---

They stood on the viaduct for an hour.

He would remember that hour as the longest of his life, longer than the ten heartbeats at the hawser, and he had thought nothing would ever be longer than those. The *Kestrel* hung under the arch, still, in the dark, with the stopped river under her and the standing rain all round. Overhead, through the wet stone of the arch, a column of the Arbiterium stood on the rails of the Iron Tramway, and talked.

He could hear them. Stone carried. Vesper's quiet covered the forward half of the ship and not the arch, and the voices came down through the masonry the way voices come down through the floor of a house: words lost, tone clear. Men being given orders. Men reporting. A voice, once, very close over the envelope, so clear he could pick the words out:

"—*not on the line. Not on the line, sir. Nothing on the line but the Express—*"

And another voice. Cold and level and quite young, a voice used to being obeyed without having to raise it.

"*Then it's under the line. Where else would it go? Send to the embankment. I want the thirtieth through the fortieth arches swept with lanterns from the river.*"

He felt every person on the deck hear that.

"*Sir. Sir, the river. Nobody can go down to the river, sir. It's the Valley. A man can't walk down there, sir, he'd—*"

"*Then lower the lanterns on lines.*"

A pause.

"*Sir. The lanterns. Once they're swinging, sir—*"

Another pause, longer. Then the cold young voice again, and it had changed a little, the way the voice of a man changes when he has been given a fact he cannot argue with by somebody he despises for knowing it.

"*Very well. Hold the viaduct. Nobody fires. Nobody throws. Nobody lowers anything. We'll have it by morning when the light comes. It can't hang there for ever.*"

The boots moved away. Not all of them. Some stayed.

*We'll have it by morning when the light comes.* Vane looked at the stopped river, and the arch, and the curtain of rain standing between the piers on either side. At first light the column would see the *Kestrel* from the embankment, plain as a fish in a bowl, hanging under the thirtieth arch. And the gunnery officer would do his sums again, and find a way to lay one gun.

*It can't hang there for ever.*

No. But in the Valley, a thing that started to move would go on moving, and go on getting faster.

He knew then what he was going to do.

---

He could not say it. That was the worst of it. He could not say anything. He had a ship's company spread round a dark deck under a bridge with a column overhead and a listening glass on the foredeck. He had to tell them, every one, what he was going to do, without a word.

So he did it the way the *Kestrel* had done things for two months. He took the grease-pencil out of his coat and wrote on the deck.

He wrote it on the wet planks at the foot of the wheel in big black letters, crouched, one-handed, with the rain running off the end of his nose. Then he caught Mags's eye and pointed, and Mags came aft along the rail without a sound, crouched, and read it. She looked at him. She went forward, to the engineer, and crouched in front of him, and made the signs in the dark, slowly, with her one good hand, the *Kestrel*'s hand-speech, a word at a time.

Vane watched Kaelen read them.

He watched the engineer close his eyes.

When he opened them he looked aft, past Mags, at Vane. And then he looked down at his own left arm in its black sack across his knees: at the shape of it, nine inches too long, and at the open collar of his shirt, where the edge of the glass came up out of the cloth along the bone, and the green thread in it ran right to the top.

He nodded.

---

It went like this.

The engineer got up. Slowly, without a sound, with Mags's good hand under his right elbow. He went to the bow, past Vesper on the bitts, into her quiet. He stood at the stem, where the forestay came down to the bowsprit, facing the thirtieth pier of the Hanging Water viaduct, where the *Kestrel*'s stem lay against it. It was a wall of grey wet stone going up into the dark.

He pulled the sack off the arm. The cyan came out into the arch, faint, and the stone round it took the light and shone.

He put his left hand flat on the pier.

Vane watched him. Everyone watched him. Tobiah in the cradle with his hand on the valve. The knight at the stern. Sable in the hatch. The woman with the wings, with her hand over her priest's mouth.

*What is still stays still,* Vane had written on the deck. *What is moving goes on getting faster. The engineer pushes off. Once. Then nobody touches anything.*

Kaelen pushed.

He did not push hard. He could not have pushed hard; he had a hundredweight of glass on his arm and an iron ball's worth of speed drunk into it, and his face in the light of it was grey. He leaned on the pier with the glass hand flat on the stone, the way you lean on a quay to fend a boat off. The *Kestrel* moved.

A foot. Away from the pier, out from under the arch, down the Valley toward the west. Slow. Slower than a man walking. The envelope slid along under the crown of the arch, and out from under it, and the standing rain came onto the deck again.

And she did not stop.

She went on. Out from under the viaduct, across the stopped river, along the Valley floor, forty feet up, in the dark. At the speed of a man walking. Then a little more than that. Then a little more. The rain burst on her and ran and whined away. The viaduct fell away behind her into the curtain, arch by arch, and the column on top of it did not see her go, because she made no noise. She had no engine and no sail and nothing in her that turned or pushed. She was simply moving, and in the Valley what was moving went on getting faster.

A man running. A horse trotting. Far behind, at the north end of the viaduct, the red points of the slow-matches on the embankment dwindled and went out of sight, like the lights of a town seen from the back of a coach at night.

A horse at the gallop.

Nobody spoke. Nobody touched anything. Kaelen stood at the bow with his glass hand still out in front of him where the pier had been. Behind him, Vesper sat on the bitts with her quiet round the forward half of the ship like a hand cupped round a candle.

The Valley went by under them in the dark. The stopped river. The green floor. The curtain of standing rain thinning, and gone, and then clear air, starlit, with the Valley walls going by on either side faster and faster. The wind of their own going came onto the deck and grew. The wasp-whine of the rain on the rigging was gone. In its place was a new sound, a long rising hiss along the envelope's flanks, like a sledge on snow.

Faster than any ship he had ever sailed. Faster than the *Wave-Cutter* in the Roaring Strait with the Drift behind her and every sail drawing. Faster than a skyship had any business going, and still going faster.

"*Vane*," said Sable, in the hatch, very quietly. She had worked it out. He had known she would. "*The end of the Valley.*"

He knew. He had known when he wrote it on the deck. Eleven miles of Valley, and then the head-wall at the west end where the spire-root stood, and the Saddle beyond it. And past the Saddle, ordinary air, where the law was whole and what was moving slowed down, as things should, as things had always done, at once.

He did not know how fast they would be going when they reached it. He had done the sum three times and got three different numbers, and the smallest of them was bad.

"Tobiah," he said. Aloud. It did not matter now; they were three miles from the viaduct and going faster than any column could march. "When I say, give her everything you've got. Full lift, all three. And Mags, every pound of ballast over the side. Everything."

"*Everything?*"

"Everything. We're going to go up. As high as she'll go, as fast as she'll go, before we hit the edge. The higher we are when we hit it, the more air there is to slow us down before we hit anything else." He looked at the Valley's head-wall coming toward them out of the dark, a black ridge against the stars, with a single spark of cold blue light on its crest where the spire-root stood. Coming quickly. Coming more quickly every second. "Everyone else. Hold on to something that's bolted down. And don't let go of it till I say."

---

At a mile out Tobiah opened all three tanks to the stops, and Mags cut the ballast-bags away one-handed, and the *Kestrel* went up.

She went up the way the Grandmother had gone up: slowly for the first moment and then, because she was moving, faster. The Valley walls dropped away. The head-wall dropped away beneath the bow. They came up out of the cleft of the Valley into the open night over the Saddle at a speed Vane would never be able to put a number to. The spire-root's blue spark flashed past beneath the keel.

And they hit the edge.

It was like flying into a wall made of feathers. Not a crash; a *stop*. Everything that had been moving with them stopped too, all at once, from the stem to the stern. The law of the world came back round the *Kestrel* like a door slamming, and every ounce of speed she had gathered in eleven miles of Valley went out of her in about a second and a half, and had to go somewhere.

It went into the ship.

He was thrown into the wheel and heard his ribs go. He heard the envelope above him split. Not one seam: a dozen, a long rippling crack all down the starboard flank like a sheet torn lengthways. He heard the shrouds go on the port side, one, two, three, snapping like gunshots, and the foretopmast come down across the bow. He heard the press shift on its bolts on the foredeck with a scream of iron. He heard, below, in the hold, every crate on the *Kestrel* slam forward against its lashings at once, and four hundred voices that had been saying their names in the dark cry out together. He heard the Aegis's iron groan.

Kaelen went over the bow.

He saw it. The engineer had been standing at the stem with his hand out, and when the law came back he went forward the way everything went forward, off his feet, over the bowsprit. And at the last instant his right hand caught the forestay. He hung there off the front of the ship by one hand over two thousand feet of night, with the glass arm swinging under him, lit.

Vesper had him by the collar before Vane could move. She was off the bitts and onto the bowsprit and had one white hand knotted in the back of his coat, and the claws of the other dug into the timber of the spar to the first knuckle. She held him there. Then she pulled, and he came up over the bow onto the deck and lay on his back on the planks, breathing.

The *Kestrel* drifted. Slowly. At the speed of a man walking, over the dark Saddle, under the stars, in ordinary air, with her envelope split down one side and her foretopmast in the water-butt and her shrouds hanging in loops. And she was still up.

Vane let go of the wheel and found that he could not stand, and sat down on the deck with his back against the binnacle.

---

At dawn they counted.

He did it, because it was what he did. He sat on the deck against the binnacle with three ribs cracked and the stitches torn open in two places on his face, and counted them as the light came up grey over the Saddle.

The engineer, on his back on the foredeck where Vesper had dropped him, awake, with his right hand at his throat. He had not said a word since the bow.

The Cantor, in the hold, on her cot. Bettany had tied her to it for the crossing, which was the only reason she was there. She had a cut over her eye from the cot-frame, and she had written on her slate, in the dark, by feel, and sent it up by Hetty: *WHO?*

Bettany. Hetty Bram, with a broken wrist from the galley stove-rail, setting it herself with her teeth.

The knight, at the stern rail. She had held on.

The woman with the wings, in the lee of the cradle, with the priest. She had wrapped her broken wings round him and herself and the cradle's frame like a cloak round a post. One of the long plated blades was bent now where it had not been bent before, and she had not made a sound.

Mags at the windlass, with her one good arm wrapped through the windlass-bars to the shoulder. Bruised from neck to hip, and she would not let Bettany look.

Vesper at the bow, sitting on the bitts with her claws still dug into the wood, looking east. Mote in her coat, leaning east.

The Aegis in the hold, which had thrown itself across the forward crates when the law came back and held them against the bulkhead with its back, and had a new dent in its shoulder the size of a man's head, and was humming.

Sable in the hold, among the crates, with the Ledger.

Tobiah in the tank-cradle, in Pennick's place, where he had been all night. His bandage was black with blood to the hip. He was asleep sitting up, with his hand still on the Dawnday's master-valve, because he had been told to hold it and nobody had told him he could let go. Bettany had tried to move him at the fifth hour, and he had woken and looked at her and said *not yet, she's still settling*, and gone back to sleep.

Thirteen.

He did the sum again. He had done it every night for two months. Last night it had come out thirteen.

Twelve. Counting the one in iron.

And three hundred and ninety-six, in the hold, saying their names.

---

Sable came up at the sixth hour with the Ledger under her arm and stood in front of him.

"I need his name," she said.

He looked up at her.

"His name. For the entry." The archivist's face was grey and her eyes were red at the rims, and her voice did not change at all. "Pennick. Engineer, of the *Drift-Kestrel*. Lost over the Valley of Velocity, the fifteenth of Ashfall, the dead hour. I have *Pennick*. I don't know if it's his first name or his last. I don't know where he was from. I don't know his age." She opened the Ledger to a page. Vane saw it: thirty-six lines in her small clear hand, already entered, a number and a name and a date on each, and against every one the same word. *Broken.* "I need his name."

Vane opened his mouth.

He had known Pennick for two years. He had hired him off the dock at Stillwater Landing for two sovereigns a month and his keep, because he had stood at the foot of the gangway talking to the *Kestrel*'s lift-tanks as if they could hear him, and Vane had liked that. Pennick had slept in the tank-cradle. He had named the tanks. He had called Vane *Captain* every single time he spoke to him, even drunk, even the night in Fortis when Vane had been so drunk he could not stand and Pennick had carried him up the ladder.

He did not know his first name. He had never asked.

"*Jonah*," said Tobiah, from the tank-cradle.

They both looked round.

The boy was awake. He was sitting where he had been all night, among the three tanks, with his hand on the valve and his bandage black and his face the colour of candle-wax.

"Jonah Pennick," said Tobiah. "From Greystairs, in the Peaks. He was twenty-four. He had a sister in the Lodestone Vaults. He was going to send her his wages at the Tramway, if we got there, so she could get out." He looked at the valve under his hand. "He told me. In the engine-room. Every night, after you'd all gone to sleep. He talked the whole time. He talked to the tanks and he talked to me. He said I was the only one who'd listen to him about the gaskets." His mouth moved. "He said the Grandmother didn't like to be rushed."

Sable wrote it down. *Jonah Pennick. Of Greystairs. Twenty-four.*

"She didn't," said Tobiah. "She never did." And then he put his face down against the cold copper of the Dawnday tank, and his shoulders began to shake, and he did not take his hand off the valve.

Nobody told him he could.

---

They went down into the hold at the seventh hour, all of them who could walk, and left the engineer on the foredeck.

He did not have to be sent. He had got up from where Vesper had dropped him, and gone and sat with his back against the press, and when Vane went past him to the hatch he had looked up and said, quite steadily, in his ringing double voice:

"I'll be here. Don't tell me anything. Don't tell me where."

Vane stopped.

"I should have thought of it," said Kaelen. "On Dawnday night. I heard you talking at the wheel. I was half-awake. I heard you say the Hanging Water, and I thought, *that's a good crossing*, and I went back to sleep." He looked at the sack on his arm. "I should have stood up and walked to the stern. I've done it a hundred times. I didn't."

"It's not your—"

"Don't." The engineer did not raise his voice. "Don't say that near the glass. He'll like it." He put his head back against the press. "Go on. Go down. Write it on the wall. Write it small."

---

They wrote it on the wall.

On the forward bulkhead of the hold, in grease-pencil, among two months of other plans. The Wendmere tower drawn from three sides. The Salt Stair. The Pilgrim Chain and its anchor-houses. The cage. They stood round it in the lamplight among the crates, in the murmur of three hundred and ninety-six names: Vane and Sable and Mags, the knight with the Blade, Vesper in the shadow with her quiet, Hetty with her wrist strapped, Bettany. Lyra was propped on her cot at the end of the hold where she could see, with the slate in her lap.

Nobody said anything. That was the rule now. Not in the hold. Not anywhere on the ship. The engineer was thirty-one feet above them on the foredeck and Vane was no longer willing to trust a foot.

Vane wrote: *NOT THE REACH.*

They looked at it.

He wrote under it: *Column on Tramway. Pickets every crossing. They know we want the Reach. Everyone knows. War there in 7 wks. We go there, we fight the column with 3 tanks, a split bag, 12 hands.* He stopped. He wrote: *11 hands.* He looked at the number and did not cross it out. *And 396 in the hold, nowhere to put them.*

Sable took the pencil from him.

She stood in front of the bulkhead with the pencil in her ink-stained fingers for a long time. Then she wrote in her small clear clerk's hand, under his, two words.

*SCHEDULE F.*

He looked at her. She did not look at him. She went on writing.

*Office routing. My hand. Incomplete draws — failed pressings, partial — "Schedule F, disposal." Disposal at Null-Watch. Over the Rift lip. I redrafted the routing in 416 — form F/3, the one still in use. I wrote "disposal." I never asked what it meant at the other end.* She stopped. Then she wrote: *I assumed it meant dead.*

Vane read it twice.

He knew something. He had not known he knew it until he read the word *Null-Watch* in Sable's hand. Then it came up out of a winter night in '18, out of a card-table at the Watch's supply depot at Cold Harbour on the Rift road, where he had put down the ninth of his eleven consignments for the Office and not asked what became of it. Across the table that night there had been a Null-Watch quartermaster with a face like a boot: the same man, it turned out, that he had won *Last Rites* off at cards in his first year as a captain. The quartermaster had remembered him, and the pistol, and laughed about it, and got drunker. Toward the dead hour he had said something to the table that nobody had laughed at, because he had not meant it to be funny.

Vane took the pencil back.

*Null-Watch QM — same one, Last Rites — Cold Harbour, '18. Said the Watch lowers bread over the lip. At night. Baskets. "Something down there that eats."*

He wrote it and looked at it. Then he added: *He said "eats." He didn't say "something." I think he said "someone."*

Sable read it. He watched her read it. He watched her understand that there was a rope going down into the Continental Rift, at night, from the lead walls of Null-Watch. That it had been going down for years. That at the bottom of it something ate. That the Office of the Catalogue had been sending people over that lip for three years with a docket in her handwriting that said *disposal*.

She took the pencil and wrote one more word under it, very small, and underlined it, and gave the pencil back to him and went and stood with her back to the crates and her arms folded and her face to the wall of the hull.

The word was *Rendered*.

It was what the clerks on the third floor had called them. Never on paper. Over coffee.

Vane looked at the bulkhead for a long time. He looked at the plans of two months, the tower and the stair and the chain. He looked at the three hundred and ninety-six crates — no, not crates; the crates were twelve, and one was in pieces — at the eleven whole crates round him in the lamplight, murmuring. He looked at the slate in the Cantor's lap at the end of the hold, where she had written something and was holding it up for him to read.

*WHERE NOBODY FOLLOWS.*

He thought about that. The Rift. The Maw at the end of the world, the eastern mouth of the thing at the bottom of the trench off Cape Dissonance. The place where the column would not march, and the Church would not send a Flambeau, and the Office never looked, because the Office had written *disposal* on the docket and filed it.

He wrote the bearing on the wall. *ESE. 5 days if she holds. Null-Watch.* Then, because he could not help it, because he had always been this man and it was too late now to be any other: *Hope they like cards.*

He heard Mags, behind him, make the small sound in her chest that was the only laugh she had.

Nobody else laughed. He had not expected them to.

---

He went up at the eighth hour to take the wheel.

The engineer was on the foredeck where he had left him, against the press, with the arm across his chest. As Vane came up out of the hatch Kaelen turned his head and looked at him, and then, deliberately, looked away, out over the starboard rail at the brown hills going by beneath them.

Vane took the wheel from Mags. He brought the *Kestrel* round. Slowly, carefully, with a split bag and three tanks and the foretopmast lashed along the deck, until the sun stood fine on her port bow, low and white, and the shadow of the mast lay back along the deck toward the starboard quarter.

East-south-east.

He did not say it. He did not have to. He saw the engineer feel the ship come round. He saw him watch the shadow of the mast swing across the deck, the way a man who has spent his life reading a compass reads the sun. He saw him work it out.

And he saw him not ask.

Kaelen sat against the press with his face turned away from the sun, and his right hand at his throat, over the place where the green thread came up under the glass. He did not say where they were going. He did not say anything. The sun came up on the port bow, and the *Kestrel* went east-south-east toward the end of the world with a dead man's tanks singing thin and uneven in her cradle. And the engineer sat on the foredeck, the one man aboard who was not allowed to know where he was going, and looked carefully in the wrong direction.

Vane counted. It was what he did.

Eleven, and the one in iron. Three hundred and ninety-six in the hold. A boy at the valves who had not let go. A ball on the deck that nobody would touch. And somewhere above the Valley of Velocity, going up, faster than it had been a moment ago and slower than it would be in a moment more, a copper tank called the Grandmother, and a man whose name he had not known, hanging from her by one arm. Talking to her, perhaps, all the way up.

He hoped so. Pennick had always talked to the tanks.

## CHAPTER TWO
### The Morning Refusal

> *STANDING ORDER NINE. A trooper whose partner falls silent will sound the alarm at once. He will not first look to see why.*
>
> — Painted over every door in Null-Watch, at the height of a man's eyes

*East-south-east. Memoryday the fifteenth of Ashfall, to Dawnday the twenty-first. Null-Watch.*

---

For five days nobody told him where he was going, and he did not ask, and the old man talked to him once.

He had known it would come. He had felt it coming since the Valley the way you feel weather in an old break: the attention at the back of his neck, the sense of somebody standing behind his chair. It had been there for seven years. Most days it was as faint as a draught under a door. Since the Valley it had been close. Patient. Interested. A man leaning in to hear the end of a story.

It came on the second night, Nullday, at the dead hour, when he was alone on the foredeck. The ship was asleep round him and the bag was patched with sailcloth, and Tobiah was snoring at the valves like a dog by a fire.

"I'm sorry about the young man with the tanks."

It came out of the glass. Not out of the air: out of the arm, out of the bone of it, the way it had come to him once before in the Tuning Room at the Malting. Quiet and courteous and very tired, and pitched so low that nobody three feet away would have heard it. A voice like a doctor sitting on the edge of a bed.

"I want you to know that. I did tell my son where you would cross. I tell Aurelian what he needs to know. That's the arrangement between us, and I have kept it for thirty years." A breath, slow and careful, a man who had to think about each one. "I did not tell him to fire. He decided that. He is a very literal young man, and he thought he could take the ship whole, and he was wrong, and a boy from the Peaks went up into the sky on a copper tank. I heard it. I heard the strap go." A pause. "I am sorry. I don't expect you to believe it. I would like you to know that it is true."

Kaelen did not answer.

He sat with his back against the press and his right hand over his mouth, and looked at the stars over the bow, and did not answer. He had decided that on the first night, in the dark under the thirtieth arch with the column's boots overhead: whatever came out of the glass, he would give it nothing. Not a word, not a breath it could read. He would be a wall.

"They haven't told you where you're going," said the voice. "That's wise of them. I'd have done the same." The faint sound of something that was nearly a laugh, and too tired to finish. "It's lonely, though. I know. I have spent seven years in a white room listening to people who don't know I'm there. You are the only one who has ever known, and you won't speak to me. I do understand."

Kaelen looked at the stars.

"It doesn't matter, you know," said the old man gently. "Wherever it is. When you get there, people will say where they are. They always do. They can't help it. *Mind the step, the wall's slippery here, it's cold on the Watch at night.* People are always telling each other where they are." A breath. "I'm not threatening you. I'm only telling you how it is. I should hate for you to think the silence will keep anyone safe. It won't. It only keeps you alone."

Then the attention eased off, like a hand taken off a shoulder, and was faint again.

Kaelen sat on the foredeck with his hand over his mouth until the sun came up. Then he took the hand away and found that he had bitten the inside of his own palm through to the blood without noticing.

---

Bettany measured him on the third morning. Lyra came up on deck to watch.

She was not supposed to be up. She was supposed to be on her cot with her throat bound and her mouth shut and a cup of honey-water at her elbow, and she had been there for five days, sleeping and waking and writing on her slate. On the third morning she came up the companion-ladder in a blanket, white as a sheet, with Hetty Bram's good hand under her elbow and the slate on its cord round her neck. She sat down beside him on the deck against the press without asking.

Bettany knelt in front of him with the knotted string.

It was Lyra's string, the one she had made in the Reach a lifetime ago to measure the glass on his collarbone. A length of waxed thread with knots in it at every quarter-inch. Bettany laid the end of it in the hollow of his throat, under the jaw, very gently, and drew it out along the bone to the edge of the glass, and looked.

She did not say anything. She showed it to Lyra.

Lyra looked at the string. Then she looked at his throat. He watched her eyes go to the place where the cyan came up under the skin along the collarbone, and stop, and go on to the thread of green. The thread had come into the glass out of the drowned bell of Wendmere and lain along the bone for two weeks like a vein of copper in a rock. Since the Valley it had run right up to the edge, to the place where the glass ended and the skin began, as if it were looking for a way out.

She took the slate off her neck and wrote on it. She held it up so that he could see.

*HALF A FINGER.*

He nodded.

She rubbed it out with the heel of her hand and wrote again. It took her a long time, because her hand was not steady. When she held it up he read it, and then he looked away out over the rail, because he could not look at her face while he read it twice.

*NOT YET.* And under it, a line. Drawn hard, with the chalk pressed so that it squealed on the slate and broke.

---

The country changed on the fourth day.

He watched it from the foredeck, because it was the only thing he was allowed to watch. The brown hills of the Heights' eastern foot went down into a long rolling grassland, empty, under a high grey sky that got lower by the hour. Then, toward evening, the grass changed. It did not die. It stopped.

He saw it first in a field. A herd of cattle, perhaps forty head, lying in long grass by a stream. Every one of them dead, every one of them perfect. Lying on their sides with their legs out straight and their eyes open and their coats glossy, the grass round them green and untrampled. No crows. No flies. No smell, when the wind came round. They had been lying in that field, he thought, for years. They looked as if they had lain down an hour ago.

He knew what it was. He had read about it in the Conservatory, in a chapter nobody had believed: the Unrotting. The first stage of the eighth law going. Nothing decays. Then the second stage, which nobody had ever described, because nobody who had seen it had come back to write it down.

Sable had come up the ladder with the Ledger under her arm. She looked at the field for a long moment with her hands clasped behind her back. Her mouth opened, and closed. Then she went below again without a word, and he understood that she had been about to name it and had remembered, just in time, what he was.

There was a village after the cattle. Twenty houses and a mill round a green, with washing on the lines and smoke in no chimney. A woman was lying on the green on her back with a basket beside her and her face turned up to the sky. Her cheeks were pink. Her hair moved in the wind. He watched until it was gone behind them and did not see her move.

After the village the wind changed.

He felt it before he understood it. It had been a cold wind out of the east all day, hissing in the rigging, singing very faintly in the stays the way wind always sings in a ship's rigging. Toward dusk the singing stopped. The wind did not stop. It came on harder, cold and steady and dry, out of the east, pressing on the patched bag so that the *Kestrel* heeled and Vane, at the wheel, had to hold her up into it. The rigging was as taut as a harp. And it made no sound. None. A wind blowing through a ship's shrouds with no pitch in it at all, like a wind in a dream.

His arm went quiet.

Not silent. Not yet. But for seven years it had rung, five hundred and twenty-eight, a high faint clear tone under everything, so constant he only heard it when it changed. And now, in the pitchless wind, it was thinner. Flatter. As if somebody had put a hand over the mouth of the bell.

He looked down at it in its sack across his knees, and then up, east, over the bow.

And there, on the far edge of the world, under the low grey sky, something that was not a horizon.

---

He could not describe it afterward. He tried, to Lyra, on her slate. He wrote it six different ways and rubbed out every one.

The land ran east to the edge of sight, grey-green and flat. Then it stopped. Not at a cliff, not at a sea. It simply stopped being there. Where the far edge of the world should have been there was a band of something that was not dark and not light and not any colour he had a name for. When he looked at it he found, a moment later, that he was looking at the deck, or the rigging, or his own hand, and could not remember turning his head.

*Forgetting that you were looking.* Somebody had said that to him once, in the Conservatory of Bores, about a place on the far edge of the continent that nobody went to. He had thought it was a figure of speech.

In front of it, on the edge of the land, black against the not-colour, a line of walls.

Null-Watch.

So that was where they were going.

He sat on the foredeck and looked at it and did not say the name aloud. He made himself think it in another way, so that even his mouth would not shape it in his sleep. *That. There. The place at the end.* And he felt, in the bone of his arm, the old man's attention come close, and lean in, and wait for somebody to say it.

---

Nobody did, for a while. The *Kestrel* came down out of the pitchless wind toward the walls at the last of the light, with every soul aboard silent. And the walls came up out of the grey to meet them, and they were the ugliest thing Kaelen had ever seen.

Obsidian. Black volcanic glass, quarried somewhere and hauled here in blocks the size of wagons, laid course on course along the lip of the land for as far as he could see in either direction. The mortar between the blocks was dull grey, and it was lead. The whole wall was laced through and through, inside and out, with cable: thick grey tungsten cable strung from iron eyes, in great slack loops and tight diagonals, like a net thrown over the wall to hold it down. Every two hundred yards there was a squat round tower, forty of them along four miles of wall, and on every tower, under a lead hood, a gun. Not cannon. Great flared brass mouths like the bells of horns, nine feet across, pointing out over the edge of the land at the thing that was not a horizon.

The dampening batteries. He had read about them in the Conservatory. He had not believed the drawings.

And everywhere, on every wall-walk and tower and stair, there were people, and every one of them was talking.

He heard it as the ship came down. Under the pitchless wind, rising up off the walls like the noise off a market: voices. Hundreds of them. Not shouting. Talking, steadily, in ordinary conversational tones, all at once, all along the wall. And as the *Kestrel* came down level with the nearest tower he could make out words.

"—and then you take your onions, and you want them sliced thin, not chopped, thin, and you want a heavy pan—"

"—forty-one, forty-two, forty-three, and that's the cable-eyes on the north face of Six done, now the south face, forty-four—"

"—told her, I said, you can have the blue or you can have the green, but you can't have both and expect your mother not to say something—"

"—Rootday, Hearthday, Massday, Stasisday, Dawnday, Driftday, Memoryday, Nullday, Rootday, Hearthday—"

Men and women in grey, in pairs, on the walls, in long coats with lead plates sewn into them that made them walk like people wading. Every one of them talking to the one beside them, on and on, about nothing, about onions and cable-eyes and the days of the week. Faces set and tired, eyes on the edge of the world. The talk rose off the walls in a long unbroken murmur, and it did not stop, and it never stopped. He would learn that over the next three days. It went on day and night, in every corridor, on every stair, in the barracks while men slept. Paired watchers sat by the bunks and talked to the sleepers, to keep the sleepers' breathing company.

And nobody, anywhere, was silent.

Except, he saw, at intervals along the very edge of the land beyond the wall, outside it, on the bare grey ground between the obsidian and the not-colour, figures standing. Tall. Hooded. In robes that hung straight to the ground without a fold, dull grey, heavy. Lead. Standing quite still, a hundred yards apart, facing the edge, like posts along a field. Not talking. Not moving.

He looked at them for a long time. None of them turned round.

---

They came down to the Watch's mooring-mast at dusk on Massday the nineteenth of Ashfall. A woman was waiting at the foot of it who did not speak at all.

She was fifty, perhaps, short and broad and grey-haired, with her hair cut close to the skull like a man's. She wore a long lead-plated coat like the others, and over it a breastplate of plain dark iron, dented and scratched. A general's sash, faded. She stood at the foot of the mast-ladder with her feet apart and her hands behind her back, and watched them come down.

Beside her, a young man in grey with a rota-number stitched on his breast instead of a name, 94, talking. He was talking to her. Steadily, quietly, as the ship came in.

"—and that's the third mast-crew to the windlass, General, and that's the lines going on, and it's a Coast skyship by the cut of her, General, and she's been knocked about, the bag's patched on the starboard side, a lot, and there's a lot of people on her—"

The woman did not answer him. She looked up the mast at the *Kestrel* as Vane brought her in. Her eyes went over the patched bag and the lashed foretopmast and the three tanks in the cradle where there should have been four. They went over the knight at the stern rail with the Blade, and the woman with the broken wings and the priest. They went over Vesper at the bow, in her quiet, and stayed there for one long moment. Then they came to Kaelen on the foredeck, and to the sack on his arm, and the open collar of his shirt.

She lifted her right hand and struck her breastplate with her knuckles. Twice, sharp. Then a long one, a flat-handed blow. Then three quick taps with the side of a finger.

"The General says," said 94, without a pause, "welcome to Null-Watch. The General says, keep talking."

---

It was iron-cant. He had heard of it: the old signal-code of the Fortis sappers, tapped on pipes in the deep galleries where voices did not carry. Theresa Ward had commanded Null-Watch in it for six years, by knuckle and palm and fingertip on the iron over her heart, because she had not had a voice since the night the Rift opened.

He would hear the story over the next three days, in pieces, from a dozen troopers, in the endless talk on the walls. Nobody told it straight, because the whole point of talk on the Watch was never to tell anything straight. But it came out of the talk the way a shape comes out of fog.

In the spring of 413 the Synod had drilled the Gate of Decay. The eighth spire, the one at the very edge of the world, the rest between notes. They had drilled it to balance the Cascade that had come out of the Dawning Spire the year before. And the Gate had cracked, and the continent had cracked behind it, a gash forty miles long running back from the Maw into the land. The first night, everything that had been holding still at the bottom of the world for ten thousand years had come up out of the gash at once.

There had been a garrison then, a small one, old, half a regiment of lead-coated men minding a spire nobody visited. Theresa Ward had been a colonel of their gunners. The batteries had been new, and nobody knew how to lay them. The thing that came up out of the Rift that night could not be seen or heard, only *not*-heard: a widening silence moving along the lip, taking everything that fell into it. And the only thing that held it was the white noise of the guns, laid exactly, fired exactly, all along the line, all night.

She had laid them by voice. Thirty-one hours. Shouting the elevations from tower to tower along the wall in the dark, because the chime-pairs had gone dead in the silence, and the runners who went along the wall did not come back. Battery by battery. Gun by gun. Until the dawn of the second day, when the thing settled back into the dark. Not beaten. Nobody on the Watch had ever called it beaten. Only quiet again, for a while, behind a door the guns held shut. And she had opened her mouth to give the order to cease firing, and nothing had come out.

*She shouted it to rags,* said a woman on Tower Six to her partner, in a voice like someone telling a story about a cousin. *Shouted her voice to bits, and the bits went over the edge, and she's never had it back. Now, the thing with gravy—*

She had commanded the Watch ever since. The Synod had made her a general, because nobody else would take the posting. They filled the garrison up behind her with the condemned, because nobody else would come, and she held the wall and nothing else. Every order in Null-Watch for six years had been tapped out on the iron over her heart.

---

Vane went down the mast-ladder first, one-handed, with his ribs strapped and his face a mess of torn stitches and white skin, and stood in front of her.

"Captain Vane," he said. "Of the *Drift-Kestrel*. I think you've had a chime about us."

Ward looked at him. She tapped. Two short, a pause, one long.

"The General says yes," said 94. "The General says it came on Rootday. From the Arbiterium, the Dread Arbiter's seal, a writ for the man with the glass arm and every soul that has sailed with him, to be held and delivered." He did not stop talking. His voice did not change. "The General says she has read it. The General says she reads everything."

Vane waited.

Ward looked past him, up the mast, at Kaelen on the foredeck. She looked at him for a long time. Then she tapped again, longer: a phrase of it, quick and flat, her knuckles on the iron like a man knocking at a door he does not expect to be opened.

"The General says," said 94, "the Watch holds the wall and nothing else. The General says the Watch obeys the Synod when the Synod is not mad. The General says the Synod has been mad since the spring of 413." He paused, very briefly, for the first time; then went on, as if he had only been drawing breath. "The General says, you are not the wall."

Vane let his breath out.

"The General says," said 94, "you have four hundred people in your hold. She can hear them."

Kaelen saw Vane go still.

"The General says she has stood on this wall for six years listening to a thing that keeps people. She knows what it sounds like when people are kept." 94's voice was quite steady. "The General says, what are you here for?"

Vane looked at her. Kaelen saw him decide something. Then Vane looked up the mast at the foredeck, at him, at the sack on his arm, and Kaelen knew what he had decided before he said it.

He got up. He went aft along the deck, past the press and the tank-cradle and the hatch, past Tobiah at the valves, to the stern rail and beyond it, as far from the bow as the *Kestrel* went. He stood there in the pitchless wind with his back to the mast and his good hand on the taffrail, and counted the planks of the deck. When he had counted them twice he looked round. Vane was at the foot of the ladder with his head bent close to the General's, and the General was looking up at Kaelen over Vane's shoulder. Her face did not change at all. She lifted her hand and tapped once on the iron, a single flat blow, and he did not need 94 to tell him what it meant.

*I see.*

---

They put him on the walls that night. He did not ask why. He knew.

A trooper came for him at the first hour after dark. A big slow man in a lead coat with 211 on his breast, who talked to him all the way up the stair from the mooring-yard without once requiring an answer: about the weather, which was always the same, and the food, which was always worse, and a dog he had had as a boy in the Peaks that could open a gate. He took Kaelen up onto the wall-walk between Towers Five and Six, where the wind came over the obsidian off the edge of the world with no sound in it. He stood beside him at the parapet and went on talking.

Below, in the mooring-yard, the *Kestrel* swung on her mast with lamps lit, and people went up and down the ladder. Far off along the wall, at the foot of Tower Nine, he could see a knot of lanterns. Vane. Sable. Mags. The General. 94. A man he did not know, standing a little apart, very still. And Lyra, a small figure in a blanket, with her slate.

They were talking. He could see their hands move. He could not hear them. He was half a mile away along the wall.

Two-Eleven was still talking about the dog.

"You're quiet," said Two-Eleven, after a while. Not breaking off; it came in the middle of the dog, as if it were part of the story. "You want to watch that, here. You go quiet on the Watch and somebody sounds the bell. Standing order. Nine. They don't look first. They don't ask. They just ring it." He glanced sideways at Kaelen, at the sack, and away. "It's not a punishment. It's because the ones that go quiet are the ones that go. You know? Over. To listen. We've had a hundred and eighty go to listen since the year of the crack, and every one of 'em went quiet first. So we talk."

Kaelen looked at the edge of the world.

"I can't," he said. His voice came out of him double, ringing, the Light and the drowned bell under it, and Two-Eleven's eyes flicked to his throat and back. "Not about anything that matters. Anything I say, somebody else hears."

Two-Eleven thought about that. Kaelen watched him think. A slow, heavy, decent man turning it over.

"Then don't say anything that matters," said Two-Eleven. "Nobody on the Watch does. That's what talk's for. That's the whole trick of it." He nodded at the wall-walk, the towers, the murmur rising off them. "You think anybody here's said anything that matters in six years? Onions. Days of the week. Counting cable-eyes. My dog." He shrugged. "Listen all you like, whoever you are. You'll get the dog."

And Kaelen stood on the wall of Null-Watch in the voiceless wind and, for the first time in five days, laughed. Once, short. He felt the old man's attention lean in to hear it, and did not care.

Then he began to talk.

He talked about the Vance Sequence. It was the only thing he had that was long enough. The eight stages of a bore-head's calibration under load, and every tolerance in every stage, three hundred figures, which he had written at twenty-four and which every Synod engineer on the continent had learned by heart, including Tobiah Marsh at fourteen. He started at the first stage and went on. He said the numbers aloud to the edge of the world in his ringing double voice, steadily, one after another. Seat clearance under a twelve-ton thrust. Flange torque at the third collar. The heat-bleed figure that nobody believed until the Foundry. On and on, numbers that had killed eleven thousand and forty people on a morning seven years ago, and that meant nothing now to anybody, and that the old man had certainly heard before.

Two-Eleven listened to him with every sign of interest, and nodded, and said *is that so*, and *well I never*, and *fancy*, and Kaelen talked until the third hour.

At the third hour the knot of lanterns at Tower Nine moved. They went along the foot of the wall to a postern-gate in the obsidian, and through it, out onto the bare grey ground beyond. Toward the edge.

---

He watched them go. Two-Eleven watched them go. The man Kaelen did not know went first. A big old man in a lead coat, with something on his back like a pedlar's pack, and a lantern, going out across the grey ground toward the not-colour at a steady walk. Then the General. Then Vane and Sable and the rest behind.

They did not go to the edge itself. They went along it, a little back from it, south, the way the wall went, toward a place where a great buttress of rock ran out from the foot of Tower Nine toward the Rift like a pier into a sea. And on the end of it, a timber frame. A crane. A gallows-shape of black oak with a wheel on top, and a rope.

The old man in the lead coat went out along the buttress to the crane. He took the thing off his back and set it down. Kaelen saw what it was by the lantern-light. A basket. A big round wicker basket with a lid, like a fisherman's creel, packed full.

He hooked it on the rope. He put his hands to the wheel. And the basket went over the edge into the Rift.

Kaelen watched it go. Out over the lip on the end of its rope, swinging, the lantern hung on its handle making a small gold point in the dark. And down. Into the gash at the edge of the world. Down past the place where the rock went black and then went to the not-colour. And still down, the gold point getting smaller and smaller and swinging very slowly, until it was a spark. He could still see it. A spark in the dark of the Rift, far below. Going down.

The old man paid out the rope through his hands at the wheel. Slowly. Steadily. Hand over hand.

And beside him, at his elbow, a smaller figure in a long coat with its head bent and its lips moving. Counting.

Vane.

He knew Vane's way of standing when Vane was counting something. He had seen it on the *Kestrel*'s deck a hundred times. Head down, very still, weight back on his heels, his good hand moving very slightly at his side like a man telling beads. He was counting the rope as it went through the old man's hands.

Not the rope. The knots in it. Kaelen could see them now, by the old man's lantern: small hard lumps, in the rope as it came off the drum, every few feet. Tied there one at a time, by hand, by a man standing on the end of a buttress of rock at the edge of the world at night. One for every time the basket went down. For years.

The spark in the Rift stopped going down.

It stopped a long way down. Three hundred feet. More. It hung there, a gold point in the dark. And then, very slowly, it moved. Sideways. As if somebody down there in the dark had taken hold of the basket and drawn it in.

And then, very faintly, so faintly he would wonder afterward whether he had heard it at all or only seen it in the way the spark moved, from far below in the Rift, a light. A second light. Answering.

Small and yellow. A cooking fire, on a ledge of rock in the dark, three hundred feet below the lip of the world.

And round it, moving, shapes.

---

Vane came back to the ship at the fifth hour. He came up onto the wall-walk between Five and Six where Kaelen was still standing with Two-Eleven, and leaned on the parapet beside him, and said nothing for a long time.

Two-Eleven talked about the dog.

"Two thousand and eleven," said Vane at last.

Kaelen looked at him.

"Knots," said Vane. "In the rope. One a basket. He ties one every time she goes down. He's been going down since the summer of '13. He's missed a few. He had a fever in '16 and couldn't get up for nine days, and somebody else went for him, and he made them tie the knots anyway." His voice was flat and quiet. He was not looking at Kaelen. He was looking at the not-colour, which was a thing you could not do for long. "Two thousand and eleven. I counted them going down and I counted them coming up. It's two thousand and eleven both ways."

He was quiet for a while.

"He remembered me," said Vane. "The Quartermaster. Remembered the pistol. Remembered the card-game, the first one and the one at Cold Harbour. He said I owed him a game. He said—" He stopped. "He said he wished I'd asked, at Cold Harbour. When he said about the bread. He said I was the only one at the table who looked like he'd heard. He said he waited for me to ask, and I didn't, and he went back to the Watch on the morning cart and never saw me again." He laughed. The short one. It went three feet from his mouth into the pitchless wind and was gone. "I was carrying a consignment for the Office. Thirty-six crates. He knew what they were. He said he could hear them from the next table."

Two-Eleven talked about the dog. Kaelen understood, slowly, that the big trooper was doing it on purpose. He was laying a blanket of talk over whatever Vane said, so that anybody listening would hear the dog louder than the captain.

Vane looked at his own hands on the parapet.

"I can't tell you what's down there," he said. "I'm sorry."

"I know."

"It's not—" He stopped. "It's people, Kaelen."

"I know," said Kaelen. "I saw the fire."

And then he heard what Vane had said. All of it. *Knots. The Quartermaster. The bread. The Watch. It's people.* Said at a parapet, in an ordinary voice, a foot from the glass.

He saw Vane hear it too. He saw it arrive in the captain's face a moment after it had arrived in his own. Vane looked down at the sack on Kaelen's arm and his torn white face went the colour of the obsidian under his hands.

"Oh," said Vane, very quietly. "Oh, no."

Two-Eleven talked about the dog. Louder.

Kaelen did not say anything. There was nothing he could say that would not make it worse, and every word of it would go where Vane's had gone.

---

He was not allowed down. Nobody said so. Nobody needed to. On the morning of Stasisday they began going down in the Quartermaster's basket one and two at a time. Sable first, with the Ledger. Then Mags. Then Bettany with her bag. Then Lyra, in a blanket, with her slate round her neck and her face set, lowered into the Rift in a wicker basket on the end of an old man's rope like a loaf of bread. He stood on the wall and watched each one go down into the dark, and each one came back up hours later with a face he could not read, and wrote on the walls of the hold, and did not look at him.

Except Lyra, who did not come back up at all. At dusk Sable came up in the basket alone, and crossed the mooring-yard to him without a word, and put the Cantor's slate into his hands. On it, in square careful capitals: *STAYING. NOT YET.*

It was not the old man's attention he minded. He had had that for seven years. It was theirs. The way they came up out of the Rift and saw him on the wall and looked away. The way a room went quiet when he came into it. The way Lyra, who had sat beside him every night for three weeks, had gone down into the dark rather than sit beside him tonight. He did not blame her. He held her slate on his knees on the foredeck until the dead hour, and did not rub it out.

He was the danger. He had always been the danger. Seven years he had walked into rooms, and the old man had walked into them with him and sat down and listened, and he had not known. For two months now he had known. Since the Valley he had understood exactly what it cost. A boy from the Peaks, and thirty-six people in a crate, saying their names, stopped in the middle.

*When you get there, people will say where they are. They always do.*

They had tried not to say it. Vane had said it anyway, on the wall, because Vane had needed to say it to somebody, and Kaelen was the only somebody there. And the Watch talked. The whole Watch talked, day and night, on every wall, about onions and cable-eyes and dogs. And somewhere in the talk, sooner or later, somebody would say *the basket*, or *the ledge*, or *the ones below*, and he would be standing within thirty feet of them. Wherever he stood, he was the leak.

---

On the night of Stasisday, at the dead hour, the General came for him.

She came along the wall-walk alone, without 94, in her lead coat and her dented breastplate, and stopped in front of him and looked at him. Then she tapped. One flat blow. *Come.*

He looked at Two-Eleven. Two-Eleven was looking at the General with an expression Kaelen had not seen on his face before, and had stopped talking about the dog.

"General," said Two-Eleven. "General, I don't—"

She looked at him. He stopped.

She turned and went along the wall-walk toward Tower Six, and Kaelen followed her.

She took him down the tower stair, round and round inside the obsidian, past troopers on the landings talking in pairs. Past a door with STANDING ORDER NINE painted over it at the height of a man's eyes. Out through a postern at the foot of the tower onto the bare grey ground beyond the wall, in the pitchless wind, under no stars. Toward the edge.

He knew then. He did not know how; something in the way she walked, steady and fast and without looking back, as if she had made up her mind to something and did not want to be talked out of it. He knew what she wanted to know.

She had read the writ. She had listened to Vane. She had stood for six years on a wall at the edge of a thing that kept people, and she had heard four hundred people in a hold, and a man's arm ringing. And she wanted to see what the edge of the world would do to Dawning glass.

He could have stopped. He could have turned round. He did not. He wanted to know too.

They walked out across the grey ground. Past the first of the lead-robed figures standing along the edge, tall and hooded and quite still. As he went by it he saw that under the hood it had no face. Not *no face*. A face covered in a sheet of hammered lead, pressed to the shape of the features like a death-mask, with no holes in it for the eyes or the mouth. It did not turn its head. He did not think it could.

The Lead Brothers. Two-Eleven had told him, in among the dog. The only men on the Watch allowed to be silent. They had taken vows and covered their mouths with lead, so that the edge could not hear them breathe. They stood along the lip day and night in shifts, and listened for the Watch. When one of them began to walk, very slowly, toward the edge, the ones on either side took hold of his arms and brought him back. Nobody knew how they knew. Two-Eleven said they didn't need to know. They just did.

He walked past them. The General walked beside him. The ground went on, grey and flat and bare. And the not-colour came up in front of him like a wall, and like no wall, and he found he was looking at his own feet and could not remember lowering his eyes.

His arm went quiet.

---

Not quieter. *Quiet.*

He felt it go. He was walking toward the edge of the world with the General at his side and his left arm in its sack at his side, and for seven years, for every moment of every day since the Foundry, it had been ringing. Five hundred and twenty-eight, high and faint and clear, so constant that it was not a sound any more but a condition, like the beat of his own heart. Since the Chime there had been a second note under it, deep, six hundred and thirty-nine, the drowned bell.

And they stopped.

Both of them. All at once, in the middle of a step, the way a voice stops when a door is shut on it.

He stopped walking.

He stood on the grey ground thirty yards from the edge of the world and listened, and there was nothing. Nothing in the arm. Nothing in the bone. For the first time in seven years, nothing. A silence so complete that he could hear, for the first time since he was twenty-seven years old, his own blood in his ears. His own breath. The faint dry creak of the General's lead coat as she stopped beside him.

And behind him, at the back of his neck, the place where the old man's attention always stood like somebody behind his chair—

Empty.

Gone. Not eased off, not faint. *Gone.* As if the man behind the chair had got up and left the room and shut the door.

Nobody was listening.

Nobody was listening to him.

---

He did not know how long he stood there. He would think afterward that it could not have been more than a minute, and he would not believe it.

It went into him like warm water.

That was the only way he would ever be able to describe it. Like getting into a hot bath after a day in the cold, like lying down after standing for a week. Something going out of every muscle at once, a loosening, a letting-go, starting at the arm and the shoulder and running down through his whole body. The ringing had stopped. The listening had stopped. The weight of the glass, a hundredweight of it on his left side for seven years, dragging his shoulder down and his spine crooked, was simply not there. He could not feel it. He could not feel the arm at all.

He could not feel the edge of the glass at his throat.

He could not feel the half-finger.

*Oh*, he thought. *Oh. This is what it is.*

It did not hurt. That was the first thing he understood, and he understood it the way you understand something you have always known. It did not hurt at all. It was the opposite of hurting. Every hurt he had ever had was going out of him, softly, gently, the way the pain had gone out of Vane when the thing in Vesper's coat lay on his chest. The Foundry. The eleven thousand and forty. Tobin Ashgrove and the Hedge. Coll Madder on the Salt Stair, with the rail Kaelen had pulled. Pell stepping off the gunwale, smiling. Jonah Pennick going up into the sky on a copper tank, and the strap, and Kaelen asleep against the press while Vane named the place.

All of it going. Not forgotten. Just put down. Like a load set down at the end of a road.

He wanted to sit down.

He wanted to sit down on the grey ground and not get up. He wanted to stay here, in the silence, where nobody was listening. He wanted to stop. Not die — he understood, very clearly, that it was not dying. *Stop.* The way a clock stops. The way a note stops at the end of a bar. He wanted to be the rest between notes, and never have to be the next one.

*Wouldn't you rather*, said something. Not a voice. Not words. A feeling, the way warmth is a feeling. *Wouldn't you rather it had never had to hurt?*

His knees began to go.

---

Iron.

A sound. Close. *Loud*. Knuckles on iron, hard and fast and flat, over and over, right beside him. *Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.* Like somebody hammering on a door.

The General.

She was standing beside him with her face a foot from his, beating her own breastplate with her fist, fast, steady, a rhythm, the same rhythm over and over. He did not know iron-cant. He did not need to. He knew what it was. It was talk. It was the only talk she had. She was standing on the edge of the world in front of a man who had gone quiet, and doing what every soul on Null-Watch was sworn to do. She was talking to him. As loud as she could. With her fist, on the iron over her heart, because she had shouted her voice to rags on this wall six years ago and had nothing else.

*Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.*

He looked at her.

She took hold of the front of his coat with her other hand and pulled.

He came. He did not want to. Every muscle in him wanted to sit down, and she pulled him anyway, backward, away from the edge, one step and another, beating on her breastplate the whole time. *Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.* Her face was set and grey and running with sweat in the cold. Ten yards. Twenty. Past the Lead Brother with the lead over his face, who had turned, Kaelen saw — had turned his blind head toward them, and lifted one hand a little from his side, as if to catch him if he fell.

Thirty yards.

The arm came back.

---

It came back like a scream.

Five hundred and twenty-eight, and six hundred and thirty-nine under it, all at once, at full strength, slammed back into the bone of his arm like a bell struck with a sledge. It went through him from the fingertips to the throat. He felt the edge of the glass at his throat ring like a struck wineglass. The hundredweight came back onto his shoulder and drove him down onto one knee on the grey ground. Every hurt he had set down came back up the road after him and climbed onto his back, every one of them, exactly the weight it had always been.

And at the back of his neck, the attention.

Back. All at once. Not leaning in. *Lunging*. The old man had sat up in his chair in the white room. Kaelen felt it the way you feel a man come through a door behind you at a run.

*Where were you?*

He did not hear it. It was not words. It was the shape of the attention, sharp and close and, for the first time in seven years, *startled*.

*Where did you go?*

Kaelen knelt on the grey ground with the General's fist knotted in his coat and his whole left side ringing like a struck bell. And he understood two things at once, so clearly that he would never afterward be able to separate them.

The first was that the old man did not know.

He had not known that this could happen. In seven years of listening through every piece of Dawning glass on the continent, he had never once lost the line. Not like that. Not to silence. Whatever was at the edge of the world, it was the one thing the old man could not hear through. And he had just found out, through Kaelen, in the same instant Kaelen had.

The second was that he must not say so.

Not a word. Not *it went quiet*, not *he couldn't hear*, not *the edge*. If he said any of it aloud, here, now, the old man would know exactly what had happened and exactly where. And then the one place on the continent where a word could be said that the Organum would not hear would become the place the Organum watched hardest.

He shut his mouth.

He put his right hand over it, the way he had under the thirtieth arch, and held it there.

The General let go of his coat. She stood over him, breathing hard, and he looked up at her. And he saw, in her grey sweating face, that she had seen. She had seen him go quiet, and the arm go quiet, and his face. She had seen him come back, and the startle go through him like a shot. She did not know what it meant. But she had watched the edge for six years and she knew that something had happened.

She held out her hand. He took it, with his right hand, and she pulled him up.

Then she tapped. Slowly, so that even he could follow it, one flat blow at a time, on the iron.

*Not. Here.*

He nodded.

---

The Morning Refusal came some hours later. He watched it from inside Battery Six with the General beside him and the arm screaming in its sack, and would remember it for the rest of his life.

Dawn on the Watch was not a sunrise. The sun did not come up over the edge of the world, because there was no edge for it to come up over, only the not-colour. It came up out of it. Or the light did. Toward the sixth hour, the grey of the sky over the Rift began, very faintly, to lighten. And as it lightened, the not-colour below it began, very faintly, to move.

He could not see it move. He could not look at it long enough to see anything. But he could feel it in the arm. The Light in the glass was pulling, very gently, *toward* it, the way a compass needle swings toward iron. And the attention at the back of his neck was very still, listening as hard as it had ever listened.

The battery was a round room of obsidian at the top of Tower Six, open to the east under its lead hood. It held one gun: the great flared brass mouth, nine feet across, mounted on an iron carriage on a rail that ran round the room, pointing out over the edge of the world. Behind it, a nest of pipes and valves and a long brass cylinder like a ship's boiler, and a crew of twelve in lead coats. All of them talking. A sergeant at the breech. Another at the elevation-wheel. Two men at the bellows, which were enormous, leather, the size of a cart, and pumped by a treadle. A woman at a little brass desk to one side with a row of dials and a pair of ear-cups made of lead. An intercept-tuner, listening.

All of them talking. *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday. Forty-four, forty-five. And then you want your onions thin.*

The woman at the dials put up her hand.

The talk did not stop. It changed. It went from a murmur to a chant, steady, everybody in the room saying the same words together, the days of the week, over and over, *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday, Stasisday*, in a deep steady rolling unison like men hauling on a rope.

"*Elevation four and a quarter*," said the sergeant at the breech, in the middle of *Dawnday*. "*Bellows.*"

The men at the treadle began to pump. The bellows filled and emptied, filled and emptied, with a vast leathery groan. Behind the gun the brass cylinder began to hum. It was a low steady sound, building. It was not a note. It was every note at once. It was the sound of rain on a roof and wind in a chimney and the sea on shingle, and a crowd in a square, all together, getting louder.

"*On my word*," said the sergeant. "*Rootday, Hearthday—*"

Kaelen felt the arm pull toward the edge. Hard now. The Light in the glass leaning out of the battery's mouth toward the not-colour as the sun came up behind it, like a dog straining on a lead.

"*—Massday—*"

And all along the wall, in every tower, the same chant. He could hear it through the obsidian. Forty batteries. Forty crews. Four miles of wall. *Stasisday. Dawnday.*

"*Fire.*"

---

It was not a bang. It was a *roar*. It came out of the great brass mouth in a solid wall of white noise. It was the loudest thing Kaelen had ever heard, louder than the Foundry, louder than the Chime. Every sound in the world at once, poured out over the edge in a flood. It went out from Battery Six, and from Battery Five beside it, and Seven, and every battery along four miles of wall, all at the same instant. A single unbroken roar the length of the edge of the world.

It hit the not-colour.

And the not-colour *flinched*.

He saw it. He could not look at it, and he saw it anyway. He felt it in the arm like a blow. The whole band of the thing at the edge of the world went back. A yard. Less. A hair. The way a hand goes back from a stove. And the pull on the glass went slack, all at once, like a cut rope. It was not a victory. Nobody on the Watch had ever called it one. It was a door held shut for one more day.

And under it — far down, far below the edge, in the dark of the Rift under the shelf where the cooking fire had been — something moved.

He did not see it. He felt it. Through the glass, through the bone, through the stone of the tower, through his boots. Something large. Something so large that the word did not apply, the way the word *large* does not apply to a mountain. Something down in the dark at the bottom of the gash at the end of the world turned over, the way a sleeper turns over when a door bangs in the house. Turned. And settled.

And, for one instant, before it settled, *reached*.

Not toward the batteries. Not toward the wall.

Toward him.

Toward the arm. Toward the Light in the glass. He felt it the way you feel someone look at you across a crowded room. Something at the bottom of the world had turned over in its sleep at the noise and, for one instant, noticed that there was Dawning glass on the wall. It had leaned toward it. The way the Light in his arm had leaned toward the edge.

The roar went on. Thirty seconds. A minute. The crews at the bellows pumped and chanted. *Memoryday, Nullday, Rootday.* The woman at the dials had her eyes shut and her hands pressed flat over the lead cups at her ears.

Then it stopped.

---

The silence after was enormous, and full of talk.

All along the wall, all at once, the crews went back to it, the murmur rising again off four miles of obsidian like steam off a horse. *And that's the Refusal done, and that's another one, and what's for breakfast, and I don't know, onions probably.* The sergeant at the breech wiped his face. The men at the bellows sat down on the floor. The woman at the dials took off the lead ear-cups and looked at them and put them on again.

The General was looking at him.

He had not noticed her looking. He noticed now. She was standing beside him at the battery's open mouth. She was not looking at the edge. She was looking at his arm, at the sack, at the place under the canvas where the glass had blazed when the thing at the bottom of the world reached for it. He looked down and saw it. The light coming through the weave. Not cyan. White. White-gold. Bright enough to throw his shadow on the battery floor.

It was fading. Slowly.

The General looked at it for a long time. Then she looked out over the edge of the world, down, into the dark of the Rift where the cooking fire had been. Then back at him. She did not tap. She did not need to. He saw it in her face, and he knew it was in his.

*It noticed you.*

*Six years it has slept down there under the Refusal, and turned over every dawn when the guns fired, and settled. And this morning, for the first time, it reached for something.*

*Because you are here.*

He looked down past the battery's lip at the Rift. Somewhere down there, three hundred feet under the edge of the world, on a ledge of rock in the dark, there was a cooking fire. Round it, there were people that somebody had thrown over the lip. An old man had lowered bread to them on a rope every night for six years and tied a knot for every basket. Two thousand and eleven knots. And Lyra and Sable and Mags had been going down to them for a day and coming back with faces he could not read.

And under them, further down, in the dark, something very large had just turned over in its sleep and reached up past them toward the light in his arm.

The attention at the back of his neck leaned close. Interested. Patient. Waiting for somebody to say where they were.

Kaelen put his right hand over his mouth, and stood in the battery at the top of Tower Six, and said nothing at all.

On the wall below him, the Watch talked.

*The Shelf. Stasisday the twentieth of Ashfall, to Dawnday the twenty-first.*

---

The Quartermaster talked to her all the way down.

She had not expected it. She had expected silence: the rope, the creak of the wheel, the wind with no pitch in it, the dark coming up round the basket. She had braced herself for it the way you brace for cold water. But the basket went over the lip of the buttress into the Rift and began to go down, and from above, from the wheel, the old man's voice came down after her. Steady. Unhurried. Pitched to carry down a rope.

"—and that's the first fifty feet, Cantor, and you'll feel her swing a bit now she's off the rock, don't mind that, she always swings, she's swung two thousand and fourteen times and never tipped anybody out yet. Two thousand and fifteen, now. That's you. You're a knot."

She held on to the rim of the basket with both hands and looked up. He was a black shape against the grey sky at the top of the rope, bent over the wheel. Paying out, hand over hand.

"Eighty feet. That's the black rock starting now. Don't touch it. It's glass. It'll have your hand off. You'll see the Shelf at two hundred, if you look down, which I wouldn't, but you will, they all do."

She looked down.

The Rift went down under her. It was a gash in the world a quarter of a mile across, with walls of black volcanic glass streaked grey and white. They went down, and down, and became dark, and became something else. Below a certain depth she could not see them. She could not see anything. She found she was looking at her own hands on the rim of the basket and could not remember having looked away.

"Told you," said the Quartermaster from above. "A hundred and twenty. Look at the wall, Cantor. Look at the wall and listen to me. I'm going to tell you about my sister's cat."

He told her about his sister's cat. It had lived in a bakery in Greystairs and grown so fat on the scraps that it could not get through the cat-door. He told it in a flat cheerful voice like a man calling soundings, and the basket went down past the black glass, and she listened to every word.

She understood, at about a hundred and eighty feet, why he was doing it. It was not for her. It was for him. A man standing at the edge of the Rift at night letting out rope into the dark could not be quiet. If he was quiet, he would stop.

She had no voice to answer him with, and her slate hung useless on its cord round her neck; she could not have written on it in a swinging basket with both hands on the rim. So she did the only thing she could. At every story's end she took her hand off the rim and rapped twice on the wicker with her knuckles, hard, so that he would feel it come up the rope.

*Here.*

After the third time he stopped in the middle of a sentence. When he went on, his voice had changed. Not much.

"Good girl," said the Quartermaster. "Two hundred. Look down now. There it is."

---

There it was.

A ledge of black rock running along the Rift wall like a shelf in a cupboard. Three hundred feet below the lip. A quarter of a mile long, perhaps forty yards deep at its widest, narrowing at either end to nothing. It was littered with shapes she could not at first make out, and in the middle of it, in the angle where the ledge met the wall, there was a fire.

And people.

They were coming out from the wall as the basket came down. She saw them by the fire's light and the basket's lantern. Dozens of them. Out of holes and lean-tos and shacks along the back of the ledge, out of the black glass itself, where there were openings cut into it, dark doorways. Moving slowly toward the place under the rope where the basket would land. Men and women in rags and blankets and the remains of grey Office smocks, with faces thin and pale in the firelight, all looking up.

All talking.

She heard it come up the Rift to meet her, before she could make out a word. A murmur. Like the wall above, but different. On the wall the talk had been heavy, dogged, onions and cable-eyes, men hauling on a rope. This was lighter. Quicker. It went up and down. There was laughter in it. Not much. Some.

She did not understand until the basket touched rock and a hundred people stood round it in a ring, looking at her. Then she heard what they were saying.

"—Corran, who can't read, and that's Maud beside him, she lost the word for mother, and there's Ansel, he lost his wife's laugh but he's got the rest of her—"

"—Bridie, my left hand, it works, look, I just don't know it's mine, it feels like somebody else's hand on my wrist, I'm used to it now—"

"—and I'm Hob, and I lost nine days in the spring of '17, I don't know what I did, I've got blood under my nails in the memory either side of them—"

Names. And what had been taken. Over and over, round the ring, the way the Watch said the days of the week. Every one of them saying who they were and what was gone, quietly, steadily, to her and to each other and to nobody. And they would go on saying it, she would learn, day and night, for as long as they were on the Shelf. Because it was the one thing they had that the press had not taken, and a person on the Shelf who stopped saying it was a person who had started to go.

A small figure came pushing through the ring to the basket. A thin child in a man's coat with the sleeves rolled back four times, with a narrow freckled face and enormous grey eyes and cropped red hair, and it put its hands on the rim of the basket and looked up at her.

"I'm Fen," it said. "They took the growing. You're the Cantor. Sable told us. You're the one who sang under the Church on the mountain. And now you can't talk." The grey eyes went to the bandage at her throat. "That's all right. Nobody minds. I'll talk for you. I'm very good at it."

She looked at the child. Twelve. Eleven. Not more.

"I'm eighteen," said Fen. "Everyone does that face. You'll stop doing it in a day. Come on. You're needed."

---

She was needed because of Bridie Cole, who was having a baby.

Not now. Not yet. Soon. Lyra knew it the moment she saw her. She knew it by the breathing. It was the only craft she had left: she had spent her life in birthing rooms and fever-rooms, and she could hear a labour coming in a woman's breath the way a sailor hears weather in the rigging. The breath had the catch in it at the top. The long slow pull. The way the woman sat, with her hands pressed into the small of her back, on a blanket in a hollow at the far end of the Shelf, out by the edge, where the ledge went narrow.

Bridie Cole was twenty-six and had lost the knowing of her left hand. She looked up at Lyra out of a broad tired face and said, "Oh, thank the Light. Not the Church's one. The real one. You'll forgive me."

Lyra knelt beside her on the black rock. She put her hands on the woman's belly. She felt the child, head down, low. Not tonight. Tomorrow, perhaps. Perhaps the night after.

She wanted, so badly that her eyes stung, to hum.

That was the first thing you did. You put your hands on the belly and hummed the tending note into it, a hundred and seventy-four, low and steady, the Root. The child heard it through the water and the mother heard it through her bones, and both of them steadied, and the room steadied with them. She had done it a thousand times. She had hummed it into a clay jar to bring Pim Carder's little sister into the world through a door she could not open. It was the oldest thing she knew.

She opened her mouth, out of habit, and nothing came.

Nothing. Not a whisper, not a breath of sound. Only the dry raw scrape of air in a throat that had been torn through on Lumina. And the pain of it, sudden and sharp, all the way down to the breastbone. She shut her mouth and stayed quite still with her hands on Bridie Cole's belly until the pain went.

Fen was watching her.

"You were going to sing," said Fen.

Lyra nodded.

"To the baby."

She nodded again.

Fen looked at her for a moment with the grey eyes. Then the girl knelt down on the other side of Bridie Cole and put her own small hands on the belly beside Lyra's, a little awkwardly. In a child's high clear voice, with no tune in it at all, she began to talk.

"Hello," said Fen to the belly. "Hello. This is Fen. You don't know me. I'm eighteen and I look like I'm twelve, and they took the growing out of me at Ebb Cross in the summer of '13, when they were still learning how. That's why I'm small. You'll be bigger than me in about nine years. Your mother's Bridie and she's nice. She can't feel her left hand but she can feel you, and you're doing very well, and the Cantor's here now, the real one, so you can come out whenever you like. Not tonight, though. Tomorrow's better. Tonight's cold."

Under Lyra's hands, the child kicked.

Bridie Cole laughed, and then put her face in her right hand and cried. Fen went on talking to her belly, steadily, about nothing. And Lyra knelt on the black rock at the edge of the world with her hands on a stranger's child and understood that she had just been given something, and did not know how to give it back.

---

She could not leave Bridie. So she stayed.

Sable and Mags and Bettany had come down before her that morning. They walked the Shelf with Orrin Hale all day, and Sable wrote, and Bettany examined people, and Mags watched. When the basket went up at dusk they went up in it, one at a time. Sable looked at Lyra from the basket, a long look, and Lyra shook her head, and Sable nodded and did not argue. Lyra sent the slate up with her. It was the only way she had of telling Kaelen anything, and she wanted him to have it more than she wanted it herself.

*STAYING. NOT YET.*

She did not let herself think about him reading it. She did not let herself think about him at all, up there on the wall in the pitchless wind with the old man in his arm, waiting for somebody to say where they were.

She watched the basket go up the black glass into the dark with its lantern swinging. The last gold point of it went over the lip, and was gone. Fen sat down beside her on the rock with her knees drawn up inside the enormous coat.

"Everybody does that too," said Fen. "Watches it go up. The first night." She was quiet for a moment. Lyra was learning that a moment was as long as Fen was ever quiet. "I did. The very first night. There wasn't a basket then. There wasn't anything. I'd come over the lip that morning with forty others in a cart. They tipped the cart. And I fell, and hit, and I didn't die, and I don't know why. Everybody else did. I sat on the edge of the Shelf all day with them lying round me. They didn't rot, you know. Nothing does, here. They just lay there looking surprised."

She said it the way the Quartermaster had told her about the cat.

"And then it got dark," said Fen. "And I looked up, and there was a man on the wall looking down. With a lantern. He'd seen me. He looked at me for a long time. And then he went away, and I thought, *well, that's that*, and I cried, because I was twelve, and I didn't know yet that I was going to be twelve for ever." She pulled the coat's collar up round her ears. "And the next night there was a rope. And a basket on it. With bread and a blanket and a lantern and a flask of water. And this coat, which was his. And a note. In very bad writing."

She was quiet again. Two moments.

"*Keep talking*," said Fen. "That's what it said. That's all it said. So I did. I've been talking for six years. You'd think I'd have run out." She looked sideways at Lyra with the grey eyes. "You don't have to. You can't. I'll do it for both of us. All right?"

Lyra took the girl's small cold hand in hers and held it.

---

Orrin Hale came and found her at the second hour of the night, by the fire in the angle of the wall, where the Shelf was widest.

She knew who he was before Fen said his name. She had seen him from the basket, standing a little apart from the ring, a big man of fifty in the rags of a clerk's black coat. Grey-bearded, broad, slow-moving. He had a strange stillness about him that she had taken at first for calm and then for exhaustion, and then, watching him walk along the very edge of the Shelf in the dusk with the drop at his boot-toes, for something else she had no name for.

He sat down across the fire from her.

"Cantor," he said. "I'm Orrin. They took my fear. I was a tally-clerk at the Hall of Carriage for nineteen years. I asked why there were names on the velvet trays on the third floor." He said it in a low pleasant voice, like a man reading out a receipt. "I'm told you can't speak. That's all right. I'm going to tell you some things, and you can nod."

She nodded.

"The scribe was down today. Sable. She walked the Shelf with me. She wrote down every name and every missing thing. Four hundred and four of us, last night. I've the count. I keep the count." He looked at the fire. "She was very quiet. The scribe. She asked me how we came here, and I told her, and she wrote it down. Then she asked to see the docket. They come with a docket, you see, the ones who live. Pinned to the coat. *Schedule F. Residual. Disposal at the lip.* I've kept them all. Four hundred and four dockets in a tin box in my hole in the wall." He looked at her across the fire. "She looked at the first one for a long time. And then she said, *that is my hand*. And she put it back in the tin, and finished walking the Shelf, and wrote down every name, and went up in the basket. And she didn't say anything else."

Lyra looked at him.

"I'm not telling you to be cruel," said Orrin Hale. "I can't be cruel. You need a bit of fear to be cruel, I've found. You need to be afraid of something to want to hurt it. I'm telling you because she won't, and somebody should. She drafted it. The routing. The docket. *Disposal at the lip.* Oh, there were dockets before hers, rougher ones; Fen came over with a scrap of card that just said *F* on it. But the one that's come pinned to every coat for three years is hers. She sat at a desk in the Organum in the spring of '16 and wrote it out fair in her best clerk's hand, and it came here." He was quiet for a moment. "And now she's come here too. And she's walked the Shelf and written down every name. I don't know what that means. Nobody here does. I thought you might."

She did not know either. She shook her head.

He nodded, as if that were an answer.

"There are other things," he said. "You'll want to know them before the morning. There are twenty-two people at the far end of the Shelf who came over three weeks ago. They're not like us. They're Hollows. They were pressed at the Malting, on the Cloister road, all the way, the whole name. They kept them in a ward there to work, the way they do. Then five weeks ago something happened at the Malting." He looked at her. "Something that made the Office close the ward. All at once. Two hundred residuals, put in carts and sent east under escort and tipped over the lip at the Watch on a single morning. Twenty-two lived."

She went cold.

*Five weeks. The Malting.*

Sable, in the harvest-house, putting Bettis Renn's name back in her. The *Kestrel* lifting out of the Cloister road with a press on her foredeck.

Two hundred.

"Yes," said Orrin Hale, watching her face. "I thought it might have been you. Sable thought so too. She didn't say. She went down to the far end and looked at them for a long time, and wrote down what they say, because they haven't got names any more, only what they say." He looked into the fire. "They're no trouble. We feed them. They loop. One of them sets out stones in a row every morning and every night, nine stones, and moves them one at a time from one end to the other. One of them sings. Just the one line, over and over. We don't know what it is."

He got up. He was very big, standing, against the firelight.

"That's the things," he said. "The baby's tomorrow, Fen says. Fen's usually right. I'll be at the edge, if you want me. I generally am."

He went off along the Shelf toward the dark at the narrow end, where the ledge ran out to nothing over the drop. She watched him go. He walked along the very lip of it, with his boots half over the edge, steady and slow, looking down. The way a man walks along a garden path looking at flowers.

"He does that," said Fen, beside her. "Every night. He walks the edge. He says somebody has to look, and he's the only one who can do it without wanting to jump." She was quiet for a moment. "I hate it. Everybody hates it. He knows."

---

Tessa Wray came at the fourth hour, when the labour began.

It began the way they always began, as Fen had said it would: early, in the cold, at the worst time. Lyra heard it in Bridie Cole's breath across the Shelf before Bridie knew it herself. She was on her feet and moving before Fen woke. By the time she reached the hollow by the edge, Bridie was up on her hands and knees on the blanket with her face white and her mouth open, breathing the way you breathe when your body has stopped asking your permission for anything.

The hollow was at the narrow end, by the drop, in the cold, exposed. Lyra looked at it, and at the edge three yards away, and at the black where the Shelf ended. She did not like it. She could not have said why; only that a birthing room should have a back wall and a door and a fire, and this had none.

She took Bridie's arm and pointed back along the Shelf. To the fire. To the angle of the wall, where it was widest, and warmest, and furthest from the edge.

Bridie looked at her. "It's a long way."

Lyra pointed again.

So they went. It took half an hour, a hundred and fifty yards, with Bridie stopping every few steps to lean on Lyra and Fen and breathe. People came out of their holes in the wall and saw what it was, and talked to her as she passed, steadily, kindly, about nothing. They laid blankets by the fire in the angle of the wall. Somebody put water on to heat in a battered pot. Somebody else brought a lamp. By the fifth hour Bridie Cole was on her back on four blankets in the warmest place on the Shelf, with the fire beside her and the black glass wall of the Rift at her head. Lyra was kneeling between her knees with her sleeves rolled up and her hands washed in hot water.

And a woman Lyra had not seen before came and sat down at Bridie's head, took Bridie's face between her hands, and began to talk.

---

"I'm Tessa," the woman said. To Bridie. To Lyra. To the fire. "They took my name, and I took it back. I'm the only one."

She was thirty, perhaps, thin, with dark hair cut short and a narrow quiet face, and hands that never stopped moving. Lyra saw that at once. Even holding Bridie's face, the fingers moved, very slightly, all the time, as if they were braiding something too fine to see.

"You've to keep talking," said Tessa to Bridie. "You know that. Down here. Even now. *Especially* now. If you go quiet, the Cantor can't hear your breath and she can't help you, and something else can hear you instead." She stroked the hair back off Bridie's forehead. "So I'll talk, and you listen, and in between you push when the Cantor says. She'll say it with her hands. All right?"

Bridie nodded, white-faced.

"I'm going to tell you something I've never told anyone," said Tessa Wray. "Not Orrin. Not Fen. I'm going to tell you, because you've got something else to think about. And the Cantor can't ask me questions. That's the thing that's always stopped me. The questions." She looked down at Lyra between Bridie's knees, in the firelight. Her face was quite calm. "You don't mind?"

Lyra shook her head.

And Tessa Wray told her.

---

"They pressed me at Ebb Cross," said Tessa, "in the spring of '16. Same house as Fen. Three years after her. They'd learned a lot by then. They'd got it down to ninety seconds.

"You go in through the front. There's a bench, and a water butt with a tin cup on a chain, and a man takes your coat at the door so you won't stop on the threshold. That's in the pamphlet. I read the pamphlet. They give it you in the queue. It says *Registration* on the front, in nice letters.

"There's a room with a chair. A plain chair. Wood. And a clerk at a desk, with a book. Very polite. A young man, with ink on his fingers. He asks you three questions. Your name. Your mother's name. Where you were born. You answer them. You think it's for the register. It is, in a way.

"Then there's a man behind the chair with a fork."

Bridie's breath caught. Lyra put her hand flat on the belly and pressed, gently: *now*. Bridie pushed, with a long low groan. Tessa held her face and did not stop.

"A brass fork. Like a tuning fork, but long, as long as your forearm. He strikes it on his knee, and it sings. Seven hundred and forty-one. The Memory note. You've never heard it, have you? It's lovely. It's like somebody you love calling you from the next room. And he puts it to the back of your neck. Here." Her free hand touched the nape of her own neck, lightly. "Under the skull. And it goes in.

"Not the fork. The note. It goes in at the back of your neck and down your spine, and up, into your head. And it *finds* you.

"That's the only way I can say it. It finds you. It goes looking through you the way you go through a house for something you've put down. It goes through every room. It opens every drawer. You can feel it. Your mother's face. The smell of your father's pipe. The first time you were kissed, by Wat Hollis behind the tithe-barn, and you were fourteen, and it was raining. Your name. It finds your name. It goes to your name like a dog goes to a bone, and it takes hold of it, and it *pulls*.

"And it comes out. You feel it come out. Out of your head, down your spine, out at the back of your neck, into the fork, along the fork, into the glass in the man's other hand. A little lilac ball. You feel yourself go into it. Your name. Wat Hollis. The rain. Your mother. You feel it go out of you like blood out of a cut, and it doesn't hurt. It doesn't hurt at all.

"That's the thing nobody tells you. They don't tell you because nobody knows, because nobody who's had it done can tell you. It's ninety seconds and three questions and eight seconds of the fork. And it doesn't hurt, and you're *awake*. You're awake for all of it. You feel every room in you being emptied. And you can't move, because the fork's got you, and you can't speak. You just sit in the chair and feel yourself go into the glass. And the young man at the desk with the ink on his fingers writes in his book, and doesn't look up."

Bridie screamed.

It was a short sharp scream, and Lyra had her hands where they needed to be. *Push*. And Tessa held Bridie's face and talked straight through it, in the same low calm voice, as if she were reading a recipe.

"And then it stopped. Half-way. It stopped half-way through me.

"The glass in his hand went *crack*. Not broke. Cracked. A sound like ice on a pond. And the pulling stopped. The note stopped. And I was sitting in the chair with half of me in a cracked lilac ball in a man's hand, and half of me still in my head. And the young man at the desk looked up for the first time. And he said, *oh, bother*. And wrote something in the margin.

"That was the seed. They told me afterward. Orrin told me. The seed in the glass, that they press you round. A bottle off the Cape. A voice off the trench. Mine was poor. It didn't take. So the press failed, and they had half of me in a cracked core, and half of me in the chair, and the half in the chair was no good to them. Schedule F. *Incomplete draw.*

"They put me in a cart. And then, because they'd been careless that day, they put the cracked core in the envelope with my docket. And pinned it to my coat. Disposal, you see. They didn't want it. It was flawed. Dispose of residual and flawed core together. Saves a form.

"So I came over the lip with half of myself in a glass ball pinned to my coat in an envelope. And I lived."

---

The child's head came at the sixth hour, in the grey before dawn.

Lyra had it in her hands. She had the shoulders. She was saying, with her face, with her eyes, with her hands on Bridie's knee, *one more, one more, you're nearly there*. She had always said it aloud, in every birthing room of her life, and she could not, and Bridie was reading it in her face instead. And Tessa Wray held Bridie's face and talked.

"I knew what it was. As soon as I came to. I was lying on the Shelf and the envelope was on my chest and I could feel it in there. Like a coal. Like something alive. *Me*. The half of me they'd taken. Wat Hollis and the rain and my mother's face and my *name*. I couldn't remember my name. I could feel it in the envelope, the way you feel somebody standing on the other side of a door.

"I didn't know what to do. Nobody knew. Nobody had ever had one back. I held it for a month. Just held it. In my hands, against my chest, at night. Talking to it. Keep talking. I told it everything I had left. I told it about the half of my mother I still had, and the half of the barn, and the bits of the rain. Everything that was still in me that matched what was in it.

"And one night I put it to my throat. Here." She touched the hollow of her own throat. "Where the voice comes from. And I tried to say my name.

"I didn't have it. I couldn't say it. But I knew the shape of the hole where it had been. I'd been feeling the shape of that hole for a month. So I said the *hole*. I said the shape of it. Into the glass. The way you'd call a dog by whistling, if you'd forgotten its name.

"And it came.

"It came home. Out of the glass, into my throat, up into my head. Back into every room. Every drawer it had come out of. It knew the way. Because I was still there, you see. Half of me was still there to call it. *A piece knows its way home, if there's someone home to call it.* That's what I worked out, afterward. I've told Orrin that much. That's all I've told anyone.

"*Push.*"

---

Bridie pushed.

The child came out into Lyra's hands in a rush, all at once, the way they do, slippery and grey and furious. A girl. Lyra had her before she knew it, turned her, cleared her mouth with a finger, and held her up in the firelight. And the girl drew breath and screamed at the edge of the world as if it had personally offended her.

The Shelf went quiet.

Not silent. Never silent; somebody somewhere along the ledge was still saying their name and what was gone, steadily, as they always did. But round the fire, for a moment, the talk stopped. A hundred people who had not heard a newborn cry in six years stood in the grey before dawn and listened to one.

Then Fen laughed out loud and began to talk again, faster than ever. And everyone else did too.

Lyra laid the child on Bridie's breast. Bridie Cole put her good right hand over her daughter's back, and her left hand too, the one that did not know it was hers. She looked at both hands on the child, and began to cry again.

Tessa Wray let go of Bridie's face. Lyra looked up at her across the mother and child. Tessa was looking at the baby. Her hands had stopped moving.

"It was my name," said Tessa quietly. To Lyra. Only to Lyra, under the noise. "That came back. And Wat Hollis, and the rain, and my mother's face. All of it. I had all of it back."

She looked up.

"And her."

Lyra did not understand.

"The seed," said Tessa. "The voice off the trench. The bottle they set my core around. It was poor, so the press failed, but it was *there*. In the glass, with me, at the centre. When I called myself home, she came too. I didn't know. I didn't know she was there till she was in my head."

Her face did not change. Her voice did not change. But Lyra saw her hands. They had started again. Faster.

"A woman," said Tessa Wray. "A fisherman's wife from the Cape. Her name was Annis. She'd gone over the side of a boat in the Screaming Year after her husband, because she heard him calling from the water. She'd been down there in the dark for three years when somebody held a bottle over the trench, and a bit of her went up into it. And a bit of that bit went into the glass with me. And then into my head." She looked at the fire. "I had her for nine days."

The baby was quiet now, rooting at Bridie's breast. Around the fire the talk went on.

"She didn't scream," said Tessa. "That's what you need to understand. She didn't scream once. She was so *calm*. She was down there, still, the rest of her, being eaten, slowly, in the cold, in the dark. And the bit of her in my head could feel it, and so could I. All of it. Nine days of it. Being held. Being digested. By something that was so — so *kind* to her." Her voice did not shake. "It didn't hurt. Not her. Not at all. That's why I screamed. Nine days. Because it didn't hurt, and she kept asking me, very politely, if it was the tide."

She looked at Lyra.

"On the tenth day she went quiet. I don't know where she went. I don't know if she went back down. Or if she's still in here, somewhere, in a room I don't go into." She touched the side of her own head, lightly, with two fingers. "Some nights I think I can hear her asking about the tide."

Lyra looked at her for a long time.

Then she reached across Bridie Cole and her daughter and took Tessa Wray's moving hands in hers, and held them still.

---

The Morning Refusal came at the first grey of dawn, and the Shelf heard it from underneath.

Fen had told her what it was like, in among everything else. The guns on the wall fired out, over the edge of the world, at the not-colour; and the Shelf was under the wall, in the lee. The roar went out over their heads and away, the way the wind goes over a man lying in a ditch. *It's loud*, Fen had said. *You'll feel it in your teeth. Then it's over and we have breakfast. Six years. It's the best part of the day.*

So when the people round the fire began, very slightly, to look up at the grey sky over the lip of the Rift three hundred feet above, Lyra looked up too.

She heard the chant first. Coming down the black glass from the wall. Faint, deep, rolling, from four miles of towers, from forty crews at the bellows. *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday.* And on the Shelf, round the fire, round the new mother and the child, the Rendered took it up. They said the days of the week with the Watch, softly, all together, a hundred voices at the edge of the world. *Stasisday. Dawnday.*

She could not say it with them. She knelt by Bridie Cole with the child in her arms, the child she had taken from Bridie to wrap, and moved her lips.

*Driftday. Memoryday.*

The roar.

It went over the Shelf like a wave over a breakwater. She felt it in her teeth and her breastbone and the raw place in her throat. A wall of white noise poured out from the lip of the Rift overhead, out and away over the edge of the world. The black glass of the Rift wall hummed against her back.

And under her, the Shelf moved.

---

Not shook. *Moved*.

She felt it through her knees. Through the rock. A long slow heave, from very deep, far down under the Shelf in the dark of the Rift. As if something down there had rolled over in its sleep.

She heard Fen stop talking.

That was what she would remember. Fen stopped talking. In six years of keeping talking, Lyra thought later, Fen had never once stopped. And she stopped now, in the middle of a word, and Lyra looked round and saw her standing by the fire with her grey eyes on the edge of the Shelf.

And then Lyra felt it reach.

She did not see it. She felt it, the way she felt breath. Something moved up from below the Shelf, past the Shelf, *through* the Shelf, from the dark at the bottom of the world. Not toward the Rendered. Not toward the fire. Past them. Up. Toward the lip three hundred feet overhead, toward the wall, toward the towers, as if something on the wall had called it.

She did not know how she knew where. She knew. She felt it go past her up the black glass the way you feel someone brush past you in the dark. And she felt where it was going. Not in her head. Lower. In the place under the breastbone where, for seven years, there had been a thread running from her to a man with a glass arm, and she had never once been able to cut it.

*Kaelen.*

And as it went past, the far end of the Shelf stopped being there.

---

There was no sound. That was the thing.

She had been in the Bleach. She had seen the light take a gull and a city. She had been at the Chime when the bells sang a man off a gunwale smiling. She thought she knew what the world sounded like when it broke. It sounded like nothing. It sounded like the absence of a sound she had not known she was hearing until it stopped.

The narrow end of the Shelf, where the ledge ran out to nothing over the drop, was simply not there.

She saw it go. A hundred and fifty yards of black rock and the people on it, and their holes in the wall and their lean-tos and their blankets. The hollow where Bridie Cole had sat last night with her hands in the small of her back, three yards from the edge. The far end of the Shelf where the twenty-two had sat, with the man who moved nine stones and the woman who sang one line.

It did not crack. It did not fall. It did not crumble away into the dark with a roar of stone. It went *quiet*. That was the only word. The rock went quiet the way a voice goes quiet. Then it was not rock. Then there was nothing there, and the edge of the Shelf was a hundred and fifty yards nearer than it had been, clean and smooth as a cut loaf.

And the people on it went with it.

She saw them. In the grey light, in the roar of the Refusal going over, along the far end of the Shelf, people sitting up in their blankets. People coming out of their holes. People standing. And then not standing. Going down. Not falling. *Going*. The way you go down into sleep. Without a sound.

Nobody screamed.

That was what she would remember for the rest of her life, longer than anything else. Not one of them screamed. A man by the far wall stood up as the ground went quiet under him and looked down. He looked at it with an expression she would see in her sleep, of enormous, startled *relief*. And he went down into the dark with his arms a little out from his sides like a man stepping into a warm bath. A woman with a blanket round her shoulders lay back on the rock as it went, the way you lie back on a bed. Two people holding hands went together, and did not let go.

At the very end, by the wall, the man with the nine stones sat over his row and moved one stone from one end to the other. And went.

The woman who sang the one line went on singing it, all the way down, until Lyra could not hear it any more, and could not tell when she had stopped.

---

Fen was running.

Lyra saw her. The girl had gone from the fire like a hare from a hedge, toward the new edge of the Shelf, the clean cut where the rock stopped. A Hollow was there, sitting. One of the twenty-two who had been at the very edge of the far end and had not gone, an old woman in a grey smock sitting on the brink with her feet over the drop, twisting her hands in her lap. And the ground under her was going quiet. Lyra could see it. A soft dark line crossing the rock toward her, like a shadow when a cloud goes over the sun.

Fen ran for her. Fen was twelve years old and eighteen, and she ran straight at the quiet as if it were a cart she could pull a child out from under.

Lyra opened her mouth to scream at her to stop.

Nothing.

Nothing came. The dry raw scrape of air in a torn throat, and the pain, all the way down to the breastbone, white. No sound at all. She was standing at the edge of the world with a newborn in her arms, watching a girl run at her death. And she could not call her name. She could not call anything. She had sung down a god on Mount Lumina, and she could not make one sound to make a child turn round.

She looked round wildly. The fire. The pot. The battered iron pot of water on its stones, half-full, still steaming.

She put the baby into Tessa Wray's arms. She did not look to see if Tessa took her. She snatched the iron pot off the fire by its handle, burning her palm without feeling it. Water slopped hissing on the stones. She seized the iron ladle out of it and began to beat the pot.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

Iron on iron. As hard as she could. Over and over. The way the General had beaten her fist on the iron over her heart at the foot of the mooring-mast, because she had no voice either. The noise went out across the Shelf, harsh and flat and ugly and *loud*. It was the only sound she could make, and she made it with everything she had.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

Fen stopped. Turned her head. Six yards from the old woman and the dark line crossing the rock between them. She looked back at Lyra beating the pot by the fire, and Lyra saw her see. See the line. See the rock going quiet at her feet. See the old woman on the brink, who had looked up at last from her hands, and was smiling.

And Orrin Hale went past her.

---

He came out of nowhere. Out of the dark along the back wall, at a steady walk. Not running. He did not run. He walked past Fen, past the dark line on the rock, *across* it, as if it were a crack in a pavement. Lyra saw the quiet take his boots. Saw them go soft at the edges like wet paper. And he walked on across it, three steps, four, to the old woman at the brink. He bent and picked her up in his arms like a child, and turned round, and walked back.

He did not hurry. Lyra saw his face as he came back across the line. It was quite calm. Interested. Faintly, politely curious, the way a man looks at weather. There was nothing in it at all of what there should have been. Nothing of the thing that would have made any other man on earth run, or freeze, or not go at all.

He walked back across the quiet with the old Hollow woman in his arms. On the far side of it, his left boot came off his foot. Not pulled off. *Gone*. The sole and the upper and the laces gone soft and grey and then gone, and his bare foot came down on the black rock, white and whole. He did not look at it.

He reached Fen. He set the old woman down on her feet beside her. The woman stood there, swaying, and went on twisting her hands. Then he took Fen by the back of the collar of the enormous coat, quite gently, the way you take a cat. He walked her back toward the fire, with the old woman shuffling after.

Behind them the dark line crossed the place where the woman had been sitting, and the place where Fen had been standing, and stopped. The edge of the Shelf moved six yards nearer.

The roar of the Refusal ended overhead.

The silence after it was enormous.

Then, from somewhere along the Shelf, a voice. Shaking. A woman's.

"Maud. I'm Maud. I lost the word for mother. I'm Maud. I lost the word—"

And another. "Corran. I can't read. Corran. I can't—"

And another. And another. And the Shelf began to talk again, all along what was left of it, faster and higher and less steady than before. Names and what was gone. Over and over, in the grey of the morning, at the edge of the world, with the new edge a hundred and fifty yards nearer than it had been at dusk.

Lyra stood by the fire with the iron pot in one hand and the ladle in the other. She found she could not open her right hand to let go of the handle. The skin of her palm had stuck to it.

---

Orrin counted. It was what he did.

He walked the Shelf from the fire to the new edge with the tin box of dockets under his arm and his one bare foot white on the black rock. He stopped at every hole and lean-to and blanket. He asked every person their name and what was gone, and opened the box and found the docket, and moved it from one side of the tin to the other. It took him an hour. He did not hurry.

When he came back to the fire he sat down across it from Lyra. Bettany had not come down yet, so it was Tessa who had prised her hand off the pot-handle and bound it in a strip of blanket soaked in cold water. Lyra sat with Bridie's daughter asleep in the crook of her good arm.

"Forty-seven," said Orrin Hale. "Thirty-nine of us. Eight of the twenty-two." He closed the tin box. "Three hundred and sixty-five of us, now. Fourteen of them." He looked at the fire. "Bridie's hollow is gone. Where she was sitting last night. Where she'd have been, this morning, if you hadn't walked her up here at the fourth hour."

Lyra looked at the baby in her arm.

"I don't know why you did," said Orrin. "I don't suppose you do either. I'm only telling you." He put the tin box down beside him on the rock. "Six years. Six years it's turned over in its sleep at the Refusal, every morning. You feel it in the rock. You get used to it. It turns over and it settles and we have breakfast. It's never done *that*. Not once. It's never reached."

Fen was sitting beside him with her knees drawn up inside the coat and her face white. She was talking, very low and very fast, to nobody, the days of the week. She stopped. She looked across the fire at Lyra with the enormous grey eyes.

"It went *up*," said Fen. "Didn't it. It didn't go for us. It went past us. Up the wall." Her voice was very small. "It's never gone up the wall. What's up there? What's up there that wasn't up there yesterday?"

Lyra looked at her.

She did not answer. She could not have answered; she had no voice and no slate. But she would not have answered if she had had both. She sat by the fire on the Shelf with a newborn asleep in her arm and her burned hand in a wet strip of blanket. And she looked up past Fen and past Orrin Hale, up the black glass of the Rift wall, three hundred feet, to the lip, and the obsidian wall above it, and the towers. Smoke was still drifting off the great brass mouths of the batteries in the grey morning air.

Somewhere up there, on the wall, a man with a glass arm was standing in the wind off the edge of the world with his hand over his mouth, saying nothing at all.

She knew he was. She could feel it, under the breastbone, the way she had always felt it.

She looked down again at the child. She did not look up at the wall again for the rest of the morning. And when Fen asked her a second time, she shook her head.

## CHAPTER FOUR
### The Third Floor

> *The Office keeps a surety copy of the name of every scribe sworn to it, against the day the scribe may need reminding of her duty.*
>
> — Oath of the Catalogue, the Third Article, as read aloud to Sable when the Office swore her in 416

*Null-Watch and the Shelf. Dawnday the twenty-first of Ashfall, to Driftday the twenty-second.*

---

Sable entered them by their dockets. It was the only way she had.

She sat on a flat stone by the fire in the angle of the Rift wall, with the Ledger open on her knees and Orrin Hale's tin box beside her. She took out the dockets one at a time, the thirty-nine that Orrin had moved from one side of the tin to the other on his long walk in the grey of the morning, and copied them into the Ledger in her small clear hand.

*Hob Tennery. Lost nine days in the spring of '17. Disposed at the lip, Harvestmonth '17. Taken by the quiet at the Shelf, the twenty-first of Ashfall, at the Refusal.*

*Ansel Dray. Lost his wife's laugh. Disposed at the lip, Frostmonth '16. Taken by the quiet—*

It was what she did. She wrote things down.

The dockets were square slips of buff card, a little larger than a playing-card, with a pinhole in the top where they had been fixed to a coat. Each had a printed heading, and a number, and three lines filled in by a clerk at the harvest-house in a fair round hand. And the heading on nearly every one of them was the same. She had known it would be before Orrin opened the tin. She had known it on the *Kestrel*, in the hold, when she wrote *SCHEDULE F* on the bulkhead in grease-pencil. And still, each time she lifted one out of the box and turned it to the firelight, she read it again, as if it might have changed.

> **OFFICE OF THE CATALOGUE — ROUTING FORM F/3**
> *Incomplete draw. Residual to be conveyed under escort to Null-Watch and disposed of at the lip. No further entry is required.*

Her words. Her sentence, from the spring of 416. She had drafted it at her desk on the second floor of the Office in the Organum, on a wet afternoon, with the rain on the clerestory and a cup of tea going cold at her elbow. She remembered it quite clearly. She had been pleased with *No further entry is required.* It saved a form.

She had not, at the time, asked herself what *disposed of at the lip* meant at the lip.

She copied the thirty-ninth docket. Then she turned to the eight who had no dockets. The eight of the twenty-two from the Malting who had sat at the far end of the Shelf, and had no names any more. And she entered them, as she had entered the Hollows of Whisper-Grove for three years, by the only thing they had left.

*The man who moved nine stones.*

*The woman who sang one line. The line was this:* and she wrote the line out in musical notation, from memory, as Orrin had hummed it to her flatly in the dawn, because it was a fragment of something and somebody, some day, might know what.

*Two people, a man and a woman, who held hands. Their loop was holding hands.*

She wrote them all. Then she sat with the Ledger open on her knees and looked at the forty-seven new lines. Her own hand, small and clear, and the date the same on every line. And she did a thing she had not done in all her years of keeping books for the Veil and the Office, or in three years of keeping this one.

She went back to the top of the page and read it aloud.

Not loudly. In the voice she used for entries. Each name, and what had been lost, and when the Office had disposed of it. Fen sat beside her with her knees drawn up inside the enormous coat and listened. Somewhere along the Shelf the talk went on, steady, the names of the living and what had gone. And Sable read the names of the forty-seven into it, one after another, so that the Shelf would have heard them said once more before they were only ink.

When she had finished, Fen said, "Thank you."

Sable closed the Ledger.

"Don't," she said. "Please."

---

She had come down at the first grey after the Refusal, alone, in the basket.

She had heard it from the wall. Not the Refusal; everyone at the Watch heard that. *After*. The silence in which the forty towers went back to their talk. And then, from far below, from three hundred feet down the black glass of the Rift, a sound she had not been able to place. It went on and on. Harsh, flat, iron, over and over, like a smith beating a bar.

She had known, at once, without knowing how, that it was the Cantor.

The Quartermaster had already been at the wheel. He had not said anything to her when she came out along the buttress in the dawn. He had looked at her face and hooked the basket on and let her down. And he had talked to her all the way, about his sister's cat, steadily, in a flat cheerful voice, with his hands shaking on the rope.

She had come down onto the Shelf and seen the edge.

She had been a scribe of the Silent Veil for longer than she could now count, and an officer of the Office of the Catalogue for three years. She had stood in the doorway of a harvest-house and counted children. She had walked the plazas of Whisper-Grove and catalogued seventy-four thousand people who did not know their names. There was very little, she had believed, that she had not seen and could not write down.

She stood on the Shelf in the grey of the morning and looked at the new edge of it, a hundred and fifty yards nearer than it had been at dusk. Clean and smooth as a cut loaf. And she found that her hands would not open the Ledger.

Lyra was by the fire with her right hand wrapped in a strip of wet blanket and a newborn in the crook of her left arm. Lyra had looked up at her across the fire. And Sable, for the first time since she had known her, could not read the Cantor's face, and did not try.

---

Tessa Wray found her at the fourth hour. Sable was at the foot of the Rift wall where the Shelf was widest, looking at the dockets in the tin.

"The Cantor wants you to talk to me," said Tessa. She sat down on the rock without being asked. She had a piece of charcoal in her fingers, and turned it over and over. "She wrote it on the wall by the fire. *ASK TESSA.* In very big letters, with her left hand. It took her a long time." She looked at the charcoal. "I'm not going to tell you what I told her. I don't think I could do it twice. I'll tell you what I told Orrin. You'll want it for your book."

Sable waited.

"I had my core back," said Tessa Wray. "My own. The cracked one. They pinned it to my coat by mistake, and it came over the lip with me. I held it for a month, and then I put it to my throat and called myself home. And I came." Her fingers turned the charcoal. "That's all. No press. No scribe. Nobody paid for it but me."

Sable went still.

"That's the bit Orrin says you'd want," said Tessa. "That nobody paid for it but me. He says that's important to you. He says you've a price, for putting people back. He says you've one left." She looked sideways at Sable. "Is that right?"

"Yes," said Sable.

"What is it?"

Sable thought about that. She had not been asked before. Not like that, so plainly, as if it were a thing a person might reasonably want to know.

"The smell of the Veil's scriptorium," she said, "on a winter morning. Before the stoves are lit. Cold stone, and ink, and the dust off the vellum, and very faintly, somebody's breakfast from the kitchens under the floor." She looked at the tin of dockets. "It's the last thing I have of myself that I can identify. There are other things. But they aren't *mine* any more. I don't know whose they are. I remember them the way you remember a book."

Tessa Wray was quiet for a moment.

"Then don't spend it on us," she said. "You don't have to. That's what I'm telling you. You put a Hollow back, you pay, because there's nobody home in a Hollow to call the piece. It's all gone out of them. Somebody has to stand in the door and say the name for them. That's you. And it costs." She turned the charcoal. "But us — we're not empty. We're only half. There's someone home. If we had our pieces in our hands, we could call them ourselves. The way I did. *A piece knows its way home, if there's someone home to call it.*" She put the charcoal down on the rock between them. "You don't have to pay for us. We'd pay for ourselves."

Sable looked at her.

"You screamed for nine days," she said.

"Yes."

"Orrin told me. And you won't say why."

"No."

"And you would let three hundred and sixty-five people do it."

"I'd let them choose," said Tessa Wray. She stood up. "Which is more than anybody at Ebb Cross did." She looked down at Sable on the rock with the tin box. Her face was quite calm. Her hands, Sable saw, had started to move again, very slightly, braiding something too fine to see. "You'd have to get them first. The pieces. Ask Orrin where they are. He knows. He's always known. He just never had anybody to tell who could do anything about it."

She went away along the Shelf.

Sable sat with the tin box and the charcoal for a long time. Then she picked up the charcoal and turned it over in her fingers, the way Tessa had. Then she opened the Ledger at the back, where the blank pages were, and began to do arithmetic.

---

It did not come out.

She did it three times, in three different ways, on three facing pages, in charcoal, because her pen was in the *Kestrel*. Every time it came to the same place and stopped, the way a cart stops at a ford it cannot cross.

*Cores in the hold: 396. Whisper-Grove harvest of 416. Pressed at harvest-houses on the Cloister road, the Malting among them.*

*Bodies of those 396: unknown. Some in the plazas of Whisper-Grove. Some at the Malting, in the residual ward. The ward was closed after we came through. 200 residuals disposed at the lip. 22 lived. 8 taken this morning. 14 left.*

*Of the 178 who did not live, or did not stay: some were the bodies of cores in my hold. I cannot know which. I do not have the Concordance. The Concordance is at the Hall of Carriage, second floor, Assignment.*

She stopped, and looked at that line, and went on.

*Of the 14 Hollows on the Shelf: some may be the bodies of cores in my hold. I cannot know which without the Concordance. If I knew, I could put one back. One. With what I have left.*

*Then I would have nothing left.*

She did not know what would happen then. Nobody did. Nobody had ever spent the last piece. She thought it likely that she would become what the fourteen at the far end of the Shelf were. That she would sit somewhere with her hands in her lap and say one sentence over and over, about ink, or winter, or stone.

*The Rendered on the Shelf: 365. Each one half-drawn. Each one's other half in a cracked core. The cracked cores—*

She did not know where the cracked cores were.

She wrote: *ASK ORRIN.*

And underneath that, very small, at the bottom of the page, because it was true and she was a scribe:

*It was my sentence that sent them here. It was my visit to the Malting that sent the two hundred after them. Two hundred people went over the lip on a single morning because I walked into a harvest-house and put a woman's name back. I did not think about what the Office would do with the ward afterward. I did not think about it at all.*

*I put one woman back. It cost two hundred.*

She looked at that for a long time. Then she closed the Ledger on it, because the arithmetic was finished. It did not come out, and nothing she could write on the page would make it.

---

She went up in the basket at dusk. She left Lyra on the Shelf with the baby and Bridie Cole and Fen, and went to find Kaelen, because there was a thing she needed to know, and only one person on the Watch could tell her.

She found him on the wall-walk between Towers Five and Six, where the Watch had kept him for three days. He was standing at the parapet in the pitchless wind with the big slow trooper who talked about a dog. Vesper was on the next stretch of wall, twenty yards off, sitting on the parapet with her back to the Rift and her coat drawn tight. Something in the coat was straining. Sable could see it from the stair. It was pressing against the cloth toward the edge of the world, the way a dog strains on a lead toward a gate. Vesper had both hands on it.

Neither of them could go down. That was the arithmetic too. Kaelen, because of the glass. Vesper, because of what was in her coat. Three hundred and sixty-five people on the Shelf, every one with a hole in them where something had been taken, and a thing that found what hurt worst in a person and ate it. Vesper had not needed to be told. She had looked over the parapet on the first night at the cooking fire three hundred feet below, and wrapped her coat round the thing inside it, and sat down on the wall, and had not moved since.

Sable stopped at the top of the stair and looked at the two of them, twenty yards apart on the wall, each holding on to what they had brought.

Then she went to Kaelen.

He saw her coming and turned. He did not say anything. He had not said anything to anyone, Two-Eleven told her later, since the morning in Battery Six, except the Vance Sequence, which he had recited to the edge of the world four times through.

He took something out of the breast of his coat. A folded square of paper, torn from the back of one of Pennick's tank-charts. He held it out to her.

She took it. She unfolded it.

His hand. An engineer's hand, square and careful, block capitals, the hand of a man who had written specifications for twenty years. She had seen it on the margins of the Wendmere drawings. It was not careful now. The letters leaned. The pencil had gone through the paper in two places.

*AT THE EDGE HE CAN'T HEAR. 30 YDS FROM THE LIP. THE ARM GOES QUIET AND HE GOES. I'VE TRIED IT. ONCE. WARD SAW.*

*BUT IT TAKES YOU. THE QUIET. I WANTED TO SIT DOWN. WARD PULLED ME BACK.*

*DON'T SAY ANY OF THIS ALOUD. NOT NEAR ME. NOT ANYWHERE. HE DOESN'T KNOW WHERE I WENT. HE'S BEEN LISTENING FOR IT EVER SINCE.*

And under it, smaller, as if he had come back to it afterward:

*IT WAS ME, THIS MORNING. WASN'T IT. WHAT IT REACHED FOR.*

Sable read it twice.

She looked up at him. He was watching her face. His own was grey and set, and his right hand was at his throat. She saw the edge of the glass there, under his fingers, the cyan and the thread of green in it, half a finger from the hollow. She saw that he already knew the answer to the last line, and that he had written it anyway, because he needed somebody to say it.

She could not say it. Not here. Not within thirty feet.

So she nodded.

He shut his eyes.

---

She burned it in the brazier at the foot of Tower Six, where the battery crew warmed their hands between Refusals. She held the paper in the coals by one corner until it caught, and watched it go black and curl and come apart in the heat. *AT THE EDGE HE CAN'T HEAR.* Gone. *IT WAS ME.* Gone.

It was the second document she had ever destroyed. The first had been a letter from a dead Prior at the top of the Salt Stair, naming the *Kestrel* to a man who would have burned her. She had thought, at the time, that she would never do it again. That a scribe who burned one document had stopped being a scribe and had become something else, and she did not yet know what.

She was learning.

She stood by the brazier and thought about what the engineer had written, and what it meant, which he would have seen too.

*At the edge he can't hear.* Thirty yards from the lip of the world, there was a place where a word could be said that would not go to the Organum. The only such place on the continent. And it was the place where the Quiet took you. Where the Lead Brothers stood with their mouths sealed in metal. Where a hundred and eighty troopers of the Watch had gone to listen. Where a man with a hundredweight of glass on his arm had wanted, so badly, to sit down.

A room where you could speak freely, if you could stand to stay in it. And to stay in it was to be pulled into the dark.

And the other thing. The thing he had not had to write, because she had read it on his face.

*This morning it reached for me.* Six years the thing below had slept under the Refusal. And on the first dawn when Dawning glass stood on the wall above it, it had turned over and *reached*. Forty-seven people had gone into the quiet because they were in the way.

She did the arithmetic. She could not help it. *If the engineer is on the wall at the next Refusal—*

She went to find the General.

---

Theresa Ward was in her room at the top of Tower Nine. It was a round cell of obsidian with a cot and a desk and a brass chime-pair on a shelf, and nothing on the walls but a map of forty miles of wall with every battery marked. 94 sat on a stool by the door, talking quietly about the price of lamp-oil. The General was at the desk, reading.

She looked up as Sable came in. She did not tap. She waited.

"The engineer," said Sable. "At the Refusal tomorrow. Where will he be?"

Ward looked at her for a long moment. Then she tapped. One long, two short.

"The General says," said 94, without a pause in the lamp-oil, "she has already thought of that. The General says she has sent the man with the glass arm to the mooring-yard, on the west side of the Watch, with Two-Eleven, at the fifth hour. As far from the lip as the Watch goes." A pause. "The General says she wants to see what happens."

"So do I," said Sable.

Ward tapped again. A long phrase, slow, deliberate, her knuckles on the iron.

"The General says," said 94, "if it reaches for him tomorrow from the west side of the Watch, then it will reach for him from anywhere. And then he must go. Far. Very far." His voice did not change at all. "The General says, if it does not reach for him tomorrow, then he must still go. Because now it knows he is on the Watch, and it will be listening. The General has stood on this wall six years, and she knows what it is like to be listened to by something that wants you."

Sable looked at the General. The General looked back. And Sable understood that she was being told something by a woman who had once been able to speak. Who had shouted her voice to rags on this wall six years ago to hold the thing in the Rift. Who had stood at the edge before dawn that morning and beaten her fist on her own breastplate to pull a man back from the Quiet. And who was telling her now, as plainly as iron-cant could tell anything, that the man would have to go, and that the General was sorry.

"Thank you," said Sable.

Ward nodded once, and went back to her reading.

---

Orrin Hale drew the Hall of Carriage that night, by the fire on the Shelf, on the back of Jonah Pennick's tank-chart.

It was Vane who brought the chart down. He came down in the basket at the third hour of the night with Mags, his ribs strapped and his face a mess, and the rolled chart under his good arm. He sat down by the fire across from Orrin and unrolled it on the black rock and weighted the corners with stones. He did not say why he had chosen that one, out of all the charts on the *Kestrel*.

Sable saw the front of it as he unrolled it. Pennick's lift-tables, and a course in pencil, and near the top in Pennick's round untidy hand, three words and a number.

*Hanging Water. Dead hour. 500.*

Vane turned it over. The back was blank.

Orrin Hale looked at the front for a moment before he turned it face-down. Then he looked up at Vane. He did not say anything. He took a stick of charcoal from the fire, knocked the glowing end off it on the rock, and began to draw.

They were round him in the firelight. Vane and Mags. Lyra, with the baby asleep in a sling of blanket against her chest and her burned hand in her lap. Tessa Wray. Fen, with her chin on her knees. Sable, with the Ledger. And beyond the circle of the fire, sitting along the back wall of the Shelf in the dark, two dozen Rendered, not talking for once, but whispering. The names and what was gone, very low, so as not to drown him out.

Three hundred feet up the black glass and half a mile along the wall, the engineer stood on the wall-walk between Towers Five and Six. He was too far away to hear any of it. Too far for anything said by the fire on the Shelf to go into the glass on his arm and along it to a white room at the Organum.

Too far to be in the room. Sable thought about that while Orrin drew. She thought about the engineer on the wall, alone, looking out at the edge of the world, while three hundred feet below him and half a mile along, the people he had walked into the Bleach for drew a plan he could not know.

---

"Registry Square," said Orrin Hale.

He drew a rectangle. Long, grey, in charcoal on the back of a dead man's chart. Four sides, each lined with oblongs.

"Four sides. Grey stone. Four storeys, every building, no ornament. A brass plate by every door. You'll know it." He did not look at Sable. "North side, the Arbiterium. Courts, Justiciars, the writs. West side, the Treasury and the Rolls. South side, the Office. The Office of the Catalogue. Where the scribe worked. Registry, records, the Registrar-General's rooms on the fourth floor." He drew a small square at the south-east corner. "Coffee stall. Sixty years. Man called Ambrose. He's served every clerk in Registry Square every morning since before I was born and he's never once asked any of us what we did."

He drew a long oblong on the east side of the square, bigger than the others.

"The Hall of Carriage. Not on any plan of the city. You won't find it on a map. It's long and grey and there's no plate on the door. People who work in the square walk past it every day of their lives and never look at it. That's the point of it." He tapped it with the charcoal. "Three floors and a basement. I worked there nineteen years. Tally-clerk. Ground floor, Receiving. I counted things in."

He drew the building again, larger, in section, the way an engineer draws a cut through a house. Four bands, one on top of the other: the basement, and three floors above it.

"Basement. Sorting." He shaded it, slowly, with the side of the charcoal, until it was black. "That's where the pressings come in from the harvest-houses. In crates. Like the ones in your hold, Captain. They're opened and the cores are graded. By voice, mostly. A sorter holds a core to her ear and listens to how it says its name, and grades it. Clear, fair, poor. And they go into racks. Rows and rows. By grade and by number. It's cold down there. They keep it cold. The cores keep better cold." He was quiet for a moment. "It smells of honey. Very faintly. Nobody ever says so."

"And the cracked ones?" said Sable.

He looked at her for the first time.

"Bin seven," he said. "At the far end of Sorting. Past the racks. A cold-room with a door, and a lock. Incomplete draws. The ones that failed in the press, that came out half." He drew a small square at the end of the black band. "The Office doesn't throw away a voice. Even a broken one. You never know, the Registrar-General used to say, when one might be wanted. So they keep them. In bin seven. On shelves. By docket number." He looked at the tin box at Sable's elbow. "*By docket number.* Every cracked core in bin seven has a tag. And the tag has the same number as the docket that was pinned to the coat of the residual that went over the lip."

He let that sit.

Sable looked at the tin box. At three hundred and sixty-five buff cards, and thirty-nine more she had copied into the Ledger that morning. Each with a pinhole in the top. Each with a number.

"It's an index," said Vane slowly.

"It's an index," said Orrin Hale. "To bin seven. I've been sitting on the Shelf for three years with an index to bin seven in a tin box in my hole in the wall. I didn't know what to do with it. There was nobody to give it to." He put the charcoal back on the drawing. "First floor, at the street. Receiving. Carriage. Where I was. Crates come in from the harvest-houses on carts and go down to Sorting. Cores come up from Sorting and go out to the Legions, and the engines, and the clients. Every one with a carrier's receipt in red wax. A key crossed with a quill, inside a ring." He drew it, small, in the corner: a key, a quill, a circle. "Tuning stock comes in at Receiving too. Forty bottles a month off the Cape. In straw, in crates marked *glass, fragile*. I signed for every one of them for three years, until they took me down to the chair." He did not look up. "I didn't know what they were. I didn't ask."

"The second floor," said Sable.

"Assignment. Where the cores are allocated. Which Legion, which engine, which client. Clerks at long desks under the windows. Ledgers. And in the corner room at the north end, the Concordance." He drew a square. "A great book. Three great books, now. Every core that's ever come through the Hall, by number. And beside every number, where the residual went. *Whisper-Grove, plaza of such-and-such. Malting, residual ward. Schedule F, the lip.* So that if a client complains a core is unruly, they can find the body it came out of and see why." He looked at Sable. "You'll want that."

"Yes," said Sable.

"Deputy Registrar runs the second floor. Hester Vell. Grey hair, spectacles, a cardigan she knits herself. She'll know you."

"She was kind to me," said Sable. "When I was new."

"She's kind to everybody," said Orrin Hale. "She was kind to me, the day they took me down to the chair. She brought me a cup of tea." He did not say anything else about that. He drew the last band. The top one.

"Third floor."

---

He drew it carefully. More carefully than the others. A long corridor down the middle of the band, running the length of the building. Doors on either side. And at the far end, at the north end, a room.

"The corridor's carpeted," said Orrin Hale. "Red. Thick. The only carpet in the Hall. You'll feel it under your feet before you see it, coming up the stair. It changes how you walk. Everybody who goes up there goes quiet without meaning to, because of the carpet." He drew small marks along the corridor, close together, all the way to the end. "The doors on the sides are offices. The Registrar-General's deputy for Carriage. The Office's own auditors. Nobody much goes in them. The corridor's what matters. And the room at the end."

He drew the room. A small square. And in it, against the far wall, a long narrow oblong.

"The cabinet," he said. "Walnut. Glass-fronted. Brass handles. Six trays, one above the other, pulling out like drawers. Each tray lined with velvet. Dark red. And in the velvet, in rows, in little hollows pressed to fit them like eggs in a box—"

"The staff trays," said Sable.

Her voice came out quite level. She heard it. She did not know how she had managed it.

Orrin Hale looked at her across the fire for a long moment.

"The staff trays," he said. "Very small cores. Smaller than a pressing. Smaller than a thumbnail. One for every scribe and clerk and officer ever sworn to the Office. Not a pressing. A copy. Taken at the swearing, with a little fork, very gently, while you say the oath. You don't feel it. You don't know. You're not meant to know. It's only a copy. You keep the original. The Office keeps the copy." He put the charcoal on the drawing, on the long oblong in the little room. "Against the day."

"*Against the day the scribe may need reminding of her duty,*" said Sable.

"That's what the card says," said Orrin. "There's a card in a brass frame on the front of the cabinet. I read it, the day I was sent up with a tally. I'd been nineteen years in the Hall and I'd never been sent up. A clerk on the third floor was ill and I took a tally up for him. And I walked down the red carpet and into the room, and there was the cabinet. And the trays were open, because somebody was dusting. And there were the little cores in the velvet, in their rows. And there were *names* on them."

He drew a row of tiny circles in the oblong. Then, under each one, a short line.

"Little ivory tags. Hand-lettered. A name on every one. I read them. I couldn't help it. I was a tally-clerk; I read labels. I read the first tray, and the second, and they were names I knew. People from the square. People I saw every morning at Ambrose's stall. Hester Vell was in the second tray. The Registrar-General was in the first."

He drew the third tray, and the circles in it, and the tags.

"I didn't read the third tray," he said. "Somebody came in. A deputy. And he saw me reading, and he asked what I was doing, and I said I'd brought a tally up. And then — I don't know why. I've thought about it for three years. I could have said nothing. I asked him why the cores in the cabinet had staff names on them."

The fire cracked.

"That's all," said Orrin Hale. "That's all I did. I asked a question." He put the charcoal down. "They took me down to the chair the next morning. Hester Vell brought me a cup of tea. I don't know what the seed was in my glass. It was poor. It failed half-way. And what they had of me when it failed was the fear."

He looked round the fire at them, one by one. At Vane, and Mags, and Lyra with the baby, and Tessa, and Fen. At Sable.

"I know what you're all thinking," he said. "You're all thinking, *what's that like*. Everybody does. They're too polite to ask. So I'll tell you." His voice did not change. Low, pleasant, like a man reading a receipt. "It's not like being brave. I want you to understand that. Brave is being afraid and doing it anyway. I'm not afraid. I know about the drop. I can see the edge. I know that if I walk off it I'll die, or worse. I know it the way I know two and two. It just doesn't *matter*. It doesn't reach me. Fear isn't knowing. It's the thing that makes knowing *matter*. It's the thing that takes hold of you by the collar and shakes you and says *this is you, this is your life, look*. I haven't got that. I know everything. It just never takes hold."

He was quiet for a moment.

"So I walk the edge every night, and look," he said. "Because somebody has to, and I'm the only one who can do it without wanting to go over. And I went across the quiet this morning and fetched old Nan back, because I could, and it cost me a boot." He looked at his bare left foot on the black rock, white in the firelight. "And Fen hates it. And Tessa hates it. And everybody on the Shelf hates it. And I don't feel anything about that either. I know they hate it. I know I ought to mind. I'd like to. I can't."

Nobody said anything.

Sable found she could not look at him. She looked at the charcoal drawing on the back of the chart instead. At the long corridor and the little room at the end and the cabinet with its six trays. She saw that Vane was looking at the fire. And Mags at her own hands. And Lyra at the baby. And Tessa at the dark. And Fen had put her face down on her knees inside the enormous coat. Nobody round the fire on the Shelf could look at Orrin Hale. And Orrin Hale sat there with his bare foot on the rock and watched them not look at him, and did not mind.

"It's in the fourth tray," he said. "Mine. My fear. The deputy told me, when they took me down. He said it was fitting. A clerk who forgot his duty, kept with the staff, so the staff could see what happened to clerks who forgot their duty." He picked up the charcoal again. He drew a small cross in the fourth tray. "There."

Then he drew another cross. In the third tray. Near the middle.

"And that's yours, scribe," said Orrin Hale. "I didn't read it. But I saw where they were dusting. The deputy came in just as the duster was lifting one out of the third tray. Near the middle. And the duster looked at the tag and said to the deputy, *this is the one from the Veil, isn't it, that went to Whisper-Grove*. And the deputy said yes, and to put it back carefully." He looked at Sable. "There's only one from the Veil that went to Whisper-Grove."

---

She did not say anything for a long time. Nobody did. The fire burned. The Rendered along the back wall whispered their names.

She looked at the cross in the third tray, in charcoal, on the back of Jonah Pennick's chart.

She had known it was there. She had known for three years, since she was told it in that room. She had thought about it for five weeks, since she gave up her third piece in the harvest-house at the Malting. She had understood then that what she had given was her own name, the true one, the one under *Sable*. The Office had a copy of it on the third floor of the Hall of Carriage, in the third tray, near the middle.

A copy. She would not get the original back. The original was in Bettis Renn now, or gone, or spent; she did not know how the price worked, only that it was paid. But a copy was not nothing. A copy, taken at her swearing, with a little fork, while she said the oath. Her name, as it had been on the morning the Office swore her in, in 416, before Whisper-Grove, before the harvest schedule, before any of it.

If she had it in her hand, she could call it home. *A piece knows its way home, if there's someone home to call it.* She was still home. Just.

And then she would have two pieces. Her name and the scriptorium.

And she could spend one.

She looked at the arithmetic, which she had not written down, because she had not needed to. Two pieces. Fourteen Hollows on the Shelf who might, or might not, be the bodies of cores in her hold. Three hundred and ninety-six cores. Three hundred and sixty-five Rendered whose other halves were in bin seven, on shelves, by docket number, with an index in a tin box at her elbow. A building four hundred miles away that she used to walk past every morning with a cup of Ambrose's coffee.

It still did not come out. It was never going to come out.

But it came out *differently*.

---

They went up in the basket at the fourth hour, before dawn. Vane, Mags, Sable. They left Lyra on the Shelf with the baby. She shook her head when Sable looked at her from the basket, and Sable did not argue.

At the fifth hour Two-Eleven took the engineer down from the wall to the mooring-yard on the west side of the Watch. It was as far from the lip as the Watch went. Sable watched them go from the top of Tower Nine: the big slow trooper talking, the engineer silent at his side with the sack on his arm. Down the stair. Across the yard. To the foot of the *Kestrel*'s mast, where the ship swung on her lines in the pitchless wind with her patched bag and her three tanks.

At the sixth hour, the Refusal.

She stood in Battery Nine beside the General and watched it. The chant. The bellows. The great brass mouth, and the roar going out over the edge of the world in a solid wall of white. She felt the not-colour flinch.

And she felt, through the obsidian under her boots, from far down in the dark under the Shelf, the thing turn over.

She held her breath.

It turned over. Slowly. The way it had turned every dawn for six years, according to Orrin and Fen and the General and everyone on the Watch who had ever stood at a battery. A sleeper turning when a door bangs.

And settled.

It did not reach. She waited, with the roar going over and the General's iron shoulder against hers, for it to reach. It did not. It turned, and settled, and lay still in the dark at the bottom of the world.

The roar stopped. The talk came back.

The General turned her head and looked at Sable. Sable looked back at her. Neither of them needed iron-cant.

*Then it was him.*

---

The chime came at the seventh hour.

Sable was still in the General's room at the top of Tower Nine. She was drinking a cup of something hot that 94 had put in her hands, and he was talking about the weather. The brass chime-pair on the shelf began to sing.

It was a thin high sound, like a fingertip drawn round the rim of a wineglass. It went up and down in the long and short of the Arbiterium's chime-cipher. The General got up from her desk and went to it and stood listening with her head bent, a pencil in her hand. 94 stopped talking about the weather. He went to the shelf and stood beside her, and when the chime stopped he took the pencil from her and the pad from the desk and began to read the cipher off a card on the wall. He wrote it out, in clear, letter by letter. The General stood behind him and read it over his shoulder as he wrote. Sable watched her face.

It did not change at all.

When 94 had finished, the General took the pad from him and read it through once more. Then she held it out to Sable.

Sable took it.

> *WRIT OF THE ARBITERIUM. Under the seal of the Justiciary.*
>
> *To the Commandant, Null-Watch; and to the crews of Batteries Eight, Nine and Ten.*
>
> *It being found that residuals consigned under Schedule F remain at the lip in a state of incomplete disposal, contrary to routing; and that provision has been made to them without authority;*
>
> *It is directed that provision cease forthwith; and that the Refusal be withdrawn from the sector of Batteries Eight, Nine and Ten; and that disposal be thereby completed.*
>
> *With effect from the Refusal of the third Nullday following receipt, the writ having first been posted at the gate for the statutory period.*
>
> *The writ of the fourth of Harvestmonth respecting the vessel* Drift-Kestrel *and all souls aboard her stands, and is to be observed. A column of the Justiciary is directed to the Watch to see both writs executed.*
>
> *Entered at the Organum. A. Vaelen, Justiciar, for the Arbiterium.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, the way she read everything that mattered, slowly, word by word, as a scribe reads. And on the third reading she understood it. Not what it said. What it *was*.

It did not say *kill*. It did not say *fire on*. There was not a verb in it with a person as its object. *Provision cease. Refusal withdrawn. Disposal completed.* Nobody would lift a hand against anybody. The Quartermaster would not lower a basket. Three batteries would not fire. The guns above the Shelf would stand silent under their lead hoods on the morning of the third Nullday, and the crews would stand beside them with their hands at their sides. And under the Shelf, in the dark at the bottom of the world, something would turn over in its sleep with no door banging to make it settle again.

And nobody would have done anything at all.

It was the best-drafted writ she had ever read. She could have written it herself. She very nearly had: *No further entry is required.*

"How did they know?" said 94. His voice had gone quite steady and quite flat. "How did they know about the Shelf? Six years. Command's known. Nobody's ever *said*—"

Sable did not answer.

She was thinking of what Vane had told her by the fire on the Shelf the night before, with his face in his good hand. A stretch of wall-walk between Towers Five and Six, at the fifth hour on the night they came, and himself leaning on the parapet beside the engineer, saying *two thousand and eleven* and *knots* and *the bread* and *it's people*. With Two-Eleven talking about his dog to cover it, as if a dog could cover anything from a man who listened through glass. Of every trooper on every wall who had talked about the ones below for three days, idly, in among the onions. People always said where they were. They could not help it.

They were people. That was all.

"*A. Vaelen,*" said 94. "That's the son. The Dread Arbiter. It's not the — it's not *him*."

"No," said Sable. "It never is. He never signs anything."

---

The General took the pad back from her.

She stood at the desk in the round obsidian room at the top of Tower Nine, and read the writ one more time. Then she set it down on the desk, squared to the edge, and put her pencil beside it. She stood for a while looking at the map on the wall. Forty miles of wall, every battery marked. Eight, Nine and Ten in a row, above the place on the map where somebody, a long time ago, had drawn in pencil a small shelf of rock in the Rift and written beside it, in very bad writing, a single word. Sable could not read it from where she stood.

Then the General lifted her right hand and struck the iron over her heart.

It was not a long phrase. Four blows. Flat, heavy, slow. With a pause between each one, as if she wanted every one of them to be heard all the way down the wall.

94 looked at her. Sable saw his throat move.

"The General says," he said.

He stopped. He had never stopped before. Sable had not heard him stop once in three days.

"The General says," said 94, "*The Watch holds the wall.*"

The General took her hand away from her breastplate. She sat down at her desk, and picked up the next paper on the pile, and began to read it.

---

Sable went down to the Shelf in the basket at noon. She sat by the fire in the angle of the Rift wall with the Ledger open on her knees and the tin box of dockets beside her, and her pen, which Vane had brought down from the *Kestrel*.

Lyra was across the fire with the baby. Bridie Cole was asleep. Fen was talking to the baby about the weather. Orrin Hale was walking the edge. Tessa Wray sat by the back wall with her hands moving.

Sable turned to a new page.

She had kept books for longer than she could remember. For the Veil, for the Office, for herself. In all that time she had written down the deaths of nine civilizations and seventy-four thousand names, and the routing of the residuals, and the forty-seven of the morning before. She had written in the voice the Veil taught and the Office perfected. The voice in which things are recorded, and nothing is done. *Disposed of. Taken by the quiet. Returned. Broken. No further entry is required.* The voice of a sentence with no one in it.

She looked at the blank page for a long time.

Then she wrote, in her small clear hand, the date. *Driftday, the twenty-second of Ashfall, 419. The Shelf.*

And under it, a sentence. Nine words. She had not written a sentence like it since she was a girl in the scriptorium of the Veil on a winter morning before the stoves were lit, with the smell of cold stone and ink and dust and somebody's breakfast coming up through the floor. Before anyone had taught her better.

A sentence with a person in it, doing something to someone.

*I am going to the Hall to fetch them.*

She looked at it. It looked strange on the page. Raw. Unfinished, the way a sentence looks when it has no clause after it to soften it.

She did not soften it. She blotted it, and closed the Ledger, and looked up across the fire.

Lyra was watching her. The Cantor had seen her write it. Lyra could not have read it upside-down at that distance, Sable thought, and had read it anyway, on her face. Lyra looked at her across the fire on the Shelf, with a newborn asleep against her chest and her burned hand in her lap. Very slowly, with two fingers of her good hand, she made the *Kestrel*'s sign.

*Here.*

Sable looked at her for a long time.

Then, badly, with ink-stained fingers, because she had learned it by writing it down, she made the sign back.
