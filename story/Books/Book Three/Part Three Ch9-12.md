# Echoes of the Shattered Octave — Book Three: The Hall of Carriage
## Part Three: The Press (Chapters 9–12)

## PART THREE: THE PRESS

## CHAPTER NINE
### Last

> *KEEP TALKING*
>
> — The first note in the first basket, summer 413, in very bad writing. Kept, with nearly two thousand others, in a biscuit tin in a hole in the wall of the Shelf

*The Shelf. Nullday the second of Frostmonth, to Rootday the third.*

---

The notes had changed.

Fen noticed it on the first morning after the old one's rope came down. The new rope went up the black glass, stiff and yellow, and came down again with a basket on the end of it, and the bread, and the blanket for Bridie's girl on top. There was a note under the blanket. There always had been. Six years of notes, every night, folded small and tucked in under the bread, and every one said the same two words in the same bad capitals, pressed so hard the pencil had gone through.

This one was a whole page.

She had taken it to the fire and unfolded it and read it aloud, because that was what you did on the Shelf; you read things aloud. And everybody round the fire had gone quiet. Not stopped. Gone quiet, the way you do in a church. It was not two words. It was a page and a half, both sides, in a big slow round hand like a child's, and it was about a dog.

*His name was Rufus and he could open a gate. My father kept sheep in the Peaks above Coldharrow and he had a gate on the top field with a latch on it, an iron latch, the kind you lift. And Rufus worked out how to lift it with his nose. Nobody taught him. He just watched us do it. He would stand at the gate and watch my father lift the latch every morning and one day he did it himself. And then he could not stop. He let the sheep out every night for a month. My father tied the latch with wire and Rufus chewed the wire. My father put a stone on it and Rufus pushed the stone off. In the end my father said, well, if he wants them out that badly he can bring them in again in the morning, and he did. Every morning. All of them. My father never had to go up to the top field again.*

*I don't know why I'm telling you this. Halvard said keep talking. I'm keeping talking.*

*Is the blanket all right? His sister knitted it. I wrote to her for him. I'll write to her again now. I don't know what to say.*

*Keep talking. — 211*

Fen had read it twice. Then she had folded it up very small and put it in the biscuit tin with the rest. She sat with the tin in her lap for a long time with her hand on the lid.

*Two-Eleven*, she thought. *The one with the dog.*

He wrote a page every night after that. Eight nights. About the dog, and the Peaks, and his mother, and a girl in Coldharrow he had wanted to marry, and the price of boot-leather. And at the bottom, every night, the two words, in his big slow round hand, not pressed through.

*Keep talking.*

She did. She read every one aloud.

---

On the ninth night, the ships came back.

She saw them before anybody. She was always the one who saw things first on the Shelf. She was small, and she sat at the edge where the big ones didn't like to sit, with her feet over the drop, and looked up. At the strip of grey sky over the Rift, at the lip of the world three hundred feet above, at the black line of the wall and the towers on it.

Two envelopes against the dusk, coming in low from the west. One brown and patched. One black, with something silver on it.

"*Orrin*," she said. "*Orrin, they're back. Both of them.*"

Orrin Hale was walking the edge. He always was, at dusk, at the narrow end where the new edge was, the one that had come a hundred and fifty yards nearer in the night. He stopped and looked up where she was pointing. He looked for a long while.

"Yes," he said. "So they are."

He came along the Shelf to where she was sitting and stood beside her, and looked up at them, and did not say anything else. And Fen, who had known Orrin Hale for three years and had learned to read what was not in his face because there was never anything in it, felt something go cold in her stomach.

"Orrin," she said. "Did it work?"

He did not answer.

---

He came down in the basket at the first hour of the night. Alone. He had gone up in it nine days ago with the scribe and the captain and the knight and the deaf woman and the others, and he came down by himself, with a sack on his back.

She was at the foot of the rope. She was always at the foot of the rope.

He climbed out of the basket. He set the sack down on the black rock, very carefully, the way you set down something that might break. He was wearing one boot, still. He had lost the other one in the quiet, carrying old Nan back, and had never found another that fitted. His bare foot was white on the rock.

He looked at her.

"Fen," said Orrin Hale.

And she knew.

She did not know how. He had never had anything in his face, not once, in three years. He did not have anything in it now. He looked at her the way he looked at the edge, the way he looked at everything: interested, polite, a little curious. And she knew anyway, the way you know the weather has turned before you feel the first drop.

"Mine's gone," said Fen.

"Yes," said Orrin.

---

She did not cry. She had thought she would. She had thought about it, sometimes, at night, in her hole in the wall, with the biscuit tin. What she would do if they found her core, and what she would do if they didn't. She had always thought that if they didn't, she would cry.

She stood at the foot of the rope and found that what came out of her instead was talk.

"How?" said Fen. "Did they — was it the hopper? The mill? Did they grind it for seed? Tessa said they grind the poor ones, she said Bridie said the young man at the—"

"No," said Orrin. "A bar. A tuned bar. It breaks cracked glass along the crack. The Registrar-General struck it, and it went down the shelves from the door, and it broke them. In order. Low numbers first. Then the deaf woman put her arm round the bar and stopped it. But not before—" He looked at her. "You were near the door, Fen. You were the fourth."

"I know I was the fourth," said Fen. "I was the fourth one ever. I was a trial." She was talking very fast. She could hear herself. "I was a trial at Ebb Cross in the summer of '13 when they were still learning how. The fourth one they ever did. Three before me and the man said *right, let's try a small one*." She stopped. She started again. "Did it hurt?"

Orrin thought about that. He always thought about things properly before he answered them.

"I don't know," he said. "I don't think so. It said half your name and stopped."

"Which half?"

"The first."

Fen looked at him.

"*Fen*'s only got one half," she said. "It's one syllable. There's no first half. There's just *Fen*." And then, before she could stop it, a sound came out of her that was not talk. It was a laugh, high and cracked, like a plate breaking. "There's no first half, Orrin. You can't say half of *Fen*. It's not my real name anyway, my real name's Fenella, nobody calls me that, my mum called me that when I was in trouble. *Fenella Ashby, you come here this minute.* So it'd have been *Fen—*. That's what it said. It said *Fen—* and then it broke." The laugh came again. It hurt this time. "It said what everybody calls me. And then it broke."

Orrin Hale stood on the black rock with his one boot and looked at her.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I mean it. I just can't feel it. I'd give a great deal to."

"I know," said Fen. "I know you can't. It's all right." She wiped her face with the sleeve of the old one's coat, which came down past her hands even rolled back four times. "It's all right, Orrin. It's all right. I'll just stay twelve. I've been twelve six years. I'm good at it. I'm the best at being twelve of anybody on this Shelf."

And then, at last, she cried.

---

He told the Shelf afterward, at the fire. All of it. He stood in the firelight with the sack at his feet and the tin of dockets in his arms and read them out. One by one. The numbers. The names. Whole, or gone.

A hundred and thirty-one whole.

Two hundred and thirty-two gone.

Fen sat at the edge of the firelight with her knees drawn up inside the coat and listened to every one. She knew all of them. She knew every docket in that tin, every name, every missing thing. She had sat by every one of them at the fire at one time or another, in six years, and talked. She heard Orrin read them out, *whole*, *gone*, *whole*, *gone*. She watched people's faces at the fire when he read theirs.

Maud, who had lost the word for mother: *gone*. Maud nodded once, as if she had known, and said very quietly, "The woman who had me," and nothing else.

Corran, who couldn't read: *gone*. Corran nodded, slowly, as if someone had told him the price of something. He looked at the fire. Then he said, very steadily, "Corran. I can't read. Corran. I can't read," the way you say it on the Shelf. As if nothing had changed. As if it never would.

Edwin Rook, who had lost the colour blue: *whole*. Edwin made a sound Fen had never heard a grown man make.

Bridie: *whole*.

Bridie was sitting by the fire with the baby at her breast, in the blanket the old one's sister had knitted. She looked up when Orrin read her number. She looked at her left hand, the one that did not know it was hers, lying on the baby's back. She did not say anything. But Fen saw her hand move. Not the left. The right. It came up and covered the left one, on the baby's back, and held it.

"Halla," said Bridie Cole, after a while. To nobody. To the baby. "Did you hear that, Halla? Mummy's hand's coming home."

They had named her on the fourth night. After the old one. *Halvard*, Bridie had said, *is no name for a girl, so Halla.* Fen had written it on a scrap of paper for the biscuit tin, so there would be a record. She had put it on top.

---

The scribe came down at the second hour. Fen saw her come.

She was different. Fen saw it the moment she climbed out of the basket. She had been here before, nine days ago, walking the Shelf with Orrin and writing everything down in her book. She had read the names of the forty-seven by the fire in her voice for entries, and Fen had said thank you, and she had said *don't*. She had been grey then, and tired, and her face had been all edges, like a face cut out of paper. Fen had liked her face. You could see everything on it if you looked. She had just never let you look for long.

Her face was smooth now.

Not calm. Fen knew calm; Orrin was calm. Not tired. *Smooth.* Like a pebble that has been in the sea a long time. Like the faces at the far end of the Shelf, the fourteen, sitting with their hands in their laps. Not that smooth. Not yet. But going that way.

She had a great book with her, a grey leather book with brass corners, as big as a gravestone, slung between two of the troopers from the Watch. She had them carry it to the far end of the Shelf, where the fourteen sat. She sat down among the Hollows on the black rock with the book open across her knees and a lantern beside her, and began to read it.

Fen went and sat near her. Not too near. You could tell with the scribe when you were too near.

The scribe turned the pages. Columns, ruled in red. Numbers. Fen could see them upside-down in the lantern-light. WG and a number, and then words, and then more words. The scribe ran her ink-stained finger down the columns, page after page, and every so often she stopped and looked up at one of the fourteen, sitting on the rock in the lantern-light with their hands in their laps. Old Nan, twisting her hands. A tall man with grey hair who sat very straight. A young woman with a long plait who rocked, very slightly, forward and back. The scribe looked at each one for some time, and looked back at the book, and made a mark in her own book, the small one, with her pen.

After a while she stopped turning pages.

She sat with her finger on one line. She did not move. Fen waited. She was good at waiting, for somebody who talked so much. You had to be, on the Shelf.

"What is it?" said Fen, after a while.

The scribe did not look up.

"A child," she said. Her voice was very even. "In the hold of the *Kestrel*. In a crate. WG-41,006. Six years old. She asked a boy his name through the side of the crate, once, and he told her. Her name is Annet Sowerby." Her finger did not move from the line. "Her body was in the residual ward at the Malting. Five weeks ago they closed the ward. Two hundred residuals. Carts. The lip."

Fen looked at the fourteen. At old Nan and the tall man and the young woman with the plait. There were no children among them. There had never been any children on the Shelf, not ever, not in six years. Only her. And she wasn't one.

"She's not here," said Fen.

"No."

"She'd have been the second," said Fen. "After me. The second one to look like a child."

The scribe closed the great book. She sat with her hands flat on its grey leather cover and her face smooth in the lantern-light, and looked at the fourteen.

"I have her name," she said. "In a crate. Saying itself. Every night. *Annet Sowerby, Annet Sowerby*, so she won't forget it." She was quiet for a moment. "There is nowhere to put it."

Fen did not say anything. She could not think of anything to say. That had happened to her perhaps four times in her life, and three of them had been in the last nine days.

So she reached across, the way the Cantor had once reached across to her, and took the scribe's ink-stained hand, and held it.

The scribe let her.

---

They brought the Registrar-General down at the third hour.

Fen had not known he was coming. Nobody on the Shelf had. Orrin had said, at the fire, reading the dockets, *and we've a prisoner. The Registrar-General. Pellam Quist. He ran it. All of it. The harvest-houses and the Hall and the forms. He's on the cutter.* And the Shelf had gone quiet. Then somebody, Fen thought it was Edwin, had said, *bring him down*. And somebody else had said it. And then all of them, round the fire, not loud, not angry, quite steady, the way you say the days of the week. *Bring him down. Bring him down.*

So they brought him down.

He came down the black glass in the basket in the dark, on Two-Eleven's yellow rope, with his hands tied in front of him and a trooper of the Watch in the basket with him. He climbed out onto the Shelf, very carefully, the way an old man climbs out of a cart. He stood there in the firelight in his good grey coat, without his spectacles, blinking.

He was small. That was the first thing Fen thought. She had thought, all her life, that the person who did it would be big. She had seen the man with the fork at Ebb Cross, and he had been big, and the clerk at the desk had been young and thin with ink on his fingers. But she had always thought that behind them, somewhere, there was somebody big. Somebody you could see across a room.

He was smaller than Orrin by half. Not short; slight, the way a bird is slight.

He looked round at the Shelf. At the fire, and the holes in the wall, and the lean-tos, and the three hundred and sixty-five people coming out of them into the firelight to look at him. At the fourteen at the far end, sitting in a row with their hands in their laps. At the new edge, a hundred and fifty yards nearer than it had been, clean and smooth as a cut loaf.

He looked at all of it with his pale tired kind eyes, and Fen watched his face. And she saw on it something she had not expected, and would not forget.

Interest.

"So this is the lip," said Pellam Quist. Softly. To himself. "I've written it so many times. I've never seen it." He looked at the fire. "Schedule F. One in nine. I always thought that was a guess."

---

They did not hurt him.

Fen thought afterward that it was the strangest thing she had ever seen, and she had seen a man walk across the quiet to fetch an old woman and lose a boot. Three hundred and sixty-five people who had been pressed half-way in a chair at a harvest-house. Who had been put in carts and tipped over the edge of the world with a docket pinned to their coats in this man's own form. Who had sat on a shelf of black rock for six years eating an old trooper's bread and watching each other go quiet one by one. And they stood round the fire in the dark at the bottom of the Rift and looked at him.

And nobody touched him.

Nobody shouted. Nobody threw anything. Nobody even said anything, for a long time. They just looked.

And then, one by one, they began to tell him.

Maud first. She came forward into the firelight and stood in front of him and said, quite steadily, "Maud Teller. Ebb Cross, spring '14. They took the word for mother. I say *the woman who had me*." And stepped back.

Then Corran. "Corran Dyce. Hollin Ford, '15. They took my reading. I can see the letters. They don't mean anything." And stepped back.

Then Edwin. Then Wenna Holt. Then Bridie, with the baby. "Bridie Cole. The Malting, '17. They took the knowing of my left hand. This is Halla. She's eleven days old." And stepped back.

One after another. All of them but Orrin. Three hundred and sixty-four. They came forward into the firelight and stood in front of the small neat man with his hands tied, and said their name, and where, and when, and what. Then they stepped back, and the next one came. It took two hours. Nobody sat down. Nobody stopped. The fire burned down and somebody built it up again. And Pellam Quist stood in the firelight in his good grey coat and listened to every one.

He did not look away. Fen watched him the whole time and he did not look away once. He looked at each of them as they spoke, with his pale tired eyes, and listened, with his head a little on one side. And after the first hour Fen understood what he looked like.

He looked like the sorter in the scribe's story. The woman at the long table, holding a core to her ear, listening to it say its name. Grading it.

---

Fen went last.

She had not meant to. She had meant to go somewhere in the middle, when nobody was looking. But she found when the line got short that she could not make herself go forward. She stood at the back with her hands in the sleeves of the old one's coat, and let everybody go ahead of her, until there was nobody left.

So she went last.

She came forward into the firelight and stood in front of him. She had to look up. Not far. He was small.

"Fen," she said. "Fenella Ashby, if you want it properly. Ebb Cross, the summer of '13. I was the fourth one ever. They took the growing." She looked at him. "I'm eighteen."

Pellam Quist looked down at her.

For the first time in two hours, something moved in his face. Not much. A small thing at the corner of the mouth.

"F-four," he said softly. "Ebb Cross. The trial pressings. The summer of '13." He looked at her for a long while. "I signed the authorisation. For the trials. It was my first year as Deputy. I didn't go down to watch. I read the report." He tilted his head. "*Subject four: female, approx. twelve years. Draw incomplete. Seed inadequate. Residual retained for observation, then routed to the lip.* I remember the line. *Retained for observation.* We wanted to see if the incomplete draws would recover." He looked at her, small, eighteen, twelve, in the old one's coat with the sleeves rolled back. "You didn't."

"No," said Fen. "I didn't."

"I'm sorry," said the Registrar-General of the Office of the Catalogue. And Fen, who had been listening to people say they were sorry all her life, heard that he meant it. He meant it exactly the way the sorter meant it when she chalked a core *P* and laid it in the tray for the mill. He was sorry the way you are sorry when a sum doesn't come out.

"Your core was in bin seven," he said. "Near the door. I'm afraid it would have been one of the first."

"I know," said Fen. "Orrin told me."

"Then you'll know I struck the bar."

"Yes."

He looked down at her with his pale kind eyes.

"What are you going to do with me?" said Pellam Quist.

---

Everybody looked at Orrin.

He was standing at the edge of the firelight, with his one boot and his bare foot. He had stood there for two hours while the Shelf went past, and had not gone forward himself, because the Registrar-General had already said his name and his tray in the cellar of the Hall of Carriage. Everybody looked at him because he was the one who walked the edge and kept the count and could do anything, because he was not afraid.

And Orrin looked at Fen.

She did not know why. She would think about it for some time afterward. Orrin Hale looked across the firelight, past three hundred and sixty-five people and the Registrar-General, at her. A girl of eighteen and twelve in an old trooper's coat. And she understood that he was not going to say anything. That he was waiting for her.

She looked back at Pellam Quist.

She thought about the bar. About a man sitting on a stool by a door all morning with a jeweller's hammer on his knee, waiting. She thought about two hundred and thirty-two people saying half their names in the dark and stopping in the middle. About *Fen—*. About *retained for observation*. About a man who had signed a paper in his first year as Deputy and not gone down to watch.

*I didn't go down to watch. I read the report.*

"You're going to watch," said Fen.

He looked at her.

"There's a press," said Fen. "On the ship. The scribe can run it backward. They're going to bring it down. And everybody whose piece came home is going to go through it, one at a time, and get it back. A hundred and thirty-one." She was talking fast again. She let herself. "You're going to watch every one. Every single one. You're going to stand there and watch, and listen, the way you listened tonight. And when it's done, you're going to know what it looks like. Not the report. *It.* You're going to see what it looks like when a person comes home." She stopped, and swallowed, and went on. "That's what we're going to do with you. That's all."

The Shelf was very quiet. Not stopped. Quiet.

Pellam Quist looked down at her for a long time.

Then he smiled. A small, soft, delighted smile, like a man who has been offered a seat at a lecture he has always wanted to hear.

"Thank you," said the Registrar-General. "I'd like that very much. I've always wanted to see one go back."

And Fen understood, standing there in the firelight with the whole Shelf behind her, that she had not punished him at all. She had given him exactly what he wanted. And that she would do it again, because it was right, and because there was nothing else on the Shelf that was.

---

The column was on the plain at dawn.

The word came down the rope with the morning basket, on a note in Two-Eleven's big slow round hand, under the bread. Not about the dog this time.

*Fires on the plain to the west. Lots. The General says Justiciary. The column for the writ. Three days, she says. Maybe four. She says the Watch holds the wall. She says to tell you she said so.*

*Also the General says the man with the glass arm can't stay on the Watch at dawn. Something about the Refusal. He's on the black cutter now, way off west, with the white-sided woman. The General says it's for the best.*

*I'm sorry about the boot. Orrin's boot. I'm sending one down. It was mine. I've big feet. It might fit.*

*Keep talking. — 211*

There was a boot in the basket. A big black army boot, lead-studded, old, with a hole in the toe. Orrin put it on his bare foot standing on the black rock at the foot of the rope. It fitted.

He looked down at it for a long while.

"I'd like to thank him," he said. "I'll write. I can do that. I don't need to feel anything to write a letter."

He wrote on the back of Two-Eleven's note, with a stub of the scribe's pencil, in a clerk's neat small hand. *Thank you for the boot. It fits. — O. Hale.* And put it in the basket for the evening. Fen watched him do it. She thought it was the saddest thing she had ever seen. She would have been glad if it had been the saddest for longer than a day.

---

They chose the order that night, at the fire.

A hundred and thirty-one names, in the tin, on buff cards. The scribe had said the press could do perhaps twenty in a day, with rest between, if nothing went wrong. Six days, perhaps seven. The writ fell on the third Nullday. Seven days. And the column three, or four.

So they drew lots. The cards in Orrin's tin, shaken, and drawn out one at a time by the scribe without looking. Read out, in her voice for entries, and laid in a row on the black rock by the fire, in order. First. Second. Third. Down the row. A hundred and thirty-one buff cards in a long line in the firelight, each one with a pinhole in the top, each one a person.

Bridie's was the tenth.

She looked at it, lying there in the row on the rock. The tenth card. *F-302. The Malting. Cole, B.* She laughed, a little, and bent and kissed the top of the baby's head.

"Tenth," she said. "First day, then. Halla, you hear that? First day. Your mummy'll have both hands by the first night."

Fen sat at the edge of the firelight and watched the scribe lay the last card at the end of the row. A hundred and thirty-one. Then the scribe sat back on her heels and looked at the line of cards. Fen looked at it too. There was no card in it that said *F-4*. There never would be.

"I want to go last," said Fen.

The scribe looked at her. So did Orrin. So did everybody round the fire.

"I know I haven't got one," said Fen. "I know there's nothing for me. I'm not daft. I want to go last anyway. After the hundred and thirty-first. I want to be the last one in the line." She pulled the old one's coat tighter round her. "I want to see them all go home. Every one. And then I want to be there at the end. So that someone is. So that when the last one comes out of the press, there's somebody standing there who's still waiting, and she can see somebody's still waiting, and know it's all right that she's the last." She stopped. "That's all. That's all I want."

The scribe looked at her for some time with her smooth face.

Then she took her pen out of her coat. She took a blank buff card from the bottom of Orrin's tin, one of the spares. She wrote on it, in her small clear hand, by the firelight. She laid it at the end of the row, after the hundred and thirty-first.

Fen leaned over to read it.

*Ashby, F. Last.*

"Yes," said the scribe.

Fen looked at the card at the end of the row. She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked up at the strip of sky over the Rift, three hundred feet above, where the stars were coming out over the lip of the world. And she began, very softly, to talk. To nobody. To the cards, and the fire, and Halla asleep at her mother's breast, and Orrin in his borrowed boot. About a dog called Rufus who could open a gate. Who let the sheep out every night for a month and in the end had to bring them all in again in the morning, every one, by himself.

She told it all the way to the end.

## CHAPTER TEN
### The Price

> *A name kept is a name paid for.*
>
> — The First Rule of the Silent Veil, written on the inside cover of every Ledger the order ever bound

*The Shelf, and the plain west of Null-Watch. Hearthday the fourth of Frostmonth, to Massday the fifth.*

---

She did the arithmetic again on Hearthday morning, by lantern-light, at the far end of the Shelf, among the fourteen. It came out differently. It came out worse.

The Concordance lay open across her knees. The fourteen sat round her on the black rock in the order they always sat, because a Hollow sits where it sat yesterday. Old Nan, twisting her hands in her lap. The tall man with grey hair who sat very straight, as if he were waiting to be called into a room. The young woman with the long plait who rocked, very slightly, forward and back. Eleven more. The last of two hundred residuals from the ward at the Malting, tipped over the lip on a single morning five weeks ago because a scribe had walked into a harvest-house and put one woman's name back.

She had found all fourteen in the Concordance. It had taken her the whole of the night. She had gone through the residual column page by page with her finger, looking for *the Malting, residual ward*, and then for the date the ward was closed, and then for the line in red ink in another hand beside each. *Ward closed. Residual routed Schedule F.* Two hundred lines. And then she had gone to the fourteen one by one with the lantern and looked at their faces, and their hands, and the little Office tattoos on the insides of their left wrists. Every residual had one, a number in blue ink, put there at the harvest-house so the body could be matched to the core. She had matched them. She had written each number in her own book with the name from the Concordance beside it.

Fourteen names. Fourteen cores. Pressed in the Whisper-Grove harvest of 416, and graded, and sent up the stair at the Hall of Carriage to Assignment, and assigned.

Eleven of the fourteen had been assigned to Legions. Carillon Legionnaires, the brass-skulled soldiers of the Arbiterium, who ran on a core behind their breastplates and sometimes said a name when they died. Eleven cores in eleven iron chests in eleven columns somewhere in Aethelgard. Or broken, if the Legionnaire had been. There was no way to know. The Concordance did not follow a core once it left the Hall.

Three of the fourteen had not been assigned to Legions. Three had been put in crates and carried. In the hold of a skyship called the *Drift-Kestrel*, by a captain whose file said *reliable, does not ask questions*. To be delivered to the Hall on the Hearthday after the Rite, and never delivered.

They were in the hold. Two hundred yards along the Shelf from where she sat, in the hold of the *Kestrel*, which had come down into the Rift the day before. It had been moored to iron pins driven into the black glass of the wall at the end of the Shelf, the first ship that had ever come down to it. Three cores in crates, saying their names in the dark.

Old Nan. *Agathe Morrow. WG-40,622. Hollin Ford.*

The young woman with the plait. *Ione Pask. WG-41,077. Hollin Ford.*

The tall man with grey hair who sat very straight. *Tobias Lennart. WG-40,902. Hollin Ford.*

She had stopped on that one for a long time.

*WG-40,902.* She knew the number. She did not need the Concordance to tell her where she had seen it. She had written it in the Ledger herself, nineteen days ago, on the morning after the Hanging Water, in a list of thirty-six, every one with the same word beside it.

*Broken.*

The aft crate on the starboard side. Forty thousand eight hundred and eighty to forty thousand nine hundred and fifteen. A twelve-pound ball going through the hold at the speed of a thing that never stops, and thirty-six people saying their names, stopping in the middle.

Tobias Lennart sat on the black rock beside her, very straight, waiting to be called into a room. His name had been in the aft crate on the starboard side. It was lilac dust on the floor of the *Kestrel*'s hold, swept out over the side somewhere east of the Valley of Velocity nineteen days ago. And he would sit here, waiting to be called, until he went into the quiet with the others. Nobody would ever call him.

She made a mark in her book beside his name. Not *broken*. She had written *broken* thirty-six times and she found she could not write it again. She made a small square mark, the kind a clerk makes for a closed account, and moved her finger to the next line.

Two, then. Two names in the hold that had bodies on the Shelf. Agathe Morrow and Ione Pask.

And Davey Pruett, in Orrin Hale's coat, whose body sat on the deck of the cutter with a grey woman beside it talking about chickens.

Three.

And she had one.

---

She knew what the one was. It was the last thing she had of herself that she could identify. The smell of the Veil's scriptorium on a winter morning, before the stoves were lit. Cold stone and ink and the dust off the vellum, and very faintly, somebody's breakfast coming up through the floor from the kitchens.

She could feel it. That was the thing she had not told anyone. She could feel it the way you feel the last coin in a purse, the weight of it in the corner. She had felt it since the morning in the anchor-house at Sol-Luminary, when she paid her mother's hands for Wren. It was all that was left of her. Everything else was furniture. Things she remembered the way you remember a book you have read. Things that had happened to somebody, and she had the record of them.

And since the cellar at the Hall of Carriage, she had felt it going thin.

Not gone. Not spent. *Thin.* Like a coin worn down by thumbs. Pellam Quist had put her name to his lips, the copy, the little lilac core from the third tray, and said it. And everything she was had stopped, the way a clock stops. When he went down under Orrin's knee and stopped speaking, it had started again. But not all of it. Something had not come back. She did not know what. She only knew the scriptorium was thinner than it had been, and her face felt strange from the inside, as if it belonged to someone she had met once and could not place.

She had seen Lyra look at her, on the *Kestrel*'s deck at the edge of the Woods, when she came over the rail. She had seen Fen look at her. She knew what they had seen.

*Smooth.*

If she spent the scriptorium, she would be what the fourteen were. She knew that as surely as she knew her own hand. She would sit on the black rock at the end of the Shelf with her hands in her lap, and someone would enter her in somebody's book by the only thing she had left. *The scribe who sat with the Ledger closed.*

And two people would have their names back. One person. Agathe Morrow, or Ione Pask, or Davey Pruett. One.

She closed the Concordance on her knees.

She sat among the fourteen at the end of the Shelf in the lantern-light, and could not make it come out.

---

Tessa Wray found her there at noon.

She came along the Shelf with her hands moving, braiding something too fine to see. She sat down on the rock without being asked, the way she had the first time, and looked at the fourteen, and then at Sable.

"You've been here all night," she said.

"Yes."

"Orrin says you found them. In the book."

"I found them." Sable did not open the Concordance. "Eleven are in Legions. One was in the crate that broke over the Valley. Two are in the hold." She looked at old Nan, twisting her hands. "And Davey Pruett is in Orrin's coat."

Tessa looked at old Nan too.

"And you've got one left," she said.

"Yes."

Tessa was quiet for a while. Her hands moved. Then she said, not looking at Sable, looking at the fourteen:

"I didn't tell you all of it. The first time. About Annis."

Sable waited.

"When I called myself home," said Tessa Wray. "Out of the cracked core. Annis came too. You know that. She was the seed. She came home with me into my head because she was set in the middle of me, and when I came she came." Her hands stopped. "But what I didn't tell you is that she *stayed*. For nine days. In my head. And she was *her*. She wasn't me. She wasn't a bit of me that had gone strange. She was a fisherman's wife from the Cape called Annis, with her own name and her own husband and her own voice, asking about the tide. She had a whole person's worth of herself, and she was living in my head. In my rooms. And I let her."

She looked at Sable.

"I didn't know I was letting her. I didn't choose it. But I was. I was being a house for her. While she was there." Her hands began again, slowly. "And it cost. That's the thing. It cost me. Not the screaming. The screaming was something else. After she went quiet, on the tenth day, I found that I'd lost something. A small thing. My grandmother's garden. I used to be able to see it. Every bed. The beans and the marigolds and the bench by the wall. I can't any more. I know it was there. I know there were beans. I can't see it." She looked at the fourteen. "I think Annis took it with her. When she went. I think that was the rent."

Sable looked at her.

She looked at her for a long while. She was doing something she had not done in three years. Thinking about the price as a thing she did not understand. Not as a thing she paid.

"The Office never put one back," she said slowly. "Not once. In all the years. So nobody knew. When I put Bettis Renn back at the Malting, I paid, and I thought it was me. I thought it was the scribe. The scribe's price. Because I was the one standing there with the press and the name. And for Wren I paid again, and I thought, *it's me, it's the scribe*. It's in the First Rule. *A name kept is a name paid for.* I thought it meant *by the keeper*."

"Maybe it doesn't," said Tessa Wray.

"A Hollow is empty," said Sable. She was not talking to Tessa now. She was talking to herself, the way she did when she worked a problem in the Ledger. "Nobody home. That's why a Hollow can't call its own name back, the way you did, the way the Rendered can. There's nobody in the house to call it. So somebody has to stand in the door. Hold it open. Be the house, for a moment, while the name finds its way in." She stopped. "And standing in the door costs. The house pays rent. An equal weight. I have always been the one standing in the door. I have always paid. Because I was there. Because I had the name in the Ledger and I said it."

She looked at Tessa.

"It doesn't have to be me," she said.

Tessa Wray's hands had stopped.

"It doesn't have to be the scribe," said Sable. "It has to be someone. Anyone with a house. Anyone who will stand in the door and hold it while the name goes in, and pay the rent when it's in." She looked down at the Concordance. "I've been doing it alone because I didn't know anyone else could. Nobody knew. The Office never put one back."

She sat there for a long moment with the knowledge settling into her, cold and clear and terrible. Bettis Renn. Wren Halloway. Her own true name and her mother's hands. She had paid them because she had not known she did not have to.

"I could have asked," she said. Very quietly. "I could have asked anyone."

"You didn't know," said Tessa.

"I could have asked."

---

She told them at the fire that night, on the Shelf, Hearthday. She told it the way she told everything, in the voice she used for entries. A Hollow could be returned by the press. The name went back through the fork into the body. But someone had to stand in the door, and the one who stood there paid. Not a thing they chose. A thing of equal weight to the name returned, taken from them, and they would not know what until it was gone. She had always been the one. She did not have to be.

She did not ask anyone. She would not. She laid it out the way she would lay out a schedule. Three names, two in the hold and one in Orrin Hale's coat, and three bodies on the Shelf and the cutter. The price, unknown, permanent. And then she closed the Ledger and sat back.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Nineteen stood up.

She was the grey woman of fifty with the scar through her eyebrow, who had been talking to Davey Pruett about chickens for eight days. She had come down in the basket that afternoon with Davey beside her, holding his elbow, because she would not leave him on the cutter. He was sitting by the fire now with his big hands in his lap and his eyes on nothing.

"I'll do Davey," said Nineteen.

Sable looked at her.

"You heard what it costs," she said. "You don't know what it'll take. It might be anything. It might be your—"

"I heard," said Nineteen. "I've been talking to the lad for eight days about his mother's chickens. I know more about those bloody chickens than I know about my own family. The brown one that got in the house. The speckled one that laid in the coal-scuttle. The cock that went for the priest." She looked at the boy by the fire. "I know what he's got to come back to. I'll stand in the door. Whatever it takes. I've got plenty."

She sat down again, next to Davey, and took his elbow.

Sable looked at old Nan, sitting at the end of the Shelf beyond the firelight with the other Hollows, twisting her hands.

"Agathe Morrow," she said. "WG-40,622."

And from the edge of the firelight, where he always stood, with his one borrowed boot and his one bare foot, Orrin Hale said:

"I'll do that one. I carried her across the quiet. She's mine, rather." He said it pleasantly, the way he said everything. "And I won't mind what it takes. That's the advantage of me. I'll know it's gone. I just won't mind."

Nobody at the fire could look at him.

"And Ione Pask?" said Sable. "WG-41,077."

There was a silence. Not a long one. Three hundred and sixty-four people looked at the fire, and at each other. Sable understood it before anyone spoke. Every one of them had half of themselves on a buff card in Orrin's tin, waiting for the press. It would run on Stasisday, at the light, Vane said, when the ship was made fast and the plank rigged and the water lowered; not before. Or in lilac dust on the floor of bin seven. Every one of them had already paid once, in a chair at a harvest-house, without being asked. They were not refusing. They were counting what they had.

"After the first day of the press," said Sable. "We'll see what it costs. Then we'll know."

Nobody argued.

---

She went up to the cutter on Massday morning, because Kaelen had sent for her.

The note came down the rope in the morning basket, under the bread, beside Two-Eleven's page about his mother. It was in Kaelen's engineer's hand, square and careful, block capitals. The letters were quite steady. That was the first thing she noticed. They had leaned, the last time. On the note at the Watch, a fortnight ago, *AT THE EDGE HE CAN'T HEAR*, the pencil had gone through the paper in two places. These did not lean at all.

*SABLE. A LETTER CAME. FROM HIM. A RIDER BROUGHT IT TO THE CUTTER AT DAWN AND WAITED FOR AN ANSWER. I'D LIKE YOU TO READ IT. YOU'LL WANT IT FOR THE LEDGER. — K.*

She went up in the basket. Two-Eleven was at the wheel, talking about his mother. He looked at her face as she climbed out on the buttress and stopped talking for a moment, and then went on.

The *Equity* was moored on the open plain four miles west of the Watch, in the voiceless wind, on a long tether to an iron stake. Far enough that the thing under the Maw, turning over at the Refusal, would not feel the glass on the deck. Close enough that a rider could reach it in an hour. One had.

He was still there when Sable came across the plain on the Watch's borrowed horse. A young man in Arbiterium grey on a good bay, sitting a little way off from the cutter's tether with his hands folded on his saddle-bow. He was waiting with the patience of a man who has been told to wait and will do it until he is told to stop. He watched her ride past. He did not speak.

Kaelen was on the cutter's foredeck. Vesper was beside him with the quiet round them both, sitting on the deck against the deckhouse with her coat drawn tight and her arms folded across it. Something in the coat was very still.

Sable climbed the ladder and stood in front of him.

He looked up at her. His face was calm. She had heard about it from Lyra, on her slate, at the edge of the Woods. She had not believed it. She believed it now. She had known Kaelen Vance for three months, and in all that time his face had been grey and set and drawn tight round something, like a fist round a coin. It was not drawn round anything now. It was open. Easy. He looked up at her the way a man looks up from a book in a warm room.

"Sable," he said. His voice came out ringing. Three notes now, she heard. The Light, and the drowned bell, and something under both of them, very faint, green, like wind in reeds. "Thank you for coming."

He held out a letter.

---

Heavy cream paper. Folded in three. A seal of plain grey wax, broken, with no device on it at all. She had seen that seal once before, on a letter on Pellam Quist's desk, and had not known whose it was. She knew now.

The hand inside was small and old and very clear, the hand of a man who had been writing letters for a hundred and twenty years and had long ago stopped wasting ink.

*Mr Vance,*

*I hope you will forgive my writing. I could, as you know, simply speak to you, and I have once or twice. But I have found that people are kinder to a letter than to a voice in their bones. And I should like you to be able to put this down, and come back to it, and burn it if you choose, which you cannot do with me.*

*I was very sorry to hear about Mrs Bram. I heard it happen. I want you to know that I did not send the cutters to the Woods. The Registrar-General did, on what he had heard from me. I tell the people who work for me what they need to know, and it is not always kind, and I am not always in a position to choose what they do with it. That is not an excuse. It is only how it is.*

*I should like to talk to you. Not to make you an offer, though I will, if you like. Only to talk. You are the only other person alive who has heard what I have heard, and who might, I think, be willing to talk to me about it. You heard the Light ask its question on the three hundredth morning, and you heard it let go. I heard it too. I have heard all eight of them for eighty years. And I have never once in all that time been able to talk to anyone about it who knew what I meant.*

*You will come when you come. I will not send for you. The door at the Organum will be open, and nobody will stop you on the stair.*

*In the meantime, I should like, if your friends will allow it, to visit the Shelf. I have never heard it, you understand. Your glass has never been near it, and your friends have been careful. But I have heard it talked about, on and off, for a fortnight, by everyone who came within thirty feet of you. The troopers on the wall talk of nothing else, in among their onions. The captain told you about the knots, on the wall, on the night you came. And on the night of the buttress I heard shooting, a long way off, and afterward I heard the captain tell the woman with the white side, on the deck of a cutter, a few yards above your head, who it was that had died. I find that I should like to see the place, and the people, with my own eyes, before my son's writ falls on it. I will come alone. On Dawnday, I think, if the weather holds. I am a hundred and forty, and I do not travel quickly.*

*With great respect, and some affection, which I hope you will not hold against me,*

*C. Vaelen*

---

She read it twice. Then a third time, the way she read everything that mattered.

She folded it along its creases and held it in her hands and looked out over the cutter's rail. The plain went east toward the black line of the Watch and the not-colour beyond, which you could not look at.

"Dawnday," she said.

"Dawnday," said Kaelen. "Two days."

"The writ falls on Nullday."

"Yes."

"He's coming to look at it. Before it falls." She heard her own voice. It was quite even. She did not know whether that was the scribe's voice or the other thing, the smoothness, and she found she could not tell any more. "He wants to see what he's going to do. Before he does it. *With his own eyes.*"

"I don't think it's that," said Kaelen.

She looked at him.

"I think he wants to see them," he said. "I think he means it. I think he's listened to people talk about them through my arm, on and off, for a fortnight. And he's an honest man, and he can't make it come out, and he wants to look at them." He was quite calm. "I think he's coming to see if he's wrong."

"And if he is?"

"Then he'll say so," said Kaelen. "And go home. And do it anyway." He looked out at the plain. "He's not a cruel man, Sable. That's what nobody understands. I've had him in my bones for seven years. He isn't cruel. He's tired, and he's been listening to tired things for eighty years, and he's decided they want to stop. And when he hears something that doesn't fit, he doesn't stop listening. He goes and looks." He was quiet for a moment. "That's worse. I know it's worse. I used to know it."

---

She looked at Vesper.

Vesper was sitting against the deckhouse with her arms folded across her coat, looking at Kaelen. Her violet eyes were very steady and very cold, and there was something in her face that Sable had seen once before, on the Mole at Port Cantabile, on the night Vane went in after her.

"The rider's waiting," said Vesper. Low. "For an answer."

"I know," said Kaelen.

"What are you going to tell him?"

Kaelen looked at the letter in Sable's hands. Then out over the rail at the young man on the bay horse, waiting with his hands folded on his saddle-bow.

"I'm going to tell him," said Kaelen, "that I'll come to the Organum. After the press. When the Shelf's done." He said it quite simply, the way a man says he will be home for supper. "I'd like to talk to him too. I find I would. I've had him listening in my bones for seven years and I've never said one word back. I'd like to." He looked at his own right hand, lying in his lap. "I'm not afraid of him any more. I used to be so afraid of him. I'd lie awake. I don't any more."

Sable looked at him.

She thought of a thing in Vesper's coat that found whatever hurt worst in a person and ate it, gently, believing it had been asked. She thought of what Lyra had written on her slate at the edge of the Woods, in letters that shook so badly she could barely read them. *IT ATE HIS FEAR. HE ISN'T AFRAID OF THE THROAT. HE ISN'T AFRAID OF ANYTHING.* She thought of Orrin Hale walking along the lip of the Rift with his boots half over the drop. *Fear isn't knowing. It's the thing that makes knowing matter.*

"Kaelen," she said. "He wants a successor."

"I know."

"He's a hundred and forty. His glass is laced and he's never let it set. He's never let it carry a note. And yours is set and singing and it's in your throat." She held the letter so tightly that the cream paper creased. "He doesn't want to talk to you. He wants you to take his chair."

"I know," said Kaelen. Quite calmly. "I worked that out at the Watch. On the wall. Talking the Sequence." He looked at her with his clear easy eyes. "It doesn't frighten me, Sable. I know it should. I keep looking for it. It's like putting your tongue where a tooth was." He shook his head, slightly, as if puzzled at himself. "I'm sorry. I know what it must look like."

Vesper stood up.

She did it suddenly, which Vesper never did. She stood up off the deck with her arms still folded tight across her coat, and the quiet went with her. Sable saw it go off Kaelen like a blanket pulled away, and saw his hand go, out of long habit, to the back of his neck. Then come down again. As if he had remembered there was nothing there he minded.

Vesper stood at the rail with her back to them both and looked out over the plain at the black line of the Watch.

"I'll be in the deckhouse," she said. To nobody. Her voice was low and dry and broken, the hum of five years without air. "With it. Where it can't get at anybody." She did not turn round. "I should have thrown it in the Rift at the Watch. On the first night. When it leaned. I should have opened my coat at the edge and let it go home."

"Vesper," said Kaelen gently.

"*Don't*," said Vesper. "Don't be kind about it. You're only kind about it because it took the thing that would have made you angry." She turned her head, and Sable saw her face, and looked away. "I'll be in the deckhouse."

She went in, and shut the door.

---

The rider took the answer back at noon. Kaelen wrote it himself, on the back of one of Pennick's tank-charts, in his square steady engineer's capitals. Sable watched him write it and did not stop him. She did not know how she could have.

*AFTER THE PRESS. I'LL COME. — K. VANCE.*

He folded it and gave it to her to take down to the rider. She took it. She walked across the plain to the young man on the bay horse and held it up to him, and he took it from her and put it in his coat without looking at it. He bowed from the saddle, very correctly. Then he turned his horse and rode away west across the plain toward the Organum, at a steady trot, without hurry.

She stood in the voiceless wind and watched him go until he was a speck.

Then she opened the Ledger and wrote it down. All of it. The letter, word for word, from memory. The answer. The rider. She wrote it in her small clear hand, in the voice the Veil had taught her, the voice in which things are recorded and nothing is done.

And at the bottom of the entry she found that she had written, without meaning to, in the same small hand, a line that was not in the voice of the Veil at all.

*He was not afraid. I was. I think I am the only one of us left who still is, and I do not know how much longer I will be able to be.*

She looked at it for some time.

She did not cross it out.

---

They did Davey Pruett on the *Kestrel*'s foredeck that night, at the second hour, by lantern-light, with the ship moored to iron pins in the black glass of the Rift wall at the end of the Shelf.

The press stood where it had always stood, bolted through the deck forward of the mast, under its tarpaulin. Sable took the tarpaulin off. It was a Catalogue machine, Office property, taken from the harvest-house at the Malting in Harvestmonth. A chair of black oak with brass fittings, and a frame over it like the frame of a printing-press. A long brass fork on a pivoted arm, tuned to seven hundred and forty-one. And a little brass cup at the end of the arm where the glass went, to be filled.

She had designed the schedule that kept a hundred of them busy. She had stood in the doorway at the Malting and watched one used. She had run this one backward twice: for Bettis Renn, and in her head, in the anchor-house, for Wren. She knew it the way a clerk knows a form.

Davey Pruett sat in the chair. Nineteen had walked him to it and sat him down and put his big hands on the arms. He sat there with his face smooth and his eyes on nothing, a boy of nineteen with a big nose.

Sable took the little core from Orrin Hale's hand, the one Orrin had carried in his coat since the cellar. New and lilac and faintly lit. Saying, very clearly, in a voice the size of a thumbnail, *Davey Pruett, Davey Pruett*. She put it in the brass cup at the end of the arm.

"Stand by him," she said to Nineteen. "Take his hand. Not the arm of the chair. His hand."

Nineteen took his hand.

"When I strike the fork, it'll go back. The way it came out, but backward. Through the fork, into the back of his neck. And it'll look for a house. There isn't one. So it'll find you, because you're holding on, and you'll have to—" She stopped. She did not know how to say it. She had done it twice and she did not know how to say it. "You'll have to let it in. Let it through you. Like holding a door. And when it's through, it'll take the rent."

"How will I know?" said Nineteen.

"You'll know," said Sable.

She swung the arm down. She set the fork against the back of Davey Pruett's neck, under the skull, where it went in.

She struck it.

---

Seven hundred and forty-one. The note of the Memory Anchor. The note Tessa Wray had said was like someone you love calling you from the next room. It rang out over the deck of the *Kestrel* at the bottom of the Rift in the lantern-light, high and pure and lovely. And in the brass cup at the end of the arm, the little core began to go dim.

She watched it go. She had watched it twice before and it was no easier. The faint lilac light in the glass went down and down, like a lamp being turned down. And as it went, the voice in it, *Davey Pruett, Davey Pruett*, went down too. Fainter. And fainter. Going somewhere.

Nineteen made a sound.

Sable looked at her. The grey woman was standing beside the chair with the boy's big hand in both of hers, and her face had gone white, and her eyes were wide and fixed on nothing. Her mouth was open. She was not breathing.

Then she was. A great shuddering breath, in, as if she had come up from under water.

"*Oh*," said Nineteen. "*Oh, there's — there's somebody — there's chickens. There's a yard. There's a yard with a gate and chickens and a woman in an apron and she's — oh, he's in me, he's going through me, he's—*"

The core in the cup went dark.

The fork stopped ringing.

And Davey Pruett, sitting in the black oak chair on the foredeck of the *Kestrel*, blinked.

---

He blinked again. He looked down at his own hand, in Nineteen's two hands. He looked up at her face, grey and white and wet. He looked round at the deck, and the lanterns, and the black glass wall of the Rift going up into the dark beside the ship. At the faces round him. Sable, with the arm of the press in her hand. Orrin, in his borrowed boot. Lyra, by the mast, with Halla asleep in a sling against her chest because Bridie was sleeping. Fen.

His mouth opened.

"The brown one," said Davey Pruett. His voice was hoarse, cracked, the voice of a boy who had not spoken in eight days. "The brown one got in the house again. Mum's going to kill her. She's going to wring her neck this time, she said, she said last time *one more*, and now she's — she's on the dresser, Nineteen, she's on the dresser in the good room on Mum's—" He stopped. He looked at Nineteen's face. "Why are you crying?"

Nineteen could not answer him.

She put her face down on his big hand in hers and wept. She did not stop. Davey Pruett sat in the chair and looked at her, bewildered, and then round at the deck. And then, slowly, Sable saw it come into his face. Where he was. What had happened. The cellar, and the dark, and the fork at the back of his neck.

"I was in a room," said Davey Pruett. Slowly. "With no doors. For days. And I could hear her." He looked down at Nineteen. "I could hear her the whole time. Talking about the chickens. I could hear every word. I couldn't answer." His big hand closed round hers. "I couldn't answer. I tried. I couldn't find my mouth."

*The Hollow was awake the whole time.* Sable had learned it at the Malting. She had written it in the Ledger. She had told nobody, because there had been nobody to tell who could bear it.

"You can now," said Orrin Hale. Pleasantly. From the edge of the lantern-light. "Answer. You can now, Davey."

Davey Pruett looked at Nineteen weeping on his hand.

"The speckled one," he said. Hoarse. "The speckled one laid in the coal-scuttle. Did I tell you? Did I tell you about the speckled one?"

"*Yes*," said Nineteen into his hand. "*Yes, you bloody did, nine times.*"

And Davey Pruett, who had stolen a horse, and talked all the way from the edge of the world to the Organum about his mother's chickens, and been a room with no doors for eight days, began to laugh.

---

Nineteen found out what it had cost her an hour later. She was sitting on the deck by the mast with Davey asleep against her shoulder, and Fen beside her asking questions, the way Fen did.

"Where are you from?" said Fen. "Before the Watch. Where were you born?"

"I was born in—" said Nineteen.

She stopped.

Sable, at the press with the tarpaulin in her hands, looked up.

"I was born in—" said Nineteen again. She frowned. "It's a village. In the Reach. On a river. There was a mill, and a bridge, and my father had the forge by the bridge, and it was called—" She stopped again. Her face went very still.

They all waited.

"It's gone," said Nineteen, after a while. Quite steadily. "The name. Of the village. I know I was born there. I can see the bridge. I can see the forge. I can see my father at the anvil. I can't remember what it was called." She looked down at Davey asleep against her shoulder, his big nose, his open mouth. "That's what it took. The name of the place I come from."

Nobody said anything.

"Doesn't matter," said Nineteen. She put her hand on Davey's head. "It burned down anyway. In the Greening. There's nothing there now." She was quiet for a moment. "I'd have liked to know what it was called, though."

Fen took her other hand and held it.

---

They did Agathe Morrow at the fourth hour.

Orrin Hale stood beside the black oak chair with the old woman's twisting hands in his big ones, quite still, and Sable struck the fork. The core in the cup went dim. Orrin's face did not change at all as it went through him. Not once. He stood there like a man waiting for a kettle to boil, and the core went dark, and old Nan stopped twisting her hands.

She looked up at him.

"It was you," said Agathe Morrow. Her voice was very old and very thin. "Wasn't it. Across the dark. When the ground went soft. You picked me up." Her hands lay quite still in his. "I couldn't say anything. I couldn't say thank you. I was a room with no doors and you came in and picked me up and carried me out of it, and I couldn't say anything." She looked at him. "Thank you."

"You're welcome," said Orrin Hale.

And Sable, who had watched his face for three weeks, saw nothing in it at all. Not pleasure. Not relief. Nothing. He stood holding the old woman's hands and said *you're welcome* as if she had thanked him for passing the salt.

Afterward, when Agathe Morrow had been taken below to Bettany and the tarpaulin was back on the press, Sable asked him.

"What did it take?"

Orrin thought about it. He always thought about things properly.

"My mother," he said.

Sable went still.

"Her face," said Orrin Hale. "I can't see it. I could, before. She had a mole by her mouth, here, and grey eyes, and she used to sing while she ironed. I know all that. I've got the facts of it. I can't see her face." He looked at the dark wall of the Rift going up beside the ship. "I know I had a mother. I know she had a face. It's like knowing the capital of a country I've never been to."

"Orrin," said Sable. "I'm—"

"Don't," said Orrin pleasantly. "Please. I don't mind. That's the thing. I can feel that I ought to. I can feel the shape of where it would go, if I had it. The minding." He looked at her. "It was in a velvet tray on the third floor of the Hall of Carriage. It's in your coat now, I think. I felt it in the cutter, when you sat by me. It's cold, and it wants to get away. I'd know it anywhere."

Sable put her hand to her coat. Over the inside pocket, against her breast, where she had carried it since the third floor without telling anyone. A little cracked grey core, cold, dull, unlit, with something in it that wanted very badly to get away.

"Yes," she said.

"Then I'd like you to keep it," said Orrin Hale. "For now. Until after." He looked at the line of cards that would be laid out by the press at dawn, in the order they had been drawn. A hundred and thirty-one. "Somebody has to walk the edge tomorrow. While the press runs. Somebody has to watch for the quiet. And I'm the only one who can do it without wanting to go over." He was quiet for a moment. "After, I'll think about it. I'll think about whether I want it. It's an odd thing, to have to think about whether you want to be afraid."

He went away along the deck toward the ladder, to go down to the Shelf and walk the edge.

Sable stood by the press with her hand on her coat, over the little cold core. She did not move for a long time.

---

She wrote it all down at the fifth hour, sitting on the deck against the press, by the last of the lantern.

*Davey Pruett. Of Coldharrow, in the Peaks. Returned. The door held by Nineteen, of the Watch. The rent: the name of the village of her birth.*

*Agathe Morrow. Hollin Ford, 416. Returned. The door held by Orrin Hale. The rent: his mother's face.*

And under it, at the bottom of the page, in the same small hand, because it was true and she was a scribe:

*A name may be returned by anyone who will stand in the door. The scribe is not required. The scribe was never required. I did not know. Nobody knew, because the Office never put one back.*

*I paid twice because I did not know I could ask.*

*I have one left. I will keep it. Not for myself. Because tomorrow there is a press, and a hundred and thirty-one, and someone will have to run it who still remembers what it is for.*

She closed the Ledger.

Across the deck, Lyra was sitting by the mast with Halla asleep against her chest, looking at her. The Cantor's face in the last of the lantern-light was very tired, and her chin was still stained with old blood, and her burned hand was bound in a clean strip of linen. Her eyes were the eyes Sable had known for three months. Not smooth. Not calm. Afraid, and awake, and *there*.

Lyra did not have the slate. She lifted two fingers of her good hand.

*Here.*

Sable looked at the sign. She knew what it meant. She had learned it by writing it down. And she found, sitting on the deck of the *Kestrel* at the bottom of the Rift with one piece of herself left and her face going smooth, that the sign meant something to her it had not meant before. She could not have said what. A door, perhaps. Somebody standing in it.

She made it back. Badly. With ink-stained fingers.

Then she put her head back against the press and closed her eyes. For the first time since the cellar at the Hall of Carriage, she slept.

## CHAPTER ELEVEN
### The Tenth

> *What works is iron and cold water and about four people who do not panic.*
>
> — What the Rust Coast knows about quiets

*The Shelf. Stasisday the sixth of Frostmonth.*

---

They began before the light, because the light was when the guns fired.

Lyra stood on the *Kestrel*'s foredeck in the dark with her burned hand bound. The press was in front of her with its tarpaulin off. The black oak chair. The brass frame. The long fork on its pivoted arm, and the little cup at the end of it where the glass went. Behind her the ship lay moored to iron pins in the black glass of the Rift wall, at the end of the Shelf, with a plank run out from her rail to the rock. Beyond the plank, along the Shelf in the dark, by the light of a dozen lanterns, the line.

A hundred and thirty-one people. In the order of the cards. Standing on the black rock in the cold, in the lee of the batteries three hundred feet above, waiting. They were talking. Of course they were talking. Names and what was gone, very low, all along the line, like the sea under a cliff.

And at the very end of the line, past the hundred and thirty-first, at the far end of the Shelf where the new edge was, a small figure in an old trooper's coat with the sleeves rolled back four times. Holding a baby.

Bridie had asked her to. *Fen, will you have her? Just for the first bit? I don't want her near the — I don't want her near it. When I'm in the chair. I want her to be with someone who's still waiting.* And Fen had taken Halla in both arms, very carefully, the way you take something that is not yours. She had carried her to the end of the line and stood there, holding her, talking to her very softly about a dog.

Lyra could see them from the deck, by the last lantern. A small figure and a smaller one, at the end of everything.

---

Sable stood at the press. Her face was smooth in the lantern-light, and her hands, when she set them on the brass arm, were quite steady.

Pellam Quist sat on a crate beside the press with his hands tied in front of him and his good grey coat buttoned to the neck against the cold. He was where Fen had put him. Close enough to see every face in the chair. Close enough to hear every word. He sat with his back very straight and his pale tired eyes on the chair, and he had not said anything since he was brought aboard. He was watching. That was all. That was his sentence, and he was serving it with an expression of the deepest attention, like a candidate at an examination.

Lyra did not look at him. She could not.

Vane was at the rail by the plank, with his ribs strapped, handing people up. Mags was at the windlass behind the press with a boat-hook across her knees, as she had insisted. *In case.* Bettany at the hatch, with her bag. Tessa Wray on the Shelf at the head of the line, by the plank, saying each name as it went up, so that the person going up would hear it said by someone who knew what it was to come home.

And Orrin Hale walking the edge.

Lyra could see his lantern from the deck. A small gold point moving slowly along the new edge of the Shelf, past the end of the line, past Fen and the baby, and back. Along and back. Looking down. He was watching for the quiet. He was the only one who could do it without wanting to go.

Sable looked at the first card.

"Nessa Lark," she said. "F-three-hundred-and-forty. The Malting, '18."

Tessa, at the foot of the plank, said, "Nessa." And Nessa came up.

---

Lyra would remember the nine as a single thing afterward, the way you remember a long night of labour as a single thing. One birth, though there were nine.

It was not like Davey Pruett. Davey had been empty. A room with no doors. The name had gone back into him through someone else, through Nineteen standing in the door. The Rendered were not empty. They were half. There was somebody home. The press did not have to find a house for the piece; the house was there, and had been waiting six years with the door open. The fork only carried it across.

Sable put the cracked core in the brass cup. Nessa Lark sat in the chair. Sable set the fork to the back of Nessa's neck, under the skull, and said, "Call it."

And Nessa called.

Lyra heard her do it. Not out loud. Something under out loud, a shape in the breath. Lyra knew breath. She had spent her life reading it in birthing rooms and fever-rooms, and she heard Nessa Lark reach into herself for the shape of the hole where the word had been. And call into the hole. The way Tessa had said. The way you call a dog by whistling when you have forgotten its name.

Sable struck the fork.

Seven hundred and forty-one, high and pure in the dark. The cracked core in the cup went dim. Not slowly, the way Davey's had gone, a lamp being turned down. Quickly. The way a coal goes dark when you drop it in water. Gone into Nessa Lark in the time it takes to draw breath.

Nessa sat in the chair with her hands on the arms.

Her face did a thing Lyra had seen a thousand times, in a thousand rooms. The thing a mother's face does in the moment after, when the child is out and on her breast and she looks down at it for the first time. Recognition. Not joy, yet. Joy comes after. Recognition. *Oh. It's you.*

"Mother," said Nessa Lark.

She said it to nobody. To the dark over the Rift. She said it as if she were tasting it.

"*Mother.*" Again. Louder. And then her face broke, all at once, the way faces break. She put both hands over it in the black oak chair and said it into her palms, over and over, weeping, the word she had not had for a year. *Mother, mother, mother.* The woman who had had her. The woman who had sung to her and slapped her and died in the spring of '12 of a fever in a house by a river. The woman Nessa had been calling *the woman who had me* for a year because there was no other word.

Lyra knelt by the chair and put her good hand on Nessa's back and breathed beside her, slow and steady, until the weeping eased.

On his crate beside the press, Pellam Quist leaned forward very slightly. His lips moved. Lyra did not hear what he said. She did not want to.

---

Edwin Rook, who had lost the colour blue. He came out of the press and looked round wildly at the dark and the grey and the black glass, and there was no blue anywhere. Not in the Rift, not in the sky, not in anything. He began to shake. Then Lyra took his arm and walked him down the plank and along the Shelf to the end of the line, where Fen stood, and Fen held the baby up for him. Edwin Rook looked down into the baby's eyes in the lantern-light, which were the deep slate-blue of all new eyes. He sat down on the deck very suddenly and did not get up for a long time.

Wenna Holt, who had lost the sound of rain. She came out of the press and wept because it was not raining. Then it began to rain, very lightly, out of the grey over the Rift. She stood on the deck with her face turned up to it and her eyes shut, laughing.

Dunstan Marrow, who had lost the morning his son was born. He came out of the press and sat in the chair very still. Then he said, quite quietly, "He had black hair. All over. Like a little monkey. The midwife laughed." He put his face in his hands.

A woman whose name Lyra did not catch, who had lost the ability to recognise her own handwriting. She came out of the press and took a letter out of her coat that she had carried for four years. She read it standing at the rail in the lantern-light, and began to cry, because it was a letter she had written to her sister on the morning of the harvest. She had carried it for four years thinking it was from a stranger.

Two more. Three. Lyra held hands and breathed beside them and wiped faces. Some of them screamed when the piece came home, a short sharp sound. They remembered the chair, Tessa said, the fork at the back of the neck. The first time had been the going out, and the coming back was the same road, and some people's bodies remembered the road before their minds did. They screamed, and then stopped, and looked round, and laughed. Or wept. Or sat very still.

Nine.

The grey began to come up over the lip of the Rift, three hundred feet above. Faint. The first edge of the light. And from the wall, very faint, coming down the black glass, the chant began. *Rootday, Hearthday, Massday.* Forty batteries. Forty crews at the bellows.

Sable looked at the tenth card.

"Bridie Cole," she said. "F-three-hundred-and-two. The Malting, '17."

---

Bridie came up the plank with her face shining.

She had been waiting in the line for an hour, and she had watched the nine go through and come out. Lyra had seen her watch, from the deck: the way she leaned forward each time, the way her right hand came up and covered her left. She came up the plank now, past Tessa saying *Bridie*, past Vane at the rail. She was laughing a little, under her breath, the way women laugh in the last minutes before a birth when they have been told it is nearly done.

She stopped at the chair. She looked back along the Shelf, toward the end of the line, where Fen stood with Halla in the grey.

She lifted her right hand and waved.

Fen, at the end of the line, lifted one of Halla's tiny fists in the blanket and waved it back.

Bridie laughed and sat down in the chair.

"Go on, then," she said to Sable. "I've been one-handed two years. I'd like to hold my daughter properly."

Sable put the cracked core in the brass cup. Bridie's core. Lyra looked at it. It was the same as the others: lilac, faintly lit, with a bright hairline flaw across it like ice. Saying half a name in a voice the size of a thumbnail. *Bri—, Bri—.* It was the same.

She would think about that afterward, for the rest of her life. That she had looked at it, and it was the same, and she could not have known. Nobody could have known. *If none were real it would be a cheat and we'd have hanged them years back. If all were real it would be a mercy. It's some.*

Sable set the fork to the back of Bridie's neck.

"Call it," she said.

And Bridie Cole, laughing a little, with her right hand over her left, called.

Sable struck the fork.

---

Seven hundred and forty-one. High and pure in the grey.

The cracked core in the cup went dim. Quickly, the way the others had. A coal dropped in water.

Bridie gasped. Lyra saw her left hand on the arm of the chair close. Slowly. The fingers curling in, one by one, as if they had heard their own names called. Bridie looked down at it. Her face began to do the thing. *Oh. It's you.*

And then the core in the cup did not go dark.

It went dim, the way the others had. Nearly out. And then, at the very bottom, at the centre of the glass, where the seed was, the thing the press had set her name around, there was something else. Lyra saw it. A faint grey shimmer in the lilac, like a bubble rising through deep water.

It came up.

Not into Bridie. Out. Out of the glass, up the brass arm of the press, into the air over the chair. The way a wasp comes out of a jar.

And the world went off.

---

It was not like going deaf. Lyra had never been deaf, but she knew Mags, and she knew that it was not like that. It was not like having no voice, either, though she had had no voice for three weeks and thought she knew what silence was.

She had not known.

One moment the foredeck of the *Kestrel* was full of sound. The chant coming down the black glass from the wall, *Stasisday, Dawnday*. The rain, very light, on the deck. The murmur of the line along the Shelf. Bridie's gasp. The fork's note, high and pure, still ringing. The creak of the ship on her pins, and Vane's breathing at the rail, and her own heart.

The next moment there was nothing.

Not quiet. *Nothing.* The air had forgotten how to carry. The fork was still ringing; she could see it shiver on the brass arm. She could not hear it. The rain was falling on the deck. She could see the drops strike the planks and burst. She could not hear them. She could see Vane at the rail with his mouth open. Sable at the press with her mouth open. Mags on her feet at the windlass, and Bettany at the hatch, and Pellam Quist leaning forward on his crate, all with their mouths open, all shouting, and there was no sound at all. Lyra felt her own breath go out over her teeth, and heard nothing. Her own heart, which she had heard in her ears her whole life without knowing it, was gone.

*A room that was never built.*

And in the middle of it, over the chair, the thing.

---

Oona Salt had told the Mole once, and the Mole had told the *Kestrel*, that the eye does with them what the ear does: it invents. Lyra's eye gave her a shape about the size of a seated woman, low and wide and wet, grey-blue, folded over itself like a tongue, with a fringe along its underside that moved. It had come up out of the cup and over the brass arm of the press. It hung in the air over the chair for a moment, the way smoke hangs. Then it settled.

On Bridie.

It did not lunge. It leaned. Its fringe went over Bridie's shoulders and down, the way a shawl goes over a woman's shoulders. And Bridie, in the chair, with her left hand curled closed on the arm of it, knowing it was hers for the first time in two years, looked up at it.

Lyra saw her face.

She would see it every night. She would see it in every birthing room she ever stood in again. Bridie Cole looked up at the thing settling over her in the grey, and her face did not do what a face should do. It did not twist or scream. It went soft. The way the faces had gone soft on the Shelf when the ground went quiet. The way Corvan Lisle's had gone on the buttress, Vane said. Soft, and surprised.

And then relieved.

And Lyra could feel the warm going out of her. From six feet away, across the dead air. A bath-water warmth coming off the thing as it took. As it leaned over Bridie and folded its fringe round her and drew. Lyra could feel it go into the thing, Bridie's warmth, the warmth of a woman twenty-six years old who had given birth on this Shelf fifteen days ago. Going.

*They take the warm first, and the warm takes a while.*

---

Mags moved first.

Of course she did. She was the only one on the deck to whom this was not new. Lyra understood it afterward. Mags Tennant had lived her whole life in a room with no sound in it. Not a room that was never built; a room with the door shut, she would have said. But a room with no sound. She had spent forty years reading the world through her eyes and her skin and the deck under her feet. She did not need to hear. She had never needed to hear.

She came off the windlass with the boat-hook in her one good hand. She did not shout, because there was no point shouting, and she had never in her life wasted a breath on a shout. She went straight at the thing on the chair and drove the iron hook of the boat-hook through its fringe, where it spread over Bridie's shoulder, and down into the oak of the chair-back. Pinned it.

The thing did not scream. It had no scream to make. But Lyra saw it *move*. It flinched along its whole length, the way a slug flinches from salt, and drew back off Bridie a little, an inch.

*Iron.*

And Mags looked at Lyra.

Across the dead air. Over the chair and the thing and Bridie. Mags's eyes found hers, steady and grey and absolutely without panic. And Lyra understood that there were two people on this deck who did not need to speak to each other. One who could not hear, and one who could not talk. And that every order and warning and plan that everyone else on the deck was screaming silently at each other with their mouths open was useless. Mags and Lyra had been doing without it for weeks.

Mags tipped her head, very slightly. Toward the mast.

The water-butt.

---

Lyra ran.

She did not feel her feet on the deck. She did not hear them. She ran aft past the press through the dead air to the foot of the mast, where the *Kestrel*'s water-butt stood in its iron hoops: a great oak barrel, chest-high, full. Rain-water and the Watch's water, lowered in the basket and poured in. It had stood there in the cold of the Rift all night with a skin of ice on top. She put her good hand on the rim and pushed.

It did not move. It was full. It weighed more than she did.

She put her shoulder to it. Her burned hand. Her whole weight. Nothing.

And then there were other hands on it. Big ones. Vane's, one good and one splinted, and his face beside hers, white, his lips moving. She could not hear him. She did not need to. And on the other side, Bettany's, red to the wrist from a hundred births, and Bettany's face grim. And the barrel tipped.

It went over on its side on the deck with a crash Lyra could not hear. The water came out of it. A great sheet of ice-cold Rift water, sixty gallons of it, pouring out across the foredeck of the *Kestrel* toward the press and the chair and the thing on the chair. Silent. Like a wave in a dream.

It hit.

---

She saw the thing feel the cold. It drew in. All along its length it drew in and shrank, the way a hand draws back from a stove. The fringe came off Bridie's shoulders. It slid down off the chair onto the deck, into the water, and the water was cold and moving and there was too much of it, and the thing could not take its warmth the way it had taken a woman's. It got smaller.

Mags followed it down with the boat-hook. Pinned it to the deck. Lyra seized the tarpaulin off the press, the heavy oiled canvas, and threw it, and Vane caught the other end. They dragged it over the thing on the deck, and held it down, in the water, in the cold.

It shrank. It was smaller than a seated woman now. Smaller than a dog. It struggled under the canvas, silently, the fringe moving against the oilcloth like fingers under a sheet.

It did not die.

Lyra knelt on the edge of the canvas with her whole weight in the icy water and understood, with a cold slow certainty, that it was not enough. On the Rust Coast they had broken out a seacock and let the January sea into a hold, the whole harbour. Here they had a water-butt. Sixty gallons. And the water was already spreading out across the deck, thinning, running off between the planks and over the side into the Rift. Losing its cold. And under the canvas the thing had stopped shrinking.

It began to grow.

Slowly. It found the warm. Not Bridie's now. The deck's. The canvas's. The warmth of the hands on the canvas holding it down. Lyra's hand, and Vane's, and Mags's. It was drawing it up through the oilcloth. She could feel it. A slow spreading cold creeping into her fingers on the canvas, into her wrist, her arm. Going out of her.

She looked up.

---

The light was coming. The grey over the lip of the Rift, three hundred feet above, was going white. And on the wall, at the top of the black glass, on the tower above the Shelf, she saw something move.

The battery.

Battery Nine. The great flared brass mouth on its iron carriage under its lead hood, at the top of the tower above the end of the Shelf. It pointed out over the edge of the world at the not-colour, as it had pointed every dawn for six years. It was moving. Turning on its rail. Slowly. Ponderously. Coming round. Its mouth swung away from the not-colour and along the wall, and down.

Down.

Into the Rift.

The great brass bell of it tilting on its carriage, lower and lower, until it was looking straight down the black glass wall at the end of the Shelf. At the *Kestrel*, moored to her pins. At the foredeck, and the press, and the canvas, and the four people kneeling on it in the spreading water. At the place where the sound had stopped.

Lyra could see a small figure at the top of the tower. Beside the gun, under the lead hood. Short and broad, with cropped grey hair, and something over her heart that caught the first of the light. Iron.

The General had seen it. From the top of Tower Nine, three hundred feet up, in the grey before the Refusal. She had looked down at the *Kestrel* and seen the people on the deck with their mouths open and no sound coming out. She had seen four of them kneeling on a canvas in the water. She had stood on that wall for six years, listening for exactly this. And she knew.

*The Refusal is withdrawn from the sector of Batteries Eight, Nine and Ten.*

Not yet. Not until Nullday.

Battery Nine had a dawn to fire.

---

Lyra did not hear it fire. Nobody on the deck could. It was the loudest thing in the world, and they were inside the one place in the world where there was no sound.

She felt it.

It came down the black glass wall of the Rift like a wall of water coming down a weir. Every sound in the world at once. The rain and the wind and the sea and a crowd in a square, poured out of the great brass mouth at the top of Tower Nine and down into the dark. And it hit the quiet.

The quiet broke.

Like ice. Like a held breath let go. All at once, from the edges in, the dead air came alive. The roar came into it from every side, and the room that had never been built was full, suddenly, of everything, and Lyra heard.

She heard the roar. She heard the rain. She heard Vane screaming beside her, and Sable at the press, and Bettany, and the line along the Shelf, a hundred and twenty voices, shouting. She heard her own heart, slamming. She heard the white noise of Battery Nine pouring down over the deck of the *Kestrel* like a waterfall. And under the canvas, under her hands, she felt the thing go small.

Smaller. And colder. And slack.

The roar went on. Thirty seconds. A minute. It was so loud it was not sound. It was weight, pressing her down onto the canvas, into the water. She could not have moved if she had tried. She knelt on the oilcloth with her eyes shut and her hands over her ears, and let it pour over her, the Refusal. The Watch's every-dawn *no*, turned down into the Rift, onto one ship and four people and a thing out of a bottle.

It stopped.

The silence after it was the silence of a world where things carried again. Rain on the deck. Water running off the planks. Somebody sobbing. The creak of the ship on her pins.

Mags lifted the corner of the canvas with the boat-hook.

Under it, in the last of the water on the deck, there was something the size of a fist. Grey-blue. Slack. Not moving. Mags looked at it for a long moment. Then she put the iron hook of the boat-hook under it, and lifted it, and carried it to the rail. She dropped it over the side into the Rift, the way Oona Salt had pushed what was left of the *Nancy*'s quiet out through a broken seacock into the harbour.

It fell into the dark below the Shelf. Lyra did not watch it go.

She looked at the chair.

---

Bridie Cole was sitting in it with her head back against the oak and her eyes open, looking up at the strip of white sky over the Rift.

She was very pale. Her lips were blue. Lyra could see her breath, faint, at her mouth, a little cloud in the cold air, slow. She was alive.

Lyra was beside her before she knew she had moved. She had her good hand on Bridie's cheek. Cold. So cold. Colder than any living face she had ever touched, and she had touched the faces of the drowned in the water-meadow at Oakhaven. Bridie's eyes moved. They found her.

Her lips moved. Lyra bent close.

"*Is Halla warm*," said Bridie Cole. A breath. Barely that.

Lyra nodded. She did not know. She nodded anyway.

Bridie's eyes went past her. Toward the plank, the Shelf, the end of the line. Lyra did not look round. She knew what Bridie was looking at. A small figure in an old trooper's coat at the far end of the Shelf, holding a baby in a knitted blanket, two hundred yards away, out of the reach of anything.

"*Good*," said Bridie.

Then she lifted her left hand off the arm of the chair. Slowly. The hand that had not known it was hers for two years, that had come home ten minutes ago through a brass fork. She lifted it, shaking, and looked at it in the white light. She closed the fingers. One by one. And opened them. She looked at it as if she had never seen anything so beautiful in her life.

"*Mine*," said Bridie Cole.

And Lyra had her, and held her, and breathed beside her. Slow and steady. In and out. The way a midwife breathes beside a labouring woman, so that there is one steady thing in the room. She had nothing else. No voice, no whistle. Only her breath. She breathed for Bridie Cole as long as Bridie breathed. She held her new hand in both of hers until it was as cold as the rest of her. And then a while longer.

The rain went on falling on the deck of the *Kestrel* at the bottom of the Rift. It did not stop.

---

She found Pellam Quist when she stood up.

She had forgotten him. Everybody had. He had been sitting on his crate beside the press, three feet from the chair, with his hands tied in front of him and his back very straight. He had been watching, the way Fen had sentenced him to watch. And when the thing came up out of the cup and settled on Bridie, three feet away, he had not moved.

He had leaned forward.

That was what Sable told Lyra afterward. Sable had seen it, from the press, in the dead air, with her mouth open screaming at Mags and nothing coming out. The Registrar-General of the Office of the Catalogue had seen a quiet come up out of a core, out of the seed his own Office had bought off the trench at six sovereigns a bottle, forty a month, for six years. And he had leaned toward it. With his face lit with interest, and his lips moving, as if he were taking notes.

And the thing on Bridie, when the iron went into it, had flinched off her. An inch. Toward the warmest thing in reach that was not holding iron. Its fringe had gone across his knees, Sable said, and stayed there for as long as it took three people to tip a water-butt. He had not drawn his legs away.

Pellam Quist sat on his crate in the grey, still leaning forward a little, with his hands tied in his lap. His eyes were open. They were pale blue and very tired and quite clear, and they were fixed on the chair where Bridie Cole had sat. His face was grey-white, and his lips were blue. There was a fine frost on his eyelashes and on the collar of his good grey coat. And on his face, frozen there, an expression that Lyra would never be able to describe to anyone. It was not fear. It was not pain.

It was the face of a man who has, at last, after a long and patient life, seen exactly what he wanted to see.

He was quite cold. He had been dead, Bettany said, since before the guns fired.

---

The press stopped for the day.

Nobody said so. Sable did not say so. She stood at the press with the brass arm in her hands and Bridie Cole's empty core in the cup, dark. Its seed was gone back to wherever it came from, or into the dark of the Rift in pieces. She looked at it for a long while. Then she took the core out of the cup and put it in her coat, and put the tarpaulin back over the press, wet and torn. She stood with her hand on it.

"I don't know which ones," she said. To nobody. Her voice was quite level. "The seed is in the centre. Under the name. You can't hear it. You can't see it. I didn't see it. It was the same as the others. I looked." She did not take her hand off the tarpaulin. "There's a hundred and twenty-one cards left. And some of them were set on bottles. And some of those bottles were real, and some were mimics, and some were quiets. And I can't tell which. Nobody can tell which, until the stopper's out."

The line was still standing on the Shelf. A hundred and twenty-one people, in the rain, in the order of the cards. They had seen it. They had all seen it. They stood on the black rock in the white morning light and looked at the *Kestrel*'s foredeck. At the overturned water-butt and the torn canvas. At the chair with a woman in it under a blanket. At a small neat man on a crate with frost on his eyelashes.

Nobody in the line moved.

And then Tessa Wray, at the foot of the plank, said the next name.

"Ivo Tarrant," said Tessa. "F-two-hundred-and-forty-one. Hollin Ford."

Sable turned round and looked at her.

"Tessa," she said.

"He's asking," said Tessa. "He's next. He's asked to go." She looked up at Sable on the deck with her narrow quiet face, and her hands, for once, were quite still. "They're all asking. Every one in the line. I've been down it. While you were with Bridie. They all know. They saw." She looked along the line. "They're asking anyway."

Sable stood at the press with her hand on the canvas.

"Not today," she said, after a long time. "Tomorrow. Tomorrow, at the light. When there's water. When the General—" She stopped. "When there's a gun on the tower that'll fire."

Tessa looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded, and turned round, and said it to the line. Quite steadily, the way you say the days of the week. *Tomorrow. At the light. Tomorrow.* And it went down the line from mouth to mouth along the Shelf, a hundred and twenty-one people, in the rain. *Tomorrow. Tomorrow.*

And nobody left the line.

They sat down where they stood, on the black rock, in the order of the cards. In the rain. And began to talk. Names and what was gone, very low.

They were waiting.

---

Lyra carried Halla's mother's blanket down the plank at noon. Bridie's own, the brown wool one she had worn over her shoulders on the Shelf for two years, not the knitted one. She walked along the Shelf past the line sitting in the rain, past every one of them, to the far end, where the new edge was.

Fen was still standing there. She had not moved since dawn. She had stood at the very end of the line with Halla in her arms in the knitted blanket, two hundred yards from the *Kestrel*. She had watched it all. The quiet coming up, the water-butt going over, the gun on the tower coming round and down. The canvas. The chair. She had not come closer, because Bridie had asked her to be at the end, and she had promised.

She looked up at Lyra coming along the Shelf with the brown blanket. She looked at Lyra's face.

She did not ask. Lyra had nothing to tell her with, no voice, no slate. She did not need either. Fen looked at her face, and then down at the baby in her arms, and her own face did a thing that Lyra had never seen it do in all her talk. It went still.

Lyra put the brown blanket round both of them. Round Fen, and Halla inside Fen's arms. She tucked it in at the back the way you tuck a child in. She stood with her good hand on Fen's shoulder, and the rain fell on all three of them at the edge of the Shelf.

For a long time, Fen did not say anything. Lyra had not known that was possible.

Then she said, very quietly, looking down at the baby:

"She's warm."

Lyra pressed her shoulder.

"I kept her warm," said Fen. "The whole time. I didn't come any nearer. Bridie said. She said, *be with someone who's still waiting*. So I waited. I kept her warm." She looked down at the small face in the knitted blanket, asleep. "I was at the end of the line. I'm always at the end of the line."

She was quiet again.

Then she lifted her face and looked out over the new edge of the Shelf at the grey light coming down into the Rift. Out at the wall of black glass on the other side, a quarter of a mile off. Up at the strip of white sky three hundred feet above, where the gun on Tower Nine had swung back to face the edge of the world, and smoke was drifting off its brass mouth in the rain.

"I can't grow up," said Fen. Quite steadily. To nobody. To the Rift. "They took it. It's gone. It broke on a shelf near the door. I'm going to be twelve for ever." She looked down at Halla. "So she'll have to. She'll have to do the growing for both of us. And I'll stay." She pulled the brown blanket tighter round them. "I'll stay exactly the same. So that when she's grown, and she looks back, there's somebody who's still waiting at the end of the line. Who still looks the way she remembers."

She looked up at Lyra in the rain.

"That's all right," said Fen. "Isn't it? Somebody ought to stay the same. Somebody ought to be last."

Lyra could not answer her. She had no voice to do it with.

So she knelt down on the black rock at the edge of the Shelf in the rain. She put her arms round both of them, the girl who would never grow and the baby who would have to, inside the brown blanket. She held them there and breathed beside them, slow and steady. In and out. So that they would know she was there.

Behind them, along the Shelf, a hundred and twenty-one people sat on the rock in the rain in the order of the cards, and talked, and waited for the light.

## CHAPTER TWELVE
### The Visit

> *That law may never wound one without wounding the other, an Arbiter and his heir shall be bound in Symmetry, wound for wound, and neither shall outlive the other by an hour.*
>
> — The Edict of Symmetry, proclaimed at the Organum in 389, when the city played for nine hours and three people in the lower town bled from the ears

*Null-Watch and the Shelf. Stasisday the sixth of Frostmonth, to Massday the thirteenth.*

---

The column came over the edge of the plain at the fourth hour of the afternoon, and Vane counted it, because there was nothing else he could do.

He was on the wall above the postern at Tower Nine with Mags, in the voiceless wind. Below and behind him, at the foot of the black glass, the *Kestrel* lay moored to her pins at the end of the Shelf. Bridie Cole lay on her foredeck under a blanket, and a small neat man lay beside her under another, and a hundred and twenty-one people sat on the rock in the rain in the order of the cards and waited for the light. He could not do anything about any of that.

So he counted the column.

It came out of the west in a long dark line across the grey-green plain, the way a river comes, slow and wide and without hurry. Infantry first, in the black coats and silver collars of the Justiciary, in blocks of two hundred, carbines sloped. Then guns. Field-pieces on limbers, drawn by horses, eight of them. Then two long-barrelled siege guns on iron carriages, each drawn by twelve. Then more infantry. Then wagons. Then, behind everything, a single closed carriage, black, drawn by four greys, with no device on its door.

*Two thousand foot*, said the clerk in the back of his head, who never stopped. *Eight field-pieces. Two siege. A hundred wagons. And whoever is in the carriage.*

He knew who was in the carriage. Everybody on the wall knew. You did not bring two siege guns to the edge of the world to serve a writ on a garrison of the condemned unless you were the man who had signed it.

"*A. Vaelen*," said Vane, aloud, so that Mags could read it. "*Justiciar, for the Arbiterium.*"

Mags looked at the carriage, and then at him.

*The son?* she signed.

"The son," said Vane. "The Dread Arbiter. Aurelian." He watched the black carriage come on behind the column, slow, with its four greys. "Bound to his father wound for wound. Cut one and the other bleeds. Kill one and the other's dead inside the hour." He laughed, the short one. It died in the wind three feet from his mouth. "So you can't kill him. Because then you'd have killed the old man, and the old man's the only thing in Aethelgard holding all eight of them up. Or holding them down. Depends who you ask."

Mags looked at him for a long moment. Then she signed something he did not catch, and he asked her to sign it again, and she did, slowly.

*You've been thinking about it.*

"I've been thinking about it since the Hanging Water," said Vane.

---

The Dread Arbiter came to the gate under a white flag at the sixth hour, on foot, with four men, and the General went down to meet him.

Vane went with her. So did 94, at her shoulder, talking. And Sable came up the rope from the Shelf and stood behind them in the postern with the Ledger under her arm and her smooth face. Nobody asked her to. Nobody told her not to.

Aurelian Vaelen was forty. Vane had expected him older, the son of a man of a hundred and forty. Then he did the sum and understood: born late, to a father already old, and bound to him in the Edict at the age of nine, before he could have known what he was agreeing to. He was tall, and lean, and grey-eyed, in a black coat with a silver collar like his men's and no other mark of rank. Except a small silver scale on a chain round his neck, two pans hanging level. His face was the face of a man who has spent his whole life doing arithmetic in which he is one of the terms.

He stopped in front of the General, ten feet off, in the open ground before the gate. He bowed exactly as deep as protocol required.

"General Ward," he said. His voice was quiet, level, and carried. "You know why I'm here."

The General looked at him. She did not tap.

"I'll say it anyway," said the Dread Arbiter. "For the record." He nodded to one of his men, who had a ledger open and a pen. "I am here to see two writs executed. The first, respecting the vessel *Drift-Kestrel* and every soul who has sailed in her, whom I see you are sheltering. One of them is standing behind you." He did not look at Vane. "The second, respecting the residuals at the lip, whom I am told you have been feeding, contrary to the writ, for six years. And from whose sector I am told one of your batteries fired at dawn today, depressed into the Rift. Contrary to every regulation of this garrison." He paused. "The second writ is not yet in force. It falls on Nullday. The battery's discharge was therefore lawful, if irregular. I note it."

His man wrote it down.

"And there is a third matter," said Aurelian Vaelen. His voice did not change at all. "Thirty-two men of the Justiciary came to this garrison on the twenty-third of Ashfall to post the writ. Under a Justiciar. Corvan Lisle." The grey eyes went past the General, briefly, to the Watch's wall. "He was twenty-six. He was the best I have ever trained. None of them came back."

The General lifted her hand and struck her breastplate. A short phrase.

"The General says," said 94, "nineteen were killed on the buttress, in the fight, after they shot the Quartermaster in the back for carrying bread. Three on the cutter. Ten walked to the edge." A breath. "And the Justiciar walked to the edge. The General says he was very brave and very fast and he did not know about the talking. Nobody had told him."

Aurelian Vaelen looked at 94 for a long moment.

"I see," he said.

His man wrote it down.

---

"Here is how it will be," said the Dread Arbiter.

He said it the way Vane had heard Oona Salt read the Articles on the Mole-head at Port Cantabile: flat, clear, in the voice of a man reading a tide-table.

"On Nullday, at the Refusal, the second writ falls. Batteries Eight, Nine and Ten will stand. If they do not, my guns will see to it that they do. The residuals at the lip will thereby be disposed of, as routed. On Nullday also, the vessel *Drift-Kestrel* and those aboard her will be surrendered to me at this gate, together with all Office property in their possession. That includes a press, a quantity of cores, the second volume of the Concordance, and the person of the Registrar-General, Pellam Quist." He paused. "I'm told he's aboard."

"He's dead," said Sable.

Everybody looked at her.

She stood in the postern with the Ledger under her arm and her face smooth, and spoke in her voice for entries. "Pellam Quist. Registrar-General of the Office of the Catalogue. Died at the lip, the sixth of Frostmonth, at the first light, on the foredeck of the *Drift-Kestrel*. Of a quiet. Which came up out of a core on the press, out of the seed. The seed was tuning stock off Cape Dissonance, bought by his own Office at six sovereigns a bottle." She did not look away from the Dread Arbiter's face. "He leaned toward it. He was watching. He'd been sentenced to watch."

Aurelian Vaelen looked at her for a long time.

"You're the scribe," he said. "From the Veil. The one who went to Whisper-Grove."

"Yes."

"My father speaks of you." He did not say how. "He'll be sorry about Pellam. They'd known each other forty years." He turned back to the General. "Nullday, General. At the Refusal. I'd be grateful if you would make it unnecessary for me to take the Watch. I don't want to. I have two thousand men and two siege guns and you have four miles of obsidian and lead and a garrison that cannot stop talking. I'd lose a great many men. But I will, if I have to. The writ is the writ."

The General looked at him.

Then she struck her breastplate. Four blows. Flat, heavy, slow.

94 did not need to say it. The Dread Arbiter had heard it before, Vane saw. In a chime, perhaps, from a cutter that had sent two words and stopped.

"*The Watch holds the wall*," said 94 anyway.

"Against whom, General?" said Aurelian Vaelen.

Nobody answered.

He bowed, exactly as deep as protocol required, and turned and walked back across the open ground toward his column with his four men. The white flag snapped in the voiceless wind with no sound at all.

---

He opened fire on Tower Nine at dusk.

Vane did not understand it at first. He was on the wall above the postern again, in the last of the light, watching the column's fires spring up across the plain. He saw the two siege guns being run forward on their iron carriages to a low rise a mile from the wall, and the crews swarming round them in the dusk. He thought it was positioning. For Nullday. For the morning when the writ fell.

Then the near gun fired.

He saw the flash. He heard it a long moment later, a deep flat *crump* that came across the plain and hit the obsidian under his boots. And then the sound of the ball arriving. Not at the wall. Above him. At the top of Tower Nine, three towers along.

At the battery.

The ball struck the lead hood over Battery Nine with a sound like a bell the size of a church being hit with a hammer. The hood buckled. Lead and obsidian sprayed out over the wall-walk and down into the Rift. And under the hood, through the gap, he saw the great flared brass mouth of the gun jump on its iron carriage, and tilt.

"*Lawful, if irregular*," said Vane, to nobody, and heard how it sounded. He understood it now. "*Lawful, if irregular.* And he'll not have it happen twice. He's not waiting for Nullday. He's taking the gun out now. So it can't do it again." He was already running. "So there's nothing to fire at the light tomorrow. When the press runs."

The second gun fired.

---

He reached Tower Nine as the third ball hit. He came up the stair inside the obsidian, round and round, past troopers coming down. Some carried others. All talking, high and fast, *Rootday Hearthday Massday*. The tower shook under him like a ship in a heavy sea. Dust came down out of the joints in the black glass. The lead mortar cracked and sifted. He came out onto the battery platform at the top into the dusk and the smoke.

The battery was wrecked.

The lead hood was gone. Torn off and flung back across the platform in a crumpled grey sheet. The great brass mouth of the gun lay on its side off its carriage, half over the parapet. Its bell was crushed in on one side like a stepped-on horn. The bellows were burst. The long brass cylinder behind the breech had split along its seam and was hissing. There were men on the platform. Some standing. Some not. The crew of Battery Nine, twelve of them, the ones who had swung the gun round and down into the Rift at dawn. Who had poured the Refusal onto the *Kestrel*'s deck and broken the quiet. All of them talking.

And the General was on her knees by the parapet with 94 across her lap.

---

He had been standing beside her. Vane learned that afterward from the battery-sergeant, a woman with half her face blackened by powder. When the first ball hit the hood, the General had gone to the parapet to look at the guns on the rise, and 94 had gone with her, at her shoulder, talking, the way he always did. The second ball had come over the parapet. Not the ball. A piece of the hood, the size of a door, torn off by the first ball and flung by the second. Edge-on.

The General had not been touched.

94 lay across her lap with his head on her knee and his eyes open. There was a great deal of blood. Vane did not look at where it came from. He knelt down on the other side and looked at 94's face. It was very young and very white and the lips were moving.

He was still talking.

Vane bent close. He heard it. Faint, fast, steady, the way 94 had talked every moment Vane had known him, under everything. The price of lamp-oil. *—and it was four pence the pint in Ashfall and five in Frostmonth and the quartermaster says six by Rootday, General, six, it's robbery, I said so, I said—*

The General had her hand flat on his chest. She was not tapping. She was holding it there, over his heart, the way you hold your hand on a bell to feel it ring.

"*—six pence*," said 94. "*Six. And the wicks are worse. General. The wicks—*"

He stopped.

Not at the end of a sentence. In the middle. The way the voices in bin seven had stopped, Seraphina said. A syllable and then nothing.

And the General, on her knees on the wrecked battery platform at the top of Tower Nine, with the siege guns on the rise still firing and the dust coming down, did a thing Vane had never seen her do. Nobody on the Watch had seen her do it in six years.

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came out. Of course nothing came out. She had shouted her voice to rags on this wall on the first night of the Rift and it had gone over the edge and never come back. She knelt there with her mouth open over 94's face, and her throat working, and no sound at all. Vane watched her try. He watched her try to say something to a dead boy who had been her voice for six years. He watched it not come.

Then she closed her mouth. She lifted her hand from 94's chest and struck her own breastplate. Once. Very softly.

Vane did not know iron-cant. He would never learn it. But he knew what that one meant.

---

The battery-sergeant took the General's arm and she let herself be lifted. She stood on the wrecked platform in the smoke with 94's blood down the front of her lead coat and looked at the guns on the rise. They had stopped. Their work was done. Battery Nine lay on its side half over the parapet, its brass bell crushed. And three hundred feet below, at the bottom of the black glass, at the end of the Shelf, the *Kestrel* lay moored to her pins with her deck lanterns lit. A hundred and twenty-one people sat in the rain on the rock and looked up.

No gun on Tower Nine.

No Refusal for the Shelf at the light tomorrow, if a quiet came up out of a core. No Refusal for the Shelf on Nullday, writ or no writ. Nothing between the Shelf and the thing under the Maw but the next battery along, Battery Ten, and Eight beyond it. And the writ said those would stand silent on Nullday too.

The General turned to Vane. She looked at him for a long moment. Then she tapped. Slowly, one blow at a time, so that he could follow.

He could not follow. He did not know iron-cant. 94 was dead.

The General understood it at the same moment he did. He saw it go across her face. She stood there on the wrecked platform with her hand on the iron over her heart and nobody to say what it meant.

"*General*."

A voice from the stair. Hoarse. A boy's.

Davey Pruett came up onto the battery platform out of the smoke with his big nose and his enormous hands. Nineteen was behind him, holding his elbow, the way she had held it for eight days when he was a room with no doors. He looked at 94 on the stones. He looked at the General.

"I can read it," said Davey Pruett. "The iron. Not many bother. The crews learn the battery calls and that's all. I learned the lot. I'd nothing to do on the wall but talk and listen." He swallowed. "I can say it for you. If you want. I talk a lot. Everybody says." He looked at the dead boy on the stones again, and his face crumpled, and he made it stop. "I'll say it. If you want."

The General looked at him.

Then she lifted her hand and struck her breastplate. A long phrase. Slow.

Davey Pruett listened with his head on one side. When she had finished, he spoke. His voice was hoarse and cracked and steady.

"The General says," said Davey Pruett, "*his name was Elric Orrel. Of Fenwick, in the Reach. Penal legion, Null-Watch, from 416, for debt. Adjutant to the General.*" He stopped. He drew a breath. "*Discharged.*"

And on the wrecked platform at the top of Tower Nine, the crew of Battery Nine stopped talking.

One breath. Two. Three.

Then the battery-sergeant with the blackened face said, very loudly, "*Rootday.*" And they all said it with her.

---

He went down to the Shelf in the dark, because there was nowhere else on the Watch he could stand to be. Two-Eleven let him down on the yellow rope, talking about lamp-oil, because 94 had been talking about it and somebody had to finish the sentence.

He walked along the line.

He did not mean to. He climbed out of the basket at the foot of the rope and found he could not go to the *Kestrel*. He could not look at the blanket on the foredeck. So he walked the other way, along the Shelf, past the line. A hundred and twenty-one people sitting on the black rock in the rain in the order of the cards, as they had sat since noon. Waiting.

They had seen the tower. They had heard the guns. They had seen the brass mouth of Battery Nine tip and hang half over the parapet three hundred feet above them, crushed. They knew what it meant. Sable had told them at dusk, standing at the head of the line, in her voice for entries. *No gun. No Refusal. If a quiet comes up tomorrow, there is a water-butt, and four people, and nothing else.*

Not one of them had left the line.

He walked past them, card by card, in the rain, in the dark. They were talking. Names and what was gone. Very low. He heard each one as he passed.

At the end of the line, at the far end of the Shelf, where the new edge was, Fen was sitting on the rock in the old Quartermaster's coat, with the brown blanket round her and the baby inside it. Beyond her, along the very lip of the new edge, a single lantern moved slowly. Along and back. Along and back. Orrin Hale, in his borrowed boot, walking the edge.

Vane sat down on the rock beside Fen.

She looked at him. She did not ask. Nobody asked him anything any more. They looked at his face and they knew.

"Who?" said Fen anyway.

"Ninety-Four," said Vane. "The General's voice. A boy called Elric Orrel, from the Reach. In for debt." He looked at the baby asleep in the blanket. "Davey Pruett's her voice now. He came up the stair and said he'd do it."

Fen looked at the baby too.

"Somebody always does," she said. "Don't they. Somebody always comes up the stair." She was quiet for a moment. "Two-Eleven took the rope. Davey took the iron. I've got Halla." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Somebody always does."

"Not always," said Vane.

"No," said Fen. "Not always." She looked out over the edge at the dark. "But I'm going to be here a long time, Captain. I can't grow. And I'm going to watch for it. Every time. Somebody coming up the stair." She looked at him. "That's something to do, isn't it. For ever. Watching for that."

He did not answer her. He did not have one.

They sat there together at the end of the line in the rain. After a while, he found he was counting. Not out loud. The line, card by card, back along the Shelf to the *Kestrel*. A hundred and twenty-one. And one at the end with no card who had asked to be last, and one in a blanket who had never been asked anything.

A hundred and twenty-three.

He did the sum again. It came out the same.

---

The old man came at dawn.

Vane was on the wall. He had come up the rope at the fifth hour, in the dark, because he could not sit on the Shelf any longer and watch Orrin Hale's lantern go along the edge and back. He stood on the wall-walk above the postern at Tower Nine, below the wreck of the battery, in the grey. And he watched the column's camp on the plain begin to stir, and the smoke of its cook-fires rise straight up in the still air.

Then he saw the carriage.

The black carriage with the four greys and no device on its door. It had stood all night at the back of the camp behind the wagons. It was moving now. Slowly, out through the lines, through the blocks of infantry, which parted for it. Not because they were ordered to. He could see that from the wall. Because the men in the black coats got up off the ground and stood aside as it passed, the way people stand aside for a funeral.

It came out of the front of the camp onto the open plain, a mile from the wall, and stopped.

The door opened.

A man got out.

He was very old. Vane could see that from a mile off, in the grey. Very old and very small and very slow. He came down the carriage step one foot at a time, with a hand on the door-frame and a stick in the other, the way an old man gets down from anything. He stood on the grass beside the carriage for a long moment. Then he said something to the coachman, and the coachman did not move. He said it again, and the coachman got down from his box and stood at the horses' heads. The old man turned and began to walk.

Alone. Across the open plain, toward the wall. A mile.

Vane watched him come.

He did not know how long it took. A long time. The old man walked the way a man walks who has to think about every step. Stick, and foot, and foot. Stopping, now and then, to rest, with both hands on the stick, his head bowed. Then on again. Behind him the column stood in its blocks and watched him go, two thousand men in black coats. Not one of them moved to follow. Vane saw the tall lean figure of the Dread Arbiter standing at the front of the camp beside the guns, watching his father walk away from him across the grass. He did not move either.

He could not stop him, Vane thought. He could not go after him, and he could not order anyone to. And if the old man fell, or stumbled, or turned his ankle on a stone, the son would feel it in his own ankle.

He watched his father walk toward the edge of the world, alone. He did not move at all.

---

The General met him at the gate.

Vane went down. He did not decide to. He found himself on the stair, and then in the postern, and then in the open ground before the gate where the Dread Arbiter had stood the evening before under a white flag. Ward was there, in her lead coat with 94's blood still dark on the front of it, and Davey Pruett at her shoulder. Sable had come up the rope and was standing a little behind with the Ledger.

The old man stopped ten feet from the General and leaned on his stick and breathed.

He breathed the way Vane had heard the engineer describe it. Slowly. Deliberately. A man who has to think about each breath. In and out, with a faint sound at the end of each, like wind across the mouth of a bottle. His face was very lined and very pale and very kind. His eyes were the clearest grey Vane had ever seen. He wore a plain coat of dark wool with no mark on it at all, and a soft hat, which he took off.

Under the collar of his coat, at his throat, Vane saw the glass.

Not like the engineer's. Not set, not cyan, not blazing. Laced. Fine pale threads of it under the old skin, like frost on a window, running up out of the collar and along the jaw and under the ear. Faintly lit from inside, so faint you could only see it in the grey of the morning, a glimmer, like the last light in a room after the lamp is turned out. It was everywhere, Vane thought. Under the skin of the hands on the stick. At the temples. In the whites of the eyes. A whole man laced through with it, like a leaf with its veins showing. And none of it had ever set.

"General Ward," said Casimir Vaelen.

His voice was the voice the engineer had described, and Vane had not believed. A doctor sitting on the edge of a bed.

"I'm so sorry about your adjutant," said the Supreme Arbiter. "I heard it. Not through the glass. My son told me, last night, when he came back to the carriage. He told me what he'd done, and why, and he told me the boy's name, which I gather you gave him." He turned his soft hat in his hands. "I wanted to tell you myself. I would have stopped it if I had known. I didn't know until it was done. That's not an excuse."

The General looked at him.

She did not tap.

"I'd like to go down," said Casimir Vaelen. "To the Shelf. If you'll let me. I'd like to see them." He looked past her, past the gate, at the black line of the wall. "I've heard so much about them. I'd like to see their faces. I'm a hundred and forty, General, and I find there are very few things left I'd like. This is one."

The General looked at him for a long while.

Then she lifted her hand and struck her breastplate. Twice. Short.

"The General says," said Davey Pruett, "*yes.*"

---

Two-Eleven let him down on the yellow rope.

Vane went down after him, in the second basket, because somebody had to. He was standing on the buttress when the old man was helped into the basket by Two-Eleven's big slow hands. Vane looked at the frail laced figure sitting in the wicker with his stick across his knees and his soft hat in his lap. He found he had his good hand in his coat, on the butt of *Last Rites*.

He had loaded her at the fourth hour, on the Shelf, by Fen's lantern. A pellet of Aether-Glass. A patch. The pan primed.

Two-Eleven saw his hand. He did not say anything. He looked at Vane over the old man's head, and Vane looked back at him. Then Two-Eleven put his hands to the wheel and began to let the rope out, talking. About lamp-oil.

The basket went over the edge into the Rift.

---

Here is what Vane knew, standing on the buttress with his hand on *Last Rites*, watching an old man go down into the dark on a rope.

He knew that the man in the basket was the reason for all of it. The Rite of Pure Unison, which the old man had designed. The Foundry, and the eleven thousand and forty. The Bleach. The forty singers with copper in their throats. Whisper-Grove, seventy-four thousand. The harvest-houses and the presses and the Hall of Carriage and bin seven. The cores in the *Kestrel*'s hold, one thousand three hundred and forty-two of them over two years, carried by a captain who did not ask. The Valley. Pennick. The Quartermaster. Hetty Bram. Bridie Cole. Ninety-Four. All of it went back, one way or another, by one road or another, to the old man in the basket.

He knew that the old man was going to do something worse. Under the Organum. With all eight. The engineer had told them, as much as he understood. The Grand Unison. Every law in the world sung back to its god at once, until the gods believed relief had come, and let go.

He knew that the old man was alone, and a hundred and forty, and could not run. In thirty seconds the basket would touch the Shelf. Then he would be standing on the black rock among a hundred and twenty-one people he had made, ten feet from a captain with a loaded pistol.

And he knew that if he shot him, the Dread Arbiter would die within the hour, a mile away on the plain, with a hole in him he had done nothing to earn. Because they were bound. Wound for wound.

And he knew it would be over.

Not everything. Not the Bleach, or the gods ringing down, or the thing under the Maw, or the green in the engineer's throat. But the old man's part. The Unison. The Engine. Whatever he had been planning for thirty years under the Organum. One pellet of Aether-Glass, and a dead man and his dead son, and it would be over.

*Ten heartbeats.* He had had ten heartbeats at the hawser off Cape Dissonance, with the axe in his hand and Vesper's face in the spray. He had chosen, and he had been right, and he had spent five years drinking so he would not hear it.

He had more than ten now. He had all the time it took a basket to go down three hundred feet on a rope.

He took his hand out of his coat.

He would not be able to say why, afterward. Not to Mags, not to Vesper, not to the scribe, though she asked him once, very gently, a month later. He would only ever say one thing about it, and he said it once, on the *Kestrel*'s deck, at night, to Kaelen.

*He came down to listen. You can't shoot a man who's come down to listen.*

He did not know if it was true. He thought it might be the stupidest thing he had ever done. He thought it might be the only thing.

He got into the second basket, and Two-Eleven let him down.

---

The old man walked along the line.

Vane followed. Twenty yards behind, no nearer, along the foot of the wall, the way you follow a man you have decided not to shoot. He kept his hand nowhere near his coat and watched the Supreme Arbiter of the Iron Synod walk along the Shelf, slowly, with his stick. Past the *Kestrel*, moored to her pins, with two shapes under blankets on her foredeck. Past the press under its torn tarpaulin. Past the head of the line, where Tessa Wray stood with her hands still. Along the line.

A hundred and twenty-one people sat on the rock in the order of the cards and looked up at him.

He passed Ivo Tarrant, at the head of the line, with a long look and a word Vane did not catch. He stopped in front of the second.

He did not say anything. He stood there, leaning on his stick, and looked at her. A woman of forty in a grey Office smock gone to rags, sitting on the black rock with her knees drawn up. She looked back at him.

And then, the way they had for Pellam Quist, she told him.

"Ivo Tarrant's sister," she said. "Mabry Tarrant. Hollin Ford, '16. They took my sense of smell. I can't smell bread. I can't smell rain. I can't smell my own children." She looked at him. "I've got three. They're in the Reach. I haven't seen them in three years. I'm after my brother, for the press."

The old man looked at her for some time.

"Thank you," said Casimir Vaelen. Very quietly. "Thank you for telling me."

And moved on to the next.

---

It took him three hours.

Vane would remember that. Three hours, in the grey, on the black rock, at the bottom of the Rift. An old man who had to think about every breath, walking two hundred yards along a line of people sitting on the rock. Stopping in front of every one. Listening.

It was not like Quist. Vane had not been there for Quist; he had heard it from Fen. Quist had stood still and listened the way a sorter listens to a core, grading. The old man did not stand still. He leaned on his stick in front of each one and looked at them, and his lined pale face did things while they spoke. Small things. A tightening at the mouth. A movement in the clear grey eyes. Once, in front of a man who had lost the memory of his wedding, the old man closed his eyes and stood like that for a long time, with both hands on his stick. When he opened them, they were wet.

And the glass in him.

Vane saw it. He was not near enough to be sure, and he was sure. As the old man went along the line, further from the *Kestrel*, nearer the end of the Shelf where the new edge was, the faint light in the laced threads under his skin dimmed. At the jaw. At the temples. In the backs of his hands on the stick. The glimmer of frost on a window, going out. As if he were walking into a place where it could hear less.

At the hundredth card he stopped and stood for a long while. Not in front of anybody. Just stood, leaning on the stick, with his eyes half closed and his head a little on one side, the way a man stands in a garden in the evening when the birds have stopped. Listening.

"It's very quiet here," said Casimir Vaelen. To nobody. Softly. "I'd forgotten. I'd quite forgotten what it was like."

Then he went on.

---

Orrin Hale was waiting for him at the end of the line.

Not with the line. Beyond it. At the far end of the Shelf, past the hundred and twenty-first card, past Fen sitting on the rock in the brown blanket with the baby. At the new edge, where the ledge stopped being there, clean and smooth as a cut loaf. Orrin was standing on the very lip of it in his borrowed boot and his bare clerk's coat, with his lantern out, in the grey. His back was to the drop. He was facing the old man coming along the Shelf toward him.

Casimir Vaelen stopped in front of Fen.

He looked down at her for some time. At the old trooper's coat with the sleeves rolled back four times. At the brown blanket. At the baby inside it, asleep. At Fen's face, freckled and narrow and twelve years old, looking up at him with her enormous grey eyes.

"Fenella Ashby," he said softly. "F-four. Ebb Cross, the summer of '13."

Fen stared at him.

"I read it," said the old man. "In Pellam's report. A long time ago. *Retained for observation.*" He leaned on his stick. "I'm so sorry."

"I know," said Fen. "Everybody's sorry." She looked down at the baby. "This is Halla. Her mother died yesterday on your press. Of a quiet out of your seed." She looked up at him again. "She'll grow. I won't."

The old man looked at the baby for a long time.

He did not say anything. Vane, twenty yards back, could not hear whether he did. But he saw him take one hand off his stick and reach down, very slowly, the way you reach toward a thing you are afraid of waking. He touched the edge of the brown blanket with the tips of his laced fingers. Just the edge. Then he took his hand back.

Then he looked up, past Fen, at Orrin Hale standing on the edge.

---

Vane did not hear what passed between them. Not all of it. The old man spoke very low, and the wind came up off the edge and took the words, and Vane stood twenty yards back at the foot of the wall and would not go nearer. He saw them stand facing each other: the old man leaning on his stick, the clerk on the lip of the drop with his back to it. He saw the old man speak. A long time. Quietly. His free hand moved once, a small open gesture, the way a doctor moves his hand when he is explaining something hard to a family at a bedside.

Vane knew what he was saying. He did not have to hear it. The engineer had told them. *He's tired, and he's been listening to tired things for eighty years, and he's decided they want to stop.* The Light, on the three hundredth morning, asking to put it down. Seven more like it. All eight, ringing down, worn out, asking. And the Hush, which was not cruel, which kept people and did not hurt them, which every soul that had ever been near it called *relief*. And the Shelf. Three hundred and sixty-four half-people on a ledge at the edge of the world, eating bread off a rope, watching each other go quiet one by one. And a man of a hundred and forty, who had heard all of it and could not make it come out, saying: *wouldn't you rather?*

*Wouldn't you rather it had never had to hurt?*

The old man stopped speaking. His hand came back to his stick.

And Orrin Hale answered him.

Vane heard that. Everybody on the Shelf heard it. Orrin did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice. But the Shelf was very quiet in the grey, a hundred and twenty-one people sitting on the rock not talking for once. And Orrin's voice was low and pleasant and carried, the voice of a man reading out a receipt.

"You're going to have to say it to my face," said Orrin Hale. "With my face like this." He did not move from the edge. "Tell me I'd have preferred the rest of it."

---

The old man stood in front of him for a long while.

Vane watched him. He watched the Supreme Arbiter of the Iron Synod lean on his stick at the end of a shelf of black rock, in front of a clerk with no fear standing on the lip of the drop. He watched him look at Orrin's face. Calm. Interested. Polite. Missing the one thing that would have made it any other face on earth. He watched him look at Fen at his feet with the baby. Back along the line, at a hundred and twenty-one people sitting on the rock in the order of the cards, in the rain that had stopped. At the *Kestrel*, and the blankets on her foredeck.

He watched the old man open his mouth.

Close it.

Open it again.

"I can't," said Casimir Vaelen.

His voice carried along the Shelf in the grey. Every one of them heard it. Quite clearly. Quite simply. The voice of a doctor at a bedside, when the family has asked the question he does not know how to answer.

"I can't tell you that," said the old man. "I came here to see if I could. I thought I would be able to. I have been able to say it to myself every day for eighty years." He leaned on his stick. "I can't say it to you. I don't have an answer." He looked at Orrin Hale's face for a long moment more. "I'm sorry. I don't. I thought I did."

Orrin Hale looked at him.

"No," said Orrin. Pleasantly. "I didn't think you would."

---

The old man walked back along the line.

He did not stop this time. He walked the two hundred yards back along the Shelf, slowly, with his stick, past every card, past every face, and did not stop and did not speak. They watched him go. Nobody said anything. Not one name. He walked back past the press and the *Kestrel* and the blankets on her foredeck to the foot of the rope. Vane had gone back ahead of him, and was standing there with his hands at his sides.

He stopped in front of Vane.

He was very tired. Vane could see it. More tired than when he had got down from the carriage. His breath came slow and careful with the bottle-sound at the end of it, and the laced glass under his skin was barely lit at all.

"Captain Vane," said Casimir Vaelen.

Vane did not say anything.

"I heard you load the pistol," said the old man. "Last night, at the fourth hour. You were sitting with Miss Ashby. I didn't hear it through the glass; you weren't near the glass. Miss Ashby told me, just now, at the end of the line. She tells everyone everything." He looked at Vane with his clear grey eyes. "Thank you for not using it. I know what it cost. I've heard about the hawser."

"You don't know what it cost," said Vane.

"No," said the old man, after a moment. "No. That's fair. I don't."

He looked up the rope, three hundred feet, at the buttress and the wheel and the big slow figure at the top of it.

"Tell Mr Vance I've had his answer," he said. "*After the press.* I'll be at the Organum. The door will be open." He turned his soft hat in his hands. "And tell the General—" He stopped. He seemed to search for something. "Tell the General I'll speak to my son. About the writ. I'll ask him to stay it. Not lift it. I can't lift it; it's law, and he's law, and I won't ask him to stop being what he is. Stay it. Until I know." He was quiet for a moment. "Until I have an answer for the man at the end of the line."

"And if you never have one?" said Vane.

The old man looked at him for some time.

"Then I suppose," said Casimir Vaelen, "it will stay stayed. And I'll go on doing what I was going to do anyway, under the Organum, with all eight. And I'll do it without an answer." He put his soft hat back on his head. "Which I gather is how everyone else has to do everything. I'd just never had to try it before."

He got into the basket. Two-Eleven took him up, talking.

Vane stood at the foot of the rope and watched him go. A small frail figure in a wicker basket, rising up the black glass of the Rift in the grey, with his stick across his knees. Past the place where the rock went dark. Toward the lip of the world, and the wall, and the wreck of Battery Nine, and the plain, and a column of two thousand men with two siege guns that would not fire on Nullday after all. And a son standing beside them, waiting to feel in his own body whether his father had fallen.

He did not fall.

---

The press ran at the light on Driftday, without a gun on the tower, with a water-butt full of ice-water and four people standing round it who did not panic.

Vane was one of them. He stood at the butt with his ribs strapped and an iron boat-hook in his good hand. Mags was across from him with another. Lyra by the chair with her burned hand bound, and Orrin Hale, who had come in off the edge to stand there because he could do it without being afraid. And Sable at the press, with her smooth face, struck the fork, and the line came up the plank one at a time in the order of the cards.

Ivo Tarrant. Mabry Tarrant, who could smell the rain. The man who had lost his wedding.

Eleven. Twelve. Twenty.

Nothing came up out of the cups but people coming home.

He counted them. It was what he did. He stood at the water-butt with the boat-hook all through that day and every day after it, and counted them up the plank and into the chair and out again. Eleven through thirty-one on Driftday. Thirty-two through fifty-four on Memoryday. On Nullday, the morning the writ would have fallen, the guns on Batteries Eight and Ten fired the Refusal out over the edge of the world as they had every dawn for six years. Nobody on the plain fired at them. On the Shelf, on the *Kestrel*'s foredeck, the press ran fifty-five through seventy-nine.

Four times a stranger came home with somebody's piece. A voice off the trench, a fisherman from the Cape, a girl who had gone in after her brother. They sat in a borrowed head for a day, asking very politely whether it was the tide. Then they went quiet, and took some small thing with them when they went. Sable wrote down every one, and what it had cost.

On the sixty-third, a mimic came up out of the cup. A small grey thing that said a name in a woman's voice, and was glad to be out, and asked a question it already knew the answer to. Mags put the boat-hook through it before it finished the question. They pushed what was left into the water-butt and held it there until it stopped. The woman in the chair, whose piece had come home round it, sat and wept and was all right.

Nothing else came up.

---

Eighty through a hundred and four on Rootday. A hundred and five through a hundred and twenty-nine on Hearthday. And on Massday, the last two.

The hundred and thirty-first came out of the chair at dusk on Massday. A man called Harl Duggan, from Ebb Cross, who had lost his own face in the mirror. He looked at it in Sable's little steel looking-glass for a long time, and touched it, and said, "*Oh. He got old.*" And went down the plank onto the Shelf.

And there was one card left on the rock by the press.

*Ashby, F. Last.*

Fen came up the plank in the dusk, in the old Quartermaster's coat with the sleeves rolled back four times. Halla was asleep in the brown blanket in her arms. She stopped at the top of the plank and looked at the black oak chair, empty, and the press beside it, and the little brass cup at the end of the arm with nothing in it.

Sable looked at her from the press.

Fen did not sit in the chair. There was nothing to put in the cup. She stood beside it, with the baby, at the end of the line, and looked back down the plank and along the Shelf. At three hundred and sixty-three people who had been half and were not any more, or were still half and had been told so. Sitting on the rock in the dusk. Talking. Not names any more. Not what was gone. Just talking, the way people talk at the end of a long day, about nothing.

"That's all of them," said Fen.

"That's all of them," said Sable.

Fen nodded. She stood there for a moment longer. Then she shifted the baby in her arms, and looked up at the strip of dark sky over the Rift, three hundred feet above. A yellow rope was coming down the black glass with a basket on the end of it, and bread in the basket. And under the bread, Vane knew without having to look, a page in a big slow round hand about a dog.

"I was last," said Fen. "I said I would be. I was." She looked down at Halla, asleep. "Now what?"

Nobody answered her.

So she answered herself. She did what she had done every night for six years on the Shelf, at the end of the line, at the edge of the world. She sat down on the deck by the empty chair with the baby in her lap, in the dusk, and began, very softly, to talk.

Vane stood at the water-butt with the boat-hook and listened to her. Somewhere far off to the west, four miles across the plain, on the deck of a black cutter, a man with green in his throat and no fear left in him was waiting for the press to be done so that he could go to the Organum. And somewhere further, in a white room, an old man without an answer was listening for him.

He counted. It was what he did.

Three hundred and sixty-three on the Shelf. Thirteen at the far end, with their hands in their laps. One of them a young woman with a long plait whose name was in the hold, and for whom nobody had yet stood in the door. And an old woman below decks called Agathe Morrow who had her name again. One small figure by the chair who would never grow, and one in a blanket who would have to. And on the plain, a column that would not fire. And in the hold, three hundred and ninety-five people in crates, saying their names in the dark, with nowhere yet to go.

And the engineer.

He did the sum. It did not come out. It had never come out.

"Not yet," said Vane. To nobody. To the Rift.

Fen, by the chair, looked up at him. Then she went on talking.

---

*End of Part Three.*
