## PART TWO: THE BLEACH

---

## CHAPTER EIGHT
### Twice

> *Sing the ones you are sure of once. Sing the ones you are not sure of twice: once for the dead, and once in case.*
>
> — Rubric of the Cantors of the Reach, as taught in the wind-reed orchards

*Port Cantabile. Rootday the ninth of Ashfall, to Hearthday the tenth.*

---

They dropped Ormond Dace at first light from the derrick on the Mole-head, because the Articles are specific about that too.

Lyra had not meant to watch. She had come down off the *Kestrel* before dawn because she could not sleep, and because the ship at night had become a place she could not be in — the hold murmuring its four hundred and thirty-two names, and Kaelen awake on the foredeck with his arm ringing two notes into the dark, and Vesper sitting at the bow with something under her coat that purred — and she had walked out along the Long Mole in the grey to look at the sea, which she had now been looking at for six days and still did not understand. And at the Mole-head there were twenty people standing in a half-ring in the wet, very quiet, and a rope on the derrick, and Oona Salt in her admiral's coat with a paper in her hand.

So she stayed. It seemed worse to leave.

Sable was there. Lyra saw her at the edge of the ring, grey and small, with the Ledger under her arm in its oilcloth and her hands clasped behind her back. Sable had brought the man down the Salt Stair on a string. Lyra understood, looking at her, that the archivist had come because she thought it was owed, and that she would stand there and watch all of it, and that she would not write any of it down.

Oona read the Article. The Twelfth, in full, in a flat carrying voice, the way she read the tide-tables on the Mole every morning: *the Harbour forbids theft from the Harbour's own pocket, which is a different crime and carries the drop.* Then she read the other thing, which was not an Article but which everybody on the Mole seemed to know was coming. Six years of it. The boats, and the lip, and the stopwatch. The forty a month to the House. And one line from the daybook, which she read in exactly the same voice as the rest: *M. Salt, pilot, lost off Wendmere, one bottle, held for H.Q., not to be sold under forty.*

Ormond Dace stood on the edge of the Mole-head with his hands tied behind him and the rope on his neck and his good waistcoat still on, buttoned, and looked at her.

"I never opened them," he said. "Harbour-Queen. I swear it. Not one. I never knew what was in any of them."

"No," said Oona Salt. "You never did."

She did not nod to anybody. She put her own hand on his chest — flat, almost gently, the way you put a hand on a boat to fend it off a quay — and pushed.

Lyra had seen people die. She had sat with four hundred of them in the Hollow and on the Weavers' Row and in back rooms in the fever-years, and sung most of them out, and she had stood at the North Gate while the Hedge took Ada Pellow and on the Long Mole five days ago over a boy in a sail. She had not seen this. It was quick, and it was not quick enough, and the rope creaked on the derrick in the morning wind, and nobody on the Mole made a sound, and the sea went on breathing in and out against the stones below.

When it was done, two of the Harbour's people swung the derrick out and lowered him into the water, and cut the rope, and he went down without a stone because the Coast does not waste stones on that kind.

Nobody rang the Mole-bell.

And Lyra Ashgrove, Cantor of Oakhaven, who had sung the dead for nineteen years and believed with her whole body that nobody should go into the dark unsung, stood at the Mole-head with her hands at her sides and did not sing for him.

She noticed it. That was the thing she took back down the Mole with her. Not the drop, though she would have that too. That she had stood there with the office in her mouth and a breath in her chest and had chosen, quite calmly, to keep both. She did not know what that made her. She turned it over all morning like a stone in a pocket, and could not decide whether it was the first hard thing she had learned on this coast or the first thing she had lost.

---

Kaelen let her measure it at noon.

He did not want to. He had let Bettany measure it on Memoryday night and he had not let anybody near it since, and when Lyra came up the mast-ladder with the slate on her belt and a length of knotted string, he was sitting on the foredeck with his back against the press under its tarpaulin, and he saw the string and looked away.

She sat down beside him and held it up.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he undid his shirt.

She had known it was bad. Everybody on the Coast had seen it. But she had not been this close to it since the Chime, and the light was full noon, straight down, and she had to sit for a moment with the string in her lap before she could do anything with it.

It came up out of the arm at the shoulder the way it always had, the pale cyan of Dawning glass, clear as ice on a pond, with the shadows of the bone showing through it. But it did not stop where it had stopped. It went along the collarbone now, the whole length, a smooth bright sheath over the bone like a second bone made of light, and the skin at its edge had the frosted, waxy look of a burn going white, and it ran on past where the collarbone ended into the soft hollow at the base of his throat and stopped there — just there — an inch and a half short. She could see his pulse moving under the frost at the edge of it. She could see the glass taking the pulse and giving it back, very faintly, as a flicker of light.

And through the cyan, from the point of the shoulder to the throat, the green.

It was not a stain. It was a vein: a thin cold thread of sea-coloured glass running inside the Dawning glass like a river inside ice, and it moved. Not much. The way a current moves under a skin of calm water. She watched it for long enough to be sure, and it was moving, slowly, toward his neck.

She laid the knotted string along it from the point of the shoulder, and counted the knots, and wrote it on the slate, and turned the slate to him.

*6½. 1½" FROM THE THROAT.*

He read it and nodded.

She wiped the slate with her sleeve and wrote again. She thought about it for a long time first.

*WHAT HAPPENS AT THE THROAT?*

He did not answer at once. He was looking out over the harbour, at the place out past the Cay where the Chime had been ringing for six years and was not ringing now.

"I don't know," he said at last, very low. Then, because he was Kaelen and could not leave a thing unspecified: "Glass that sets over a voice-box takes the voice. That's the Grafters' rule. Whatever's in the glass starts to speak through it." He touched the edge of it, where it met his throat. "I'm carrying a ton and a half of drowned bell in here now. Every voice it had learned to wear. A whole town's worth. And a woman called Pell, who stepped off a boat smiling."

Lyra put her hand flat on the glass over his collarbone.

It was cold. It was always cold. And under her palm, very faintly, she felt it ring, two notes at once, the holy one and the drowned one, five hundred and twenty-eight and six hundred and thirty-nine, and she thought, *a man should not have two laws in him. A man should not have one.*

She did not say anything. Every word said within reach of that glass went five hundred miles east to a white room. She took her hand away and wrote on the slate instead, and held it up, and then rubbed it out before he could look at it too long.

*NOT YET.*

It was what he had written on the deck-beam under the eight pump-men's names. He looked at her, and she saw him remember that, and his face did something she had not seen it do in a long time, and then he did his shirt up again to the throat.

---

There was a new thing on the *Kestrel*, and it was the thing everybody had been not looking at since dawn.

Vesper had come up the mast-ladder in the grey of the morning after the Mole with Vane in front of her, one-handed, his face stitched in eleven places by Bettany's needle, and Mags behind her with a belaying pin, and nobody aboard had said anything at all. She had walked across the deck to the bow and sat down on the anchor-bitts with her back to the ship and her face to the sea, and she had been sitting there more or less ever since. She did not eat what Hetty Bram put beside her. She did not sleep that anybody saw. She sat in her own small quiet with her coat round her and looked south.

The crew went round her the way water goes round a rock.

Pennick would not go forward of the mast. Hetty watched her the way Hetty watched weather. Tobiah Marsh, who could not get up off his pallet and had heard the whole story from Bettany in pieces, asked to be carried to the hatch so that he could look at her, and looked for a long time, and said, "Her *eyes*," in a voice like a boy at a fair, and was carried back down. Mags sat on the windlass, ten feet from her, and mended a line, and every so often the two of them exchanged a few signs in the hand-speech that nobody else on the ship could follow — not the Coast's hand-speech, Lyra realised after a while, but something older and quicker, a shorthand from the *Wave-Cutter*'s deck, from before.

And Mote.

Mote had come out of her coat on the first afternoon, while Vesper sat at the bow, and gone exploring.

Nobody saw it go. That was what frightened Pennick, when he found it: that a pale soft thing the size of a cat, warm as a bath and lit from inside with a night sky, had gone down the forward hatch and along the orlop and into the hold without anybody hearing it, and had been there for some hours.

In the hold, among the crates.

Sable found it. Sable went down every evening at dusk with a lamp to sit with the cores for an hour and read names, as she had read twenty a night in the plaza at Whisper-Grove, and that evening she came down the ladder with the lamp and stopped at the foot of it with her hand on the rail.

The hold was silent.

Four hundred and thirty-two people in lilac glass spheres, in crates of thirty-six, stacked to the deck-beams, who had murmured their names in the dark every night since Oakhaven — every night, without fail, a soft continuous whispering like rain in leaves, so that the crew slept with rags in their ears and Vane drank until he could not hear it — and the hold was silent.

And in the middle of the floor, on top of a crate stencilled WG-41,004–41,039, Mote lay curled in a soft pale coil, glowing faintly violet, purring.

Sable stood there a long time. Then she went back up the ladder without her lamp, and across the deck to the bow, and Lyra, who was at the rail and saw her face, followed.

"Your animal," said Sable, to Vesper's back, "is in my hold."

Vesper did not turn.

"It is eating them," said Sable. Her voice was perfectly level. "It is lying on the crates and eating them. Whatever it is in a pressed person that hurts — and I can tell you, because I designed the procedure, that what is left in a core is mostly the part that hurts — it is eating that. They have stopped saying their names." She stood very straight. "They are quiet. They are *comfortable*. I have been reading them their names for three weeks so that they would not forget them, and your animal has spent one afternoon on top of the Sowerby crate, and now the six-year-old in it has stopped asking for her mother."

Vesper got up off the anchor-bitts.

She went past Sable without a word and down the forward hatch, and Lyra heard her go along the orlop — she heard nothing; she felt the ship's sound thin as Vesper passed under the deck, like a held breath moving aft — and a minute later Vesper came back up the ladder with Mote in her arms like a child, soft and folded and disappointed, and sat down again at the bow with it in her lap, and put her hand flat on its back.

"I'm sorry," she said, to Sable, without turning round. "It doesn't know. It thinks it's helping. Everything that hurts, it thinks it's being asked."

Sable stood there.

Down in the hold, very faintly, after a minute, the whispering began again. One voice first, small and high, from the third crate. Then the others.

Sable listened until she was sure. Then she said, to nobody in particular, "Thank you," in a voice Lyra had never heard her use, and went back down the ladder to fetch her lamp.

---

Oona Salt came aboard at sunset with the bottle in her coat.

She came up the mast-ladder herself, which nobody had expected, fifty-six and built like a capstan and heavy in the shoulders, sixty feet of iron rungs in the wind, and stepped over the *Kestrel*'s rail onto the deck with her face red and her breath short and her hand inside her coat. She looked round the deck the way she had looked round the *Bitter Nancy*: everything, all at once, and priced.

Then she looked at Lyra.

"You said before you were ready," said Oona. "Are you?"

Lyra was not. She did not see how anybody could be. But she had been thinking about it for two days, since the Mole and the bottle in her hands and the breathing in it, and she had come to understand that *ready* was not one of the things on offer. She nodded.

They did it on the *Patience*, because Vesper said so.

Vesper had come aft from the bow when Oona came aboard, and stood a little way off with Mote under her coat, listening, and when Oona said *the bottle*, she said, without being asked: "Not on a ship."

Oona looked at her. They had met before, Lyra remembered. Eleven weeks ago, in the evening, when a woman came in off the water and paid in old coin.

"On the water," said Vesper. "In an open boat, in deep water, with somebody at the seacock and the lanterns dark. If it's the other kind, you want to be able to put it in the sea and sail away. You don't want it in a hold." She looked at Lyra. "And you don't take the stopper out. You never take the stopper out. You listen through it."

"Through the wax?" said Oona.

"Through a reed. That's how they did it at the lip, the ones who knew what they were about. Dace never knew. Most of them never knew." Vesper's night-sky eyes rested on Lyra's belt. "You've got a reed."

Lyra put her hand on it before she could stop herself.

It was in the little leather case on her belt with her tuning key: the last wind-reed out of the orchards of the Reach. Her father had cut it in the spring of 412, the season before the reeds turned to sugar-glass and blew away, and given it to her to tune by, and she had carried it for seven years and never once played it, because it was the last one, and because the note it gave was his.

She took it out and held it in her palm. It was a pale gold, a hand long, no thicker than a straw.

"If it's a quiet," said Vesper, "the reed goes dead first. You'll hear your own breath in it — you always hear your own breath in a reed, a little, against the wax — and then you won't. That's when Mags puts the maul through the seacock and you put the bottle over the side and you don't look back." She paused. "And if it's not a quiet, you'll hear what's in it. And it'll hear you."

---

They went out on the *Patience* in the last of the light: Oona at the tiller, Mags amidships with a maul across her knees and her good hand on the seacock housing, Lyra and Sable and Vesper in the bottom of the boat. No lantern. Mabb rowed them out a cable's length beyond the Mole-head into deep water and shipped his oars and sat with his back to them in the bow, because, he said, it was not his business. Teague had wanted to come. Bettany had not let him off his pallet.

The sea was flat. The sky was going green and then grey over the Cay. Behind them the town was lighting its lamps up the cliff, one landing at a time, and the Salt Stair showed as a zigzag of small gold points going up into the dark, and at the top of it, where the counting-house had been, there was no light at all.

Out past the Cay, where the Chime had been, nothing rang.

Oona took the bottle out of her coat and held it in both hands.

Blue glass, palm-high. The wax at the neck unbroken. The label still legible in the last of the light, in a Cantabile scribe's careful hand. Somebody had paid that scribe to write it. Somebody had looked up Merret Salt in the harbour's list of the lost, and written his name and his trade and the place and the year, carefully, in good ink, and stuck it on a bottle and held it back because his sister would pay more.

Oona handed it to Vesper.

Vesper did it with one claw. She set the dark tip of it against the wax at the bottle's neck and turned it, the way you turn an awl, very slowly, until there was a hole in the wax no bigger than the reed. She did not breach the neck. She stopped with a skin of wax still across the glass mouth, thin as paper.

"Now the reed," she said.

Lyra gave it to her. Vesper pushed it into the hole, gently, until the end of it pressed against that last skin of wax, and then — so lightly that Lyra did not see it happen — through.

She held the bottle out to Lyra with the reed standing up out of the neck like a stem out of a vase.

"Your ear," said Vesper. "Not your mouth. Listen first."

---

Lyra put her ear to the end of her father's reed.

She heard her own breath. Vesper had been right: it was there, very faint, a soft rasping hush against the far end of the reed, her own breathing coming back to her off the wax and the glass. She held still and listened to herself breathe.

It did not stop.

She waited. She counted, the way she counted in birthing rooms, the way Vane counted everything. Ten. Twenty. Her breath went on coming back to her, small and steady. Behind her Mags had her good hand tight on the maul now; Lyra could feel her there without looking. Thirty.

Under her own breath, there was another.

Slow. Much slower than hers. A long, shallow intake, a pause, a long shallow breath out, and a pause again, so long that each time she was sure it would not come back — the breathing of a man asleep, or a man so cold and so tired that breathing has become something he has to remember to do.

And under that, a voice.

So faint she was not sure, at first, that it was a voice and not the sea. Low and dry and monotonous, like a man saying something over to himself that he has said ten thousand times, and very slowly she understood the words.

*— by the mark, five. By the mark, five. And a quarter less six. By the deep, six —*

Soundings. A pilot calling soundings. A man standing in the chains of a ship that was not there, with a lead-line that was not there, calling the depth of water that was not there, over and over, in the dark, so that he would go on knowing who he was.

Lyra lifted her head.

She did not need to say anything. Oona was watching her face, and Oona had read faces across forty yards of harbour for forty years, and whatever was on Lyra's face, the Harbour-Queen read it, and something went out of her shoulders all at once, as if a rope had been cut.

"It's him," said Oona.

Lyra nodded.

"Give it here," said Oona Salt. "Give it to me."

---

She took the bottle in her two big hands with the reed standing up out of it and bent her head and put her mouth to the end of it — Vesper caught her wrist, *ear first, ear* — and then her ear, and listened, and Lyra watched her listen to her brother calling soundings in the dark, and watched her face, and would afterward be able to remember almost nothing else about that night, only that.

Then Oona put her mouth to the reed and said, very quietly, the way you speak to a child who is half asleep:

"Merret. It's Oona."

The soundings stopped.

There was a silence in the reed so long that Lyra thought it had gone dead — that this was the quiet, now, at the last moment, and she felt Mags behind her lift the maul — and then out of the end of the reed, small and dry and very far away, as if from the bottom of a well forty fathoms deep:

*Oonie?*

Oona Salt shut her eyes.

*Oonie. Is it the tide? I've been — I've been calling the marks. I thought if I kept calling the marks somebody'd bring her in.* A breath. A long one. *Is it the tide?*

"It's the tide," said Oona. "It's me. I've got you."

*You can't have. It's dark. You can't get down here.* The voice was very calm. That was the worst of it; Lyra would say that to Sable afterward, the only thing she could say about it at all: he was so *calm*. *There's no down here for you to get to. It's all just — the bell. The bell, and the town, and the singing. She sings us. She sings us so we don't — so the other thing stays off.* A pause. *But the bell's stopped.*

Oona opened her eyes and looked at Lyra over the bottle.

"Two days ago," Oona said into the reed. "The bell stopped two days ago, Merret. Somebody broke it."

There was a sound from the reed that Lyra did not at first understand. Then she did. It was a man laughing, very quietly, the way you laugh when the worst thing has finally happened and there is nothing left to be afraid of.

*Then that's what it is,* said Merret Salt. *That's why it's cold. I thought it was me getting tired.* The breath, long and slow. *She sang to us, Oonie. Five years. She was a monster and she ate us and she sang to us every day so we didn't have to hear the other thing, and now she's stopped, and it's coming up. It's coming up the tower. It's very quiet. It's very — kind.* The voice wavered for the first time. *It doesn't hurt. That's what you'd want to know. It doesn't hurt at all. It just goes on.*

Nobody in the boat moved.

Lyra had known. She had known on the Mole on Memoryday, when Kaelen came up out of the Chime with the green in his glass and the bells silent behind him; some part of her had known what it meant and put it away. Every mending borrows; her father had taught her that before she could reach the reed-rows. The glass had drunk the monster's song, every voice it had learned to wear. It had not drunk the people the voices belonged to. They had come here for a lift-tank. They had broken a monster to get it. And the monster had been the only thing standing between four hundred drowned people in a sunken town and the Hush, and now the bell was silent and the Hush was coming up the tower of Wendmere one step at a time, and it was kind, and it did not hurt, and it went on.

Kaelen had done it. And she had stood at the trumpet and been glad.

"What do you want, Merret?" said Oona into the reed. Her voice did not shake at all. "Tell me. Whatever it is. I'll do it."

A long silence.

*Sing me something,* said Merret Salt. *Nobody sings down here now. Sing me something, Oonie, while it's still me listening.*

---

Oona Salt could not sing.

Everybody on the Rust Coast knew it. She had a voice like a cable running out over a fairlead, and she could not hold a note to save her life or anybody else's, and she had never once tried in public in fifty-six years, not at a wedding or a wake or a launching. She held the bottle with the reed in it and looked down at it, and Lyra saw her open her mouth and close it again, and saw her understand that the one thing her brother had asked her for in five years in the dark was the one thing she had never been able to give anybody.

She looked up at Lyra.

She did not ask. She did not have to. She held out the bottle across the bottom of the boat, in both hands, with the reed standing up out of it, the way you hold out a child.

Lyra took it.

She did not think about her voice. That was what she would remember afterward, when Bettany asked her how she could have, with her throat the way it was: that she did not think about it at all. She put her mouth near the end of her father's reed, not touching it, a finger's breadth away, so that her breath would go down it and her voice with it into the bottle and down, down, past the glass and the wax, to wherever a pilot called Merret Salt was standing in the chains of a drowned ship in the dark under a silent bell. And she sang.

The funeral office of the Reach.

Not well. She had sung it on the Long Mole five days ago in a voice like a torn sheet, and she had sung it twice more since — once in her head on the *Patience* under a monster's version of it, and once, very softly, alone in the chandler's loft over nothing, to see if she still could. She sang it now in what was left. It came out of her cracked and low and flat on the long notes, and halfway through the first part she felt the blood come up at the back of her throat and swallowed it and went on, because it was not hers to stop.

The first part, for the one who is gone.

The second part, for the one who is left.

She sang the second part looking at Oona, because Oona was the one who was left, and Oona sat at the tiller in her admiral's coat in the dark with her hands in her lap and her face wet and did not wipe it.

Then Lyra stopped, and breathed, and began again from the beginning.

*Twice,* she thought. *Once for the dead. And once in case.*

---

Halfway through the second singing, the reed answered her.

Not the voice. A hum. Low, cracked, out of tune, the hum of a man who has never in his life been able to carry a tune either, joining in on the long notes of a song he does not know from a country he has never seen, a half-beat behind, the way people join in at funerals. It came up out of the reed under her singing, so faint she felt it in the reed against her lip before she heard it.

*That's not ours,* said Merret Salt, very softly, under the song. *That's not a Coast song. That's lovely, that is. Where's that from?*

Lyra kept singing.

*Oonie,* said the reed. *Tell Teague I'm sorry about the boat.*

And then the reed went quiet.

Lyra felt it. Her breath, going down into the reed, stopped coming back. One moment there was the soft rasp of it against the wax and the far small hum under it, and the next there was nothing — not silence, but less than silence, the reed in her mouth as dead as a stick — and her voice went down into it and did not come back up.

"*Now,*" said Vesper.

Vesper's hand came over Lyra's and closed on the reed and drew it out of the wax in one smooth movement, and pressed her thumb over the hole, and held it there; and Mags was on her knees beside them with the maul up over the seacock and her face white; and for three heartbeats the whole of the *Patience* sat on the flat dark water with nobody breathing, waiting to find out whether the air was about to go out of the world.

It did not.

Lyra could hear the sea against the planking. She could hear Mabb breathing in the bow, and the town's noise very faint across the water, and her own heart. Nothing had come up the neck. Vesper had her thumb over the wax and the bottle was still and cold and silent in Lyra's hands, and that was all.

Vesper looked down at it for a long time. Then she took a stub of candle-wax from her coat and warmed it in her palm and pressed it over the hole, and smoothed it with her thumb, and it was sealed again.

"Was it—" said Lyra. Her voice came out of her as almost nothing at all. "Was that—"

"I don't know," said Vesper.

"Did it take him? Or did he—"

"I don't know, Cantor. Nobody knows. Not from up here." Vesper's eyes in the dark were the only light in the boat. "Maybe the quiet came up the tower and got to him while you were singing, and the reed died because the quiet was in it. Maybe he stopped calling the marks because he'd heard somebody sing, and didn't need to any more, and let go." She put the bottle back into Lyra's hands. "Maybe those are the same thing. I was down there five years and I never found out."

Lyra looked at the bottle.

"That's what twice is for," said Vesper. "Isn't it? In your country. When you're not sure."

---

Oona Salt took the bottle back.

She held it in her lap for some time. Then she leaned over the gunwale of the *Patience*, quite slowly, and put it into the sea, and opened her hands, and let it go, and it went down into the black water off the end of the Long Mole without a sound and was gone.

She sat back.

"That's paid," she said.

Nobody answered her. She did not seem to need anyone to. She took the tiller, and Mabb put out his oars without being told, and they went back in toward the Mole in the dark with the town's lamps coming up to meet them, and nobody said anything at all until the *Patience* bumped against the Mole steps.

Then Oona said, without looking at anyone, "Tell your captain the Free Captains will remember this harbour's debts to him. All of them. Both sides of the ledger." She stood up in the boat. "And tell him there's a Church packet goes out of here on Hearthday, and every man in the Lantern Yard knows which ship came in on the fourth and what she was carrying, and some of them will say so for a sovereign. So whatever he's going to do, he'd best be doing it before the packet makes the Heights."

She went up the steps. At the top she stopped and looked back down at Lyra, and her face in the light from the Mole-lamp was perfectly ordinary, and red-eyed, and tired.

"Where's that song from?" she said. "The one you sang him."

"Oakhaven," said Lyra. What was left of her voice cracked on it. "In the Reach. It isn't there any more."

Oona Salt nodded slowly.

"He liked it," she said, and went up the Mole.

---

Mote would not stop facing south-east.

Lyra came back aboard to find the whole ship arranged around it in a loose, wary ring, the way people stand round a dog that has begun to growl at nothing. It was out of Vesper's coat and on the foredeck, at the very bow, on the anchor-bitts where Vesper had sat since dawn, and it was pointing.

It had no head to point with. It did it with the whole of its soft pale body — leaned, all of it, the way the quiet in the *Bitter Nancy* had leaned in Oona's telling, the way a compass needle leans — toward the south-east, out over the dark sea, over the Cay and the horizon and whatever lay beyond, and stayed like that. When Vesper picked it up and turned it round, it leaned back. When she put it in her coat, the coat leaned.

And it was humming.

In its sleep, or what passed for sleep. Very low. The note.

"Since the Mole," said Vesper. She stood over it with her arms folded and her face very still. "It heard something answer it. On the Mole, when it sang, something answered it from up there—" a jerk of her chin at the foredeck behind her, at the press under its tarpaulin, at the man sitting against it with his collar done up to the throat — "and something else answered too. Further off. It's been listening for that ever since."

Kaelen was looking at Mote. He had been looking at it since Lyra came up the ladder. His face in the deck-lantern's light was the colour it had been at the rail on Nullday night, the colour of stone.

He did not say anything. He could not say anything. Every word went east.

So Lyra took the slate off her belt, and the chalk, and sat down on the deck beside him where he could see it, and wrote, slowly, in capitals, the thing she had been carrying since the first night at Whisper-Grove when Sable had stood in a foot of lit water and told him he was a statistical anomaly, one of two.

*WHO WAS THE OTHER ONE?*

He read it. He knew what she meant. She saw him know.

*Out of eleven thousand at Sol-Luminary,* Sable had said, *you are one of two.*

*Who's the other?*

*I don't know. She fell into the Spire.*

Kaelen did not answer. He held out his hand for the chalk instead, and Lyra gave it to him, and he wiped the slate with the side of his glass hand and wrote underneath, in an engineer's small square letters, so that anybody looking over their shoulders would not be able to read it at a distance:

*THE SOLOIST. FIRST RISER. SHE HELD THE NOTE WHEN THE LIGHT TURNED. I SAW HER GO OVER.*

And under it, after a long moment:

*I CAN HEAR HER. SINCE THE MOLE. IN THE GLASS. SOUTH-EAST.*

Lyra read it twice.

Then she took the chalk back and wrote the only thing there was to write.

*THEN SO CAN HE.*

Kaelen looked at the slate. Then he nodded once, and took his hand off it, and turned his face away toward the dark sea and the south-east, where his arm and the little pale thing at the bow were both, very faintly, ringing the same note.

---

They held the council on the quarterdeck with Kaelen sent below, and it took four minutes, because there was nothing to argue about.

Sable wrote it on the back of Pennick's tank-chart.

*MOTE KNOWS WHERE SHE IS. SO DOES THE ARM. SO DOES VAELEN.*
*SHE IS THE ONLY OTHER PERSON ALIVE WHO UNDERSTOOD THE SPIRE'S QUESTION WHILE IT WAS BEING ASKED.*
*SOL-LUMINARY IS THE CHURCH'S CITY. THE CHURCH HAS A CLAIM ON THE CANTOR.*
*THE CHURCH PACKET SAILS ON HEARTHDAY.*

Vane read it over her shoulder with a lamp. He had his bandaged left hand held against his chest the way he had held it since the Mole, and his face was swollen purple down one side under Bettany's black stitches, and when he read the third line he looked across the deck at Lyra.

"They'll take you," he said. "You know that. The writ stayed the Church's claim. It didn't refuse it. You walk into Sol-Luminary and they'll have a sister of the Sun-Altar at the foot of the mooring-ladder with a paper before your boots touch the stone."

Lyra took the chalk.

*LET THEM TRY.*

Vane looked at it. Then he laughed — not the loud one; the short one, the one out of the side of his mouth, that came out of him when something hurt and he had decided to let it.

"Cantor," he said, "you've been on my ship the best part of a month and you've caught every bad habit on it."

He looked round at them. At Sable with the chalk on her fingers, and Mags on the windlass reading his mouth, and Hetty Bram in the companionway with her arms folded, and Pennick, who had a whole number-three tank now and was looking at the sky as though it owed him money. And at the bow, at the woman sitting on the anchor-bitts in her own small quiet with a soft pale thing in her lap that leaned, and leaned, and hummed.

"Pennick," said Vane. "Can she make the Heights on one new tank and three old ones?"

"She can make the Heights," said Pennick, "if nobody asks her to do it quickly, or to stop on the way, or to carry anything heavier than she's carrying now, or to do anything at all except fly in a straight line with the wind behind her and the Captain not touching anything."

"Then she can do it quickly," said Vane. "We go at first light. Before the packet."

He went forward along the deck, past the mast, past the press, to the bow, and stopped there beside the anchor-bitts. Vesper did not look up at him. Mote leaned south-east in her lap.

"You're coming," said Vane. It was not a question.

"It's going," said Vesper. She put her hand flat on Mote's back. "And where it goes, I go. That's the arrangement." She was quiet for a moment. "I think that's what I was let out for, Vane. Down there. I think that's the only reason. Something wanted this carried up out of the dark, and I was the thing it found to carry it."

Vane stood beside her and looked out at the south-east with her.

"Well," he said. "That makes two of us on this ship who think we were only kept alive to carry something." He nodded aft, toward the hatch where Kaelen had gone below. "You'll get on with him."

---

They cast off from the mast-tower of Port Cantabile at first light on Hearthday the tenth of Ashfall, with the wind in the north-west and the new tank singing sweet and full, and the *Drift-Kestrel* went up off the Long Mole like a bird for the first time since Oakhaven.

Lyra stood at the stern rail and watched the harbour fall away beneath them. The singing coral, green under the morning water. The carillon rigging on forty boats. The Salt Stair going up the cliff in its six wet flights, with one rail lashed across with a spar and one shop standing open and one dark house at the top. The *Bitter Nancy*, careened on the hard with her hold drying out. The Lantern Yard. The Harbour office, where a man called Coll Madder was not lying any more, because Hesper's boats had taken him to the Cay to lie in earth among the children.

And on the Long Mole, at the very end, under the mast-tower, a woman in an admiral's coat, standing by the Mole-bell with her hand on the rope.

As the *Kestrel* came round onto her heading, south-east, Oona Salt rang the bell once.

It carried a long way over the water. Lyra heard it from three hundred feet, clear and low and sweet in the morning air, a single bronze note going out across the harbour and the Cay and the flat sea toward the place where the Chime had been, where nothing rang.

They rang it once for the dead, when they put them in the water.

She did not know who it was for. Merret, she supposed. Or Pell, whose other name the Harbour-Queen had finally found out. Or Colm Teague, or Coll Madder, or the four hundred of Wendmere in the dark under a silent tower with something kind coming up the stairs. Or all of them.

Or the ship going away, south-east, toward a city of light that ate.

She did not sing. She stood at the rail with her hand round the last wind-reed of the Reach in its case at her belt, and listened to the bell until she could not hear it any more, and then a while longer, in case.

## CHAPTER NINE
### The Undersides

> *By order of the Sun-Altar: no glass that shows a face shall be kept, carried, or made within the chains. A reflection is a second light, and the Light is jealous.*
>
> — Ordinance of the Umbral Tiers, posted at every ladder-head in Sol-Luminary

*The Dawning Heights. Hearthday the tenth of Ashfall, to Massday the eleventh.*

---

She came and stood beside him at the wheel on the first night, and he did not hear her come. In the binnacle light her nose was swollen across the bridge and bruised yellow under both eyes where he had broken it on the Mole, and she had not done anything about it.

He had known she would. He had been waiting for it since the Mole-head, the way you wait for a squall you can see on the horizon, and he had been listening for her all evening — for a boot on the deck-planks, a hand on the rail, a breath — and there had been nothing, and then she was there, at his left elbow, on his bad side, looking out over the bow at the dark with her collar up.

The ship went quiet around her. Not silent. Thinner. The creak of the rigging, the hum of the new tank in its cradle, the wind in the envelope seams: all of it seemed to step back a pace, the way people step back from somebody at a funeral.

"You'll want to stop doing that," said Vane, "or I'll put a bell on you."

"You'd have to hear it."

"I'd hear it. I'd have Mags glue it on." He kept his eyes on the compass. "A big one. A cow-bell. You'd clank."

She did not laugh. He had not expected her to. They stood there for a while with the *Kestrel* making her heading south-east over a dark that had been sea at sunset and was land now — he could smell it, earth and pine coming up off the black, and once a scatter of lights very far below, a village that had not yet learned to put its lamps out.

"This ship," said Vesper, after a while. "Where did you get her?"

He had known that one was coming too.

"Bought her," he said. "In '17. Off a Consortium broker in Fortis who didn't ask where the money came from, because he knew." He kept his eyes on the compass. "The Catalogue paid for her. In advance. On the understanding that I'd carry crates for them and not ask what was in them. I carried crates for them. Two years. Thirteen hundred and forty-two people in them, if you want the number; the scribe's got it written down. I didn't ask what was in them." He made a small adjustment to the wheel with his good hand. "I knew what was in them. They talked at night. I drank."

Vesper said nothing for a long time.

"And now?"

"Now there's four hundred and thirty-two of them in my hold that I didn't deliver, and I don't know where to take them, and the scribe reads them their names every night so they won't forget, and the Catalogue would very much like them back." He laughed, the short one. "And a press on my foredeck that can put them back in people, if you've got people to put them in, and each one costs the scribe a piece of herself she'll never get back. And she's got two left."

Vesper was quiet again.

"So you've been carrying people in the dark too," she said.

He did not answer that. There was nothing to answer it with. The tank sang in its cradle. Forward, at the bow, he could see a small pale shape on the anchor-bitts in the light from the binnacle lamp, leaning south-east into the wind like a figurehead, and humming.

"It's going to take us somewhere bad," said Vane.

"Yes."

"You don't sound worried."

"I spent five years at the bottom of the trench off Cape Dissonance," said Vesper, "being digested by something that loved me. I've lost the knack."

---

They raised the Dawning Heights at noon on the second day, and he understood within an hour why nobody went there any more who did not have to.

It came up out of the south like a wall of chalk. Peaks, white as bone, ranked one behind another to the edge of sight, with glaciers hanging in their high corries like poured milk and snow on their summits even at the end of Ashfall. It was very beautiful. It was beautiful the way a skull on a shelf is beautiful: everything about it clean and pale and finished, and nothing growing.

And in the middle of it, the Spire.

He had seen it once, from the sea, before. Everybody on the Rust Coast had, once; you sailed down past Cape Pallor on a clear day and there it was on the southern horizon, a white needle a mile high standing up out of the peaks, and in the old days you could see it shine. The Spire of Light. The Dawning Spire. The Solar Empyrean, the priests called it. Eight months before it broke, a young engineer had stood on a gantry halfway up it and calibrated a bore to a specification he never questioned, and on the three hundredth morning of the Rite it had asked a question and nobody had answered it properly, and it had let go.

It did not shine now. It stood there over the peaks like a white bone, cracked down one side from the top a third of the way to its root, and the crack was black. And all around it, for a mile in every direction, the light was wrong.

Vane looked at it through the glass for a long time before he could say what he was seeing.

The light round the Spire was *too much*. That was the nearest he could come to it. Like a page held up too close to a lamp, so that the white of the paper swallows the ink. Everything inside that mile was overexposed — the peaks, the snow, the sky itself — gone so pale and so bright that it hurt to look at and the eye slid off it and could not find an edge. The Spire stood in the middle of it like the wick in a flame. Around it the air shimmered, very faintly, the way air shimmers over a road in summer.

And hanging from the Spire, on chains, the city.

---

"Sol-Luminary," said Sable, at his elbow, with her hands clasped behind her back. "Population before the Fracture, eighty-one thousand. Population at the Office's last census, in 417, a little over nineteen thousand. Of the difference, eleven thousand and forty died on the morning of the Fracture at the Foundry and in the upper tiers. The rest, the Office lists as *lost to the Bleach* or *departed*, which are separate columns, and which I was told at the time not to reconcile."

He did not answer. He was looking at the city through the glass.

He had heard it described. Everyone had. He had not believed it.

It hung from the Spire's flank at about the half-way mark, a thousand feet below the crack, like a wasps' nest hung from the eave of a barn: a great inverted cone of terraces and towers and domes, white stone and gold, tier on tier, its broad top lying almost level with the Spire's waist and its narrow bottom dangling far down over a gulf of nothing between the peaks. It was held up by chains. He counted them. Nine. Each link as long as a ship's boat, each chain running up at a long slant from the rim of the city to a ring set into the Spire's face somewhere above, a kilometre of black adamantine link going up into the overexposed glare until the glare swallowed it.

The top of the city was white. Not white stone. *White*: the colour of a thing left too long in a window. The domes up there had been gold once; you could tell from the shape of them. The towers had had pennants. The streets had had people. Now the whole upper half of Sol-Luminary lay inside the Spire's mile of wrong light, and it had gone the way the peaks had gone and the sky had gone, overexposed, edgeless, bleached to the colour of bone and ash and paper, so that it hurt his eyes to look at it and he could not tell, at that distance, whether anything up there was standing or only the memory of standing.

"The Bleach," said Sable.

"I gathered."

"The lower half is the Umbral Tiers." Sable pointed with her chin, not lifting a hand. "Below the line. In the city's own shadow."

He moved the glass down.

The bottom half of Sol-Luminary was dark. He had not seen it at first because his eyes were full of the white. The narrowing underside of the cone, below the line where the Spire's glare cut off — and it did cut off, sharp as a knife-edge, the light ending in a hard straight line across the city's flank like a tide-mark — was black. Black stone, black-painted windows, black awnings, black canvas stretched over every opening. Buildings had been built hanging down off the underside, upside down, clinging like bats, like barnacles on a keel, level below level, in the shade of the whole city above them. Lamps glowed in some of them, very small, behind shutters.

Nineteen thousand people, living on the underneath of a city whose top was being eaten by the light.

"And there," said Sable, very quietly, "is the trouble."

---

The trouble was the shadow.

Vane saw it as soon as she said it. The city hung in the glare like a wasps' nest, and like anything else hanging in the light, it threw a shadow — a long dark wedge of shade falling away from its underside across the gulf and the peaks to the north-west, away from the Spire. In the shade, the light was ordinary. Outside it, the closer you came to the Spire, the more the light went wrong.

And the shadow moved.

The sun was moving. It was past noon and the sun was coming round to the west, and the city's shadow was swinging slowly across the peaks below it like the hand of a clock, and the *Kestrel* was coming in from the north-west, and to reach the docks on the city's underside she would have to come in through that shadow, inside it, the whole way, and it was moving, and it was narrowing toward the city like a funnel, and anything outside it—

"Captain," said Pennick, from the tank-cradle. He did not say it loudly. "Captain. The *gull*."

Vane looked.

There were birds in the gulf. He had been seeing them without seeing them: white gulls, a few dozen, wheeling in the shade under the city on the updrafts the way gulls wheel under a cliff, living off whatever the Umbral Tiers threw down. One of them had gone out of the shadow. It had gone the way gulls go, on the wind, without thinking about it, out of the shade into the bright, and Vane watched it through the glass.

It went pale.

Not white — it had been white. It went *paler*. The grey on its back went to white, the yellow of its beak went to white, its black eye went grey and then white, and it was still flying, still beating its wings, still a gull in the shape of a gull. Then the edges of it began to go. The tips of its wings went thin, translucent, like paper held up to a lamp, so that he could see the light through them. Then he could see the light through all of it. It flew on for perhaps three more wing-beats, a gull made of light, a gull-shaped hole in the sky with the white glare showing through it.

Then it came apart, very gently, like ash off a fire, and was gone.

Nobody on the *Kestrel* said anything.

"Pennick," said Vane.

"Captain."

"How long would it take to do that to the envelope?"

Pennick looked up at the great patched bag of the *Kestrel*'s envelope over their heads, eleven new seams in it from the eleven-day crawl, the lift-cells straining full.

"I don't know," said Pennick. "I don't want to know. Keep her in the shade, Captain."

---

He kept her in the shade.

It was the best flying he had ever done, and nobody would ever know it, because it was done in silence and the only people who saw it were too frightened to watch.

The shadow was a long wedge narrowing toward the city, and it was swinging, and he had to fly the *Kestrel* up the inside of it toward the city's underside while it moved. Not straight. A straight line would take him out of it in four minutes as the edge came round. He had to curve. He had to judge the swing of the shadow against the speed of the ship against the wind, and keep her inside the wedge, close enough to the edge on the leeward side that he did not lose the wind and far enough from the edge on the windward side that the envelope did not touch the glare, and it was narrowing every minute, and the sun was not going to wait for him.

He counted. That was what he did. He counted the swing of the shadow-edge across the peaks below — so many yards a minute — and the ship's speed against it, and the narrowing of the wedge, and the distance to the city, and the wind, which was gusting, and every time it gusted the *Kestrel* drifted toward the bright side and he had to bring her back.

He flew one-handed. His left hand was a lump of bandage with two broken fingers splinted in it, strapped against his chest. His face was stitched in eleven places and swollen so that he could barely see out of his left eye. He held the wheel in his right hand and counted aloud, very quietly, under his breath, and Mags stood at his left shoulder reading his lips and relaying to Pennick at the tank-valves with her one good hand, and nobody else on the ship made a sound.

Halfway in, the starboard edge of the shadow touched the tip of the envelope.

He saw it go. A yard of the patched canvas at the very top of the bag, where it bulged highest, came out of the shade into the glare for perhaps four seconds while a gust pushed them over — and he saw the dull brown of the oiled canvas go pale. Go cream. Go white. And then he had her back in the shade, and the yard of canvas was white as a bone up there against the brown, and Pennick made a sound like a man who has been punched.

"It's holding," said Pennick a moment later, in a high flat voice. "It's holding. It's — thinner. It's gone thin. Like silk. I can see the light through it. It's holding."

"Then don't look at it," said Vane, and counted, and did not let her drift again.

---

The Keel Docks of Sol-Luminary hung off the very bottom of the city, at the tip of the inverted cone, in the deepest shade there was.

They were not docks. They were cradles: great iron baskets hung on chains below the city's lowest tier, swinging gently over a two-thousand-foot drop to the floor of the gulf, each big enough to take a skyship's gondola and hold it while the envelope above was made fast to a ring in the stone overhead. There were eleven of them. Three were occupied — a Church packet with a white sun-disc on its envelope, a pair of battered freighters — and the rest stood empty and swinging, and Vane brought the *Kestrel* into the fourth from the end with the last of the shadow narrowing round them like a closing hand, and felt the gondola settle into the iron with a long groan of chain, and let go of the wheel, and found that he could not unclench his right hand from the spoke.

Mags did it for him. One finger at a time.

Then he stood up and turned round, and the whole of the *Kestrel*'s company was standing on the deck looking at him — Pennick and Sable and Hetty Bram and Bettany, and Tobiah propped up in the hatchway, and Lyra at the rail with her slate, and Kaelen by the press with a black canvas sack pulled down over his left arm to the shoulder and tied off at the neck so that not a gleam of cyan showed — and none of them said anything at all.

Vesper, at the bow, said, "That was good."

"I know," said Vane. His voice came out of him in a croak. "I know it was. Somebody write it down. The scribe. Write it down, I want it on record somewhere."

Sable said, "I have," and he laughed, the loud one, and for the first time since Oakhaven it was not fear — and it went out over the swinging iron cradles of the Keel Docks into the gulf under the city, and the whole of the Umbral Tiers above them went, very suddenly, silent.

He stopped.

Every shutter. Every lamp. Above them, level on level, hanging down off the underside of the city like bats off the roof of a cave, the windows painted black and the awnings drawn, and behind every one of them, he understood, people listening. Not to him. To what might come to a noise like that. In the Narrow you did not laugh out loud. In the Narrow nobody did.

"Sorry," said Vane, much more quietly, to the city in general. "Sorry. Long day."

---

The harbourmaster came down a ladder from the tier above with a veil over her face.

It was a black veil, fine as a fishing net, hanging from the brim of a black hat to her collarbones, and under it Vane could see a woman's face, middle-aged and pale and very tired. She wore black gloves. She carried a black lantern with its shutters closed to a slit. Behind her came two men in black with long poles that had iron hooks on the ends, and behind them, on a cord round the neck of the younger one, a small bronze bell with a felt clapper.

She did not look at the ship. She looked at the deck, and at the shadows on it.

"Name," she said, under the veil, in the murmur of the Narrow.

"*Drift-Kestrel*. Out of Port Cantabile."

"Cargo."

"Salvage," said Vane. "Cordage. Passengers."

She did not write anything down. She walked along the deck, slowly, keeping to the shade of the envelope above, and Vane saw that she placed her feet very carefully, always in shadow, and that the two men with poles walked behind her in her shadow, exactly, step for step, like children playing a game.

*Children are taught to walk in the shade of the person ahead of them.* Somebody had told him that, once. He had thought it was a figure of speech.

She stopped by Pennick's tank-cradle and held out a gloved hand.

"Glass," she said.

Pennick stared at her.

"Your mirrors," said the harbourmaster. "Your sextant. Your shaving-glasses, your hand-mirrors, any lens that shows a face. A reflection is a second light. The Light is jealous. They'll be returned when you leave the chains."

Pennick, very slowly, as if he were handing over a child, unscrewed the index-mirror from the arm of his sextant and gave it to her.

They went round the ship. Vane's shaving-glass from the cabin. A tin looking-glass of Hetty Bram's. A brass compact of Bettany's that she had carried since she was a girl and did not want to give up and gave up. The harbourmaster dropped each one into a black velvet bag without looking at any of them, the way you drop a dead thing into a sack.

Then she stopped in front of Kaelen.

He was standing by the press with the black canvas sack over his left arm. She looked at the sack. She looked at the shape of the arm under it, nine inches too long and wrong at the shoulder. She looked at the open collar of his shirt, where — Vane saw it at the same moment she did — the edge of the glass came up out of the cloth along his collarbone, cyan, faintly lit from inside, a gleam of pale light under the deep shade of the docks.

She went down on her knees.

It was so fast that the two men with poles behind her nearly fell over her. She went down on the deck-planks in her black skirts with her black lantern clattering beside her, and pressed her forehead to the wood at Kaelen's feet, and said, in a voice that was not a murmur at all:

"*Consecrated.*"

Kaelen did not move.

"Get up," he said. "Please. Get up."

She did not get up. She stayed there, face down on the deck, and Vane, watching her, saw that she was shaking — not with reverence. With fear. The fear of a woman who has lived for seven years on the underside of a city to keep out of the light, kneeling in front of a man who carries the light in his arm.

"Consecrated," she said again, into the planks. "Forgive the Tiers. We did not know. You'll be wanted on the Altar. They'll want you on Lumina, they'll want you at the Restoration—" she lifted her head, and under the veil her eyes were wet — "please, Consecrated, keep it covered. Keep it *covered*, down here. There are children."

---

The Free Captains' woman in the Keel Docks was called Brenna Corliss, and she ran a chandlery in a hanging house on the second tier with a sign over the door that said ROPE in letters painted black on black, so that you had to know it was there to read it.

Oona Salt's letter got Vane and Sable through her door. Brenna Corliss read it under a slit of shuttered lamplight with her face turned away from the lamp, and when she turned back Vane saw why. The left half of her face was ordinary — sixty, weathered, a coast face. The right half was white. Not pale: white, the white of the peaks and the gull, the skin gone smooth and colourless and faintly translucent, so that in the lamplight he could see, very faintly, the shadow of her cheekbone through it. Her right eye was the colour of milk.

"I was on the fourth tier," said Brenna Corliss, "at a window, on the morning it let go. Facing south." She folded Oona's letter. "I turned my head. Most people didn't have time to turn their heads."

She put the kettle on a spirit-lamp in the corner. She did not ask what they were carrying. She told them, instead, what she thought they should know, the way a Free Captains' agent does when the Harbour-Queen has written *give them what they need* at the bottom of a letter and underlined it twice.

The Church had come back to the Altar.

"Six days ago," said Brenna Corliss. "The High Patriarch's own packet. You'll have seen it in the cradles. Three days after that, a proclamation, read at every ladder-head on every tier, and posted." She went to the door and opened it a crack and pointed, without leaning out, at the lamp-post on the walkway opposite, where a paper had been pasted on the black-painted iron. Vane could not read it from there. He did not have to. He could see the sun-disc at the top, white on black, and the heading in red letters that had been chosen, he thought, because red was the one colour that would still show in the shade.

"*The Restoration of the Light,*" said Brenna Corliss. "A new Rite. On Lumina, at the Sun-Altar, on the first day of Frostmonth. To call the Light home." She shut the door. "And it wants a Cantor."

Vane felt Sable go still beside him.

"Not a Sky Choir. Not a soprano. The Church's done with sopranos; it made forty of them out of copper wire and they're still up there singing." Brenna Corliss poured three cups of something black and bitter. "A Cantor of the Reach. One of the old orchard-trained, who knows the tending notes. The proclamation says the High Patriarch has been shown in prayer that the Light will hear a living chord where it would not hear a pure one." She set the cups down. "And it names her. The Cantor of Oakhaven, under the protection of the Sun-Altar since Dawnday of the last week of Harvestmonth, by warrant of Inquisitor Crane. Lyra Ashgrove. Any who harbour her, the Altar will consider to have taken the Light's own vessel. Any who deliver her will be remembered at the Restoration."

Nobody touched the bitter black stuff in the cups.

"You've got her aboard," said Brenna Corliss. It was not a question. "Oona didn't say so. Oona didn't need to. You came in on the shadow with a Consecrated on your foredeck and every veil on the Keel Docks saw it." She sat down. "They'll come for her before nightfall. Not Flambeaux. Not here. The Tiers would pull the ladders up on Flambeaux. They'll send somebody the Tiers won't refuse."

"Who," said Vane.

Brenna Corliss turned the white half of her face to the shuttered lamp, as if it were the other half that needed the light.

"Who do you think?" she said. "The only one of them the Light never touched." She turned the white half of her face back into the shade. "Seraphina Aubade. The Dawnbringer. Her own sister sang lead on the riser the morning it let go, and the Altar says the sister died in the fire, and the knight has never once said otherwise."

---

There was one more thing, and she told them at the door.

"The Unison's louder," said Brenna Corliss.

Vane looked at her.

"The Wired. Up in the Bleach." She jerked her chin at the ceiling — at the city above them, tier on tier, up through the black-painted shade to the line where the light began. "They sing. Day and night, since the Fracture, one note. You stop hearing it after a year. You hear it in your sleep. And three nights ago, they got louder." She paused. "Not more of them. *Louder.* Like they'd heard something. Like hounds that have got a scent."

"Three nights ago," said Sable.

Vane counted back. Three nights ago was the Mole-head. The dead hour. A soft pale thing at Vesper's hip, singing one pure note up a mast-tower in the rain, and a glass arm sixty feet up answering it, and five hundred miles east, an old man lifting his head.

And four hundred miles south-east, a choir.

"And there's talk," said Brenna Corliss. "On the upper tiers, where the Tiers go up to the line to scavenge. They say there's something up in the Bleach that isn't Wired. Something that moves at night on the chains. With wings." She looked at Vane with her one good eye and her milk-white one. "The Tiers call it the black angel. They leave food out for it on the line. They say it's the soprano who broke the Rite, come back out of the dark." She opened the door. "I'd be home before nightfall, Captain. The Altar keeps the hours."

---

Seraphina Aubade came down the ladder to the Keel Docks at sunset with the light behind her.

There was no light behind her. That was the first thing Vane noticed, when he understood afterward what he had noticed. The sun was going down in the west and the city's shadow lay over the whole of the underside like a black cloth, and the walkways and the cradles and the hanging houses were lit only by shuttered lamps and the faint grey glow of the evening sky in the gulf. There was no light anywhere. And she came down the ladder from the tier above in white armour, and the armour was lit.

Not bright. Not glowing. It was simply the only thing on the Keel Docks that looked as though the sun were on it.

She came down slowly, rung by rung, and the Tiers watched her come. Every shutter on every hanging house was open a crack. Nobody spoke. The veiled harbourmaster stood at the foot of the ladder with her head bowed and her hands clasped. Two sisters of the Sun-Altar came down behind the knight in white robes, carrying a folded paper between them on a cushion of black velvet, and walked in her shadow — and her shadow, Vane saw, fell the wrong way. It fell toward the light.

He had the deck of the *Kestrel* arranged by then. He had had an hour, and he had used it.

Kaelen was below, in the hold, with the arm under its sack and Mags sitting on the hatch-cover over him. Vesper was in the deep shade under the overhang of the cradle's rim at the bow, and you could not see her, and you could not hear her, and if you did not know she was there you would not have known anything at all except that that end of the deck seemed very quiet. Sable stood by the press. Pennick at the tanks, with his hand on the main valve, ready to dump lift and drop the ship out of the cradle into the gulf if Vane gave the word, which Vane hoped very much he would not have to do, because Pennick had been honest with him about what would happen next.

Lyra stood at the rail.

He had asked her to go below. She had looked at him, and taken the slate off her belt, and written on it, and shown it to him, and then wiped it, and stayed.

*I AM NOT CARGO.*

---

The knight stopped at the edge of the cradle and looked at the ship.

She was young. That was the second thing. Twenty-three, perhaps; tall, broad in the shoulder as a rower, with fair hair cropped close to her skull and a face like the face on a coin — very still, very even, as if it had been struck once and set. Her armour was plate, white enamel over steel, plain, without device except a small sun-disc at the throat. Across her back, in a scabbard wrapped in black cloth, she carried a sword, and the black cloth round the scabbard was singed at the edges, as if whatever was inside it had been trying all day to burn its way out.

*The Sun-Blade*, Vane thought. *A splinter of the god's own body. And the light won't eat its own bone.*

She looked at the ship the way he looked at a ship: everything. She looked at the white yard of canvas at the top of the envelope, and her face did not move, but he saw her see it. She looked at the press under its tarp. She looked at the deep shade at the bow where nothing was, and her eyes stayed there for one beat longer than they should have, and then moved on.

Then she looked at Lyra.

"Lyra Ashgrove," she said.

Her voice was a warm low mezzo, and it carried without being raised, and it was not a murmur. In the Umbral Tiers, where nobody had spoken above the Narrow in seven years, it went up the hanging houses and along the walkways and into every cracked shutter, and Vane saw people flinch from it.

"I am Seraphina of House Aubade," she said. "Knight of the Sun-Altar and bearer of the Dawn. I am sent by the High Patriarch to bring you up to Lumina, under the protection of the Altar, which you have been under since the last week of Harvestmonth by the warrant of Inquisitor Crane, and which was stayed by the Arbiterium's writ, and which the High Patriarch has today, in council, unstayed." She did not take the paper off the cushion. "You need not read it. I have. It says what I have told you."

Lyra did not move from the rail.

"I'm told you have no voice," said Seraphina. "I'm told the Rite at Oakhaven took it." Something passed across the still coin of her face and was gone. "I'm sorry for that. I know what it is to see a voice taken. The Altar will give you rest, and a physician, and time. The Restoration is not until the first of Frostmonth. Nobody will ask anything of you until then."

"And then?" said Vane.

The knight's eyes moved to him. She looked at his stitched face and his bandaged hand and, he thought, at the way he was standing, which was the way a man stands who is counting how many steps it is to three different things.

"Captain Vane," she said. "Of the *Wave-Cutter*, once. Your registry was in the Office's circular. They want you more than the Altar does." A pause. "And then, Captain, the Cantor will be asked to hold one note at the Sun-Altar for as long as the Light needs to hear it, and the Light will come home, and the Tiers will come up out of the dark, and the people of this city will see the sun." She said it simply, as if reading a tide-table. "That is what I have been told. I believe it as much as I am required to."

"Your sister believed it," said Vane. "Didn't she?"

The coin of her face did not change at all. That was how he knew he had hit something. Nobody's face stays that still unless they have spent years teaching it to.

"My sister died seven years ago," said Seraphina Aubade.

---

And Mote came out from under the overhang at the bow.

Nobody had told it to. Vane would have sworn on his ship that Vesper had not let it go. But it came out of the deep shade at the bow, soft and pale and folded, lit faintly violet from inside, and went across the deck toward the cradle's edge, leaning, the way it leaned south-east, the way it had leaned for three days toward the Bleach — except that it was not leaning toward the Bleach now.

It was leaning toward the knight.

It went right across the deck to the rail, past Lyra's feet, and stopped there, at the edge, a yard from the white armour, and leaned up toward her — the whole of its soft body tilting up toward her face, the way a sunflower tilts — and began to hum.

The note.

Five hundred and twenty-eight. Pure and high and sweet, the note it had sung on the Mole-head in the rain, the note it had been born on in the dark at the bottom of the trench when something fell out of the sky still singing.

It sang it up at Seraphina Aubade.

And down in the *Kestrel*'s hold, under the hatch-cover where Mags sat, under a black canvas sack, a glass arm that had been ringing faintly under its sack since the cradle took them rang louder, answering it — Vane heard it come up through the deck, a second voice on the same note.

Seraphina did not move.

She stood at the edge of the cradle with the two sisters of the Altar frozen behind her and the whole of the Keel Docks watching through its cracked shutters, and looked down at a small pale thing out of the Hush singing her sister's note at her, and her face — her struck-coin, set, seven-years-still face — came apart.

It did not last long. A second. Less. Vane saw it because he was looking for it, and because he had seen a face do exactly that once before, on a deck in the spray, through ten heartbeats. Then she had it back. She had it back so completely that he wondered, for a moment, if he had seen it at all.

Then Vesper said, from the shade at the bow, "*Mote*," very low, and the soft pale thing stopped humming at once and went back across the deck to her, and the glass in the hold went quiet, and the Keel Docks of Sol-Luminary were silent again.

---

Lyra spoke.

It cost her. Vane heard what it cost her. She had not used her voice out loud since the *Patience*, two nights ago, since she sang the office twice down a reed to a man in the dark, and what came out of her now was not much more than a whisper: torn, cracked, low, like a sheet ripped slowly down the middle. But in the silence of the Keel Docks it carried. Everything carried.

"Your sister," said Lyra Ashgrove, "is alive."

Seraphina looked at her.

"She's up there." Lyra did not point. She did not have to. "In the Bleach. On the chains. The Tiers leave food out for her on the line. That—" a tilt of her head toward the bow, where Mote had gone — "was born on her note, the morning she fell. It's been looking for her since. And there is a man in the hold of this ship who was on the gantry at the Foundry that morning and saw her go over, and who can hear her now, in his arm, singing." She stopped. Breathed. The next words came out of her rougher still. "So you can tell me your sister's dead. But then you have to tell me who's up there singing her note."

The knight of the Sun-Altar stood in the shade with the light on her armour and said nothing for a long time.

Then she said, very quietly, in a voice that did not carry at all — so quietly that only Lyra and Vane, at the rail, could have heard it:

"I have not looked up since I was sixteen years old."

---

Lyra went with her.

Vane had known she would, from the moment she wrote *I AM NOT CARGO* on her slate. He had known it the way you know a wind is going to back before it backs. He did not try to stop her. He had tried to stop her once already, on the quarterdeck at Port Cantabile, and he had lost.

She turned from the rail and looked at him. She did not write anything. She held out her hand, and he took it with his good one, and she held it for a moment — tight, a Cantor's grip, the grip of a woman who has held four hundred hands while their owners pushed something into the world or out of it — and let go.

Then she turned to the hatch, where Mags sat over the hold, and over whatever was under the hatch-cover, and put her hand flat on the wood, the way she put her hand flat on the glass over Kaelen's collarbone. Held it there. Took it away.

And then she turned to the bow, where the Aegis Sentinel had been standing since the cradle took them, nine feet tall in the deep shade, its iron hands at its sides, its helm-slot dark — and which had begun, the moment Seraphina said *Lyra Ashgrove*, to move. Very slightly. A tick in the chest. A shifting of weight from one iron foot to the other. Vane had seen it, and been counting it, and had been very much afraid of what it would do if the knight put a hand on the Cantor.

Lyra looked at it, and held up her palm, flat. *Stay.*

The Aegis stopped.

It stood there in the dark at the bow with its helm turned toward her and the faint light in its slot fixed on her face. It did not move again. But inside its chest, very faintly, so faintly that Vane only heard it because the whole Keel Docks was silent, something began to chime: a few notes of a hymn, slow, an alto line, over and over, the way a child hums to keep from crying.

Lyra stepped over the rail onto the cradle's iron rim, and the knight of the Sun-Altar put out a gloved hand to steady her, and Lyra did not take it.

They went up the ladder. Seraphina first, in her white armour with the light on it and her shadow falling the wrong way. Then Lyra, in her grey, with her slate on her belt and her father's reed in its case beside it. Then the two sisters with their black cushion and their unread paper.

Vane stood at the rail and counted the rungs.

Thirty-one. Thirty-two. The white armour went up into the dark of the tier above and was lost among the hanging houses, and the grey went after it, and he kept counting, because counting was a thing to do, until there was nothing on the ladder at all and he was counting nothing.

Fifty.

Behind him, the hatch-cover moved. Mags got off it. Kaelen came up out of the hold with the sack still over his arm and his collar open and the cyan glass gleaming at his throat in the dark, and stood beside Vane at the rail and looked up at the ladder.

He did not say anything. He could not. Every word went east.

But his arm was still ringing, very faintly, under the black canvas. And it was not ringing toward the ladder, where Lyra had gone.

It was ringing up. Up through the city, through the hanging houses and the Umbral Tiers and the hard straight line where the shade ended, into the white. Toward the Bleach. Toward something up there in the overexposed ruins on a chain a kilometre long, singing one note, alone.

Vane looked at Kaelen. Kaelen looked back.

At the bow, in the deep shade, Mote had begun to lean again.

## CHAPTER TEN
### The Bleach

> *Q. What is the Light?*
> *A. The Light is the first note and the last.*
> *Q. What does the Light want?*
> *A. The Light wants to be held.*
>
> — Catechism of the Choir-Church of the Pure Note, first lesson

*Sol-Luminary. Massday the eleventh of Ashfall, the dead hour, to Stasisday.*

---

They planned it on the slate in the hold with Kaelen in the room, because there was no longer any point sending him out of it.

He had said so. He had come down the ladder from the deck after Lyra went up the other ladder, and stood among the crates with the arm under its black sack and his collar open, and said it out loud, flat, to all of them — to Vane and Sable and Mags and Vesper, and to the man five hundred miles east who would hear it as clearly as they did.

"He knows where we are. He knows why. He heard the animal sing on the Mole and he heard my arm answer it, and he's heard everything said on this ship within thirty feet of me since the Foundry." Kaelen looked at the slate in Sable's hands. "There's nothing left to hide from him. Only from the Church. And the Church can't hear me."

So they wrote it with him watching, and it was short.

*K. — VESPER — MAGS. UP THROUGH THE TIERS AT THE DEAD HOUR. INTO THE BLEACH. FIND HER.*
*BRING HER DOWN IF SHE'LL COME.*
*IF SHE WON'T — FIND OUT WHAT SHE HEARD.*
*V. AND SABLE: FIND WHERE THEY'VE PUT THE CANTOR.*

Vane read it twice. He was grey under the stitches. He had not sat down since the ladder.

"Why Mags?" he said.

Sable answered, in the voice she used for entries. "Because there are forty copper-throated singers up there who were made into one mind by a note, and who sing that note day and night, and who — according to the Free Captains' woman — have begun singing it louder since the Mole. Anything that sings can call. Anything that calls can lure." She looked at Mags across the hold. "Mags Tennant is the one of us no voice can lure. She walked through the Aegis's chord in the Cloister when it put the rest of us on the floor."

Mags, who had read every word of that off the archivist's lips, nodded once and went to get her belaying pin.

"And her," said Vane, looking at Vesper.

"Because I'm going," said Vesper, "and you can't stop me, and the animal's going whether I go or not." Mote was in her coat, leaning; Kaelen could see the shape of it under the dark cloth, tilted up toward the deck-beams, toward the city overhead and the white beyond it. "And because I'm the only one on this ship who's been somewhere worse."

---

They went up at the dead hour.

The Umbral Tiers of Sol-Luminary at night were a cave hung upside down. Kaelen would think of it that way afterward, when he tried to draw it for Tobiah: a cave whose floor was the sky. Ladders went up from the Keel Docks through the hanging houses, iron rungs bolted into black stone, and walkways ran between the houses on chains, swaying very slightly with the movement of the whole city on its nine great links, and below every walkway there was nothing — the gulf, two thousand feet of dark air, and the peaks at the bottom of it pale as teeth under the stars.

People slept in hammocks slung under the walkways. He had not expected that. Whole families of them, rolled in black blankets in rows like bats, swinging gently over the drop, because the hanging houses were full and a hammock in the shade was better than any bed on the tiers above the line. He saw a woman's hand trailing out of a blanket, the fingers moving very slowly in a dream. He saw a child asleep on its father's chest with its thumb in its mouth, and the father's eyes open, watching them pass, not moving.

Nobody spoke to them. The Tiers did not speak at night. Every few walkways there was a hush-lamp on a hook — the same as in Oakhaven, a lamp with a reed-whistle in the chimney so that a house would never go entirely silent — and the whistles made a thin faint chorus all through the dark, a hundred small high notes, so that the whole underside of the city breathed with them, very softly, like a sleeping animal.

And under the whistles, from far above, from up through the stone and the shade and the line, the other note.

He had been hearing it since the cradle took them. The whole ship had. You stopped noticing it after a while, Brenna Corliss had said, and he understood that now, because it had been in his ears for twelve hours and it had already become a kind of floor under everything else: one high pure tone, sustained, endless, without a breath in it anywhere, coming down out of the Bleach through the body of the city like a wire through a wall.

Five hundred and twenty-eight.

His arm was ringing with it. Under the black sack, under the knotted cord at the shoulder, the glass was singing back at it note for note, the way it had sung back to the Church's Pure Note on the Greenroot road. He could feel it in the bone. He could feel it in the edge of the glass at his throat, that last inch and a half, a cold hum there under his jaw like a tuning fork pressed to the skin.

Mags walked ahead of him in the dark, and he walked in her shadow, the way the children did. He did not know he was doing it until he noticed, and then he could not stop.

---

The line was at the ninth tier.

You came up the last ladder through a hanging house that had been gutted and left — a dark room with nothing in it, black walls, a black-painted window — and out through its roof onto a walkway, and there it was. The city's underside ended. Above you, the stone stopped hanging and began to stand; the tiers went from being houses on the underneath of things to being houses on top of them. And across the face of the city, slanting, from left to right, straight as a ruled line, the shade stopped and the light began.

It was night. That was what made Kaelen stand still on the walkway with his hand on the chain-rail.

It was the dead hour, three hours after midnight. There was no moon. Below the line, the Umbral Tiers were as dark as the inside of a hat. And above the line, the city was lit.

White. A flat sourceless white glow that came from nowhere and from everywhere, from the stone itself and the air and the overexposed sky, with no shadows in it that pointed down — every shadow above the line pointed away from the Spire, outward and down the city's flank, long and thin and black, as if the Spire were a sun that never set. And it was not daylight. It did not warm. It did not show colour; there was no colour in it to show. It lay on the white ruins of the upper tiers like snow lies on a field, and it did not move, and it hummed.

The Light was ringing down. A law does not vanish when its singer stops; it decays, like a struck bell. Sable had said that to him once at Whisper-Grove, in her entry-voice, as if reading it off a page. He had not understood it until now. The Chorister of Light had let go of its law on the three hundredth sunrise of the Rite, seven years ago, and its law had not gone out. It was going out. Here, nearest the Spire, it was still going out — the last of seven years of ringing, the glow of a struck bell — and what was left of it did not give. It took.

"Wrap up," said Vesper, very low.

They had brought black cloth from Brenna Corliss's chandlery: the Umbral Tiers sold it by the bolt. Mags wound hers round her head and face until only her eyes showed, and pulled black gloves on, and Vesper did the same, and Kaelen did the same except for the arm, which was already in its sack. Then Vesper went up the last few rungs of the walkway's end-ladder to the line, and stopped just below it, and looked up and down it for a long time.

On the line itself, on the stone where the shade ended, there were things.

A loaf of bread, black on the crust, half wrapped in a cloth. A tin cup with water in it. A candle-end, unlit, in a saucer. A child's drawing on a scrap of paper, weighted with a pebble: a black shape with wings, and a sun crossed out. Three pears. A folded blanket, black.

Offerings. Left on the edge of the light by the people who lived in the dark, for the thing that lived in it.

"The black angel," said Kaelen.

Vesper looked down at him from the ladder. Under her black wrappings, her eyes were the only light in the shade, violet, points of cold in them.

"Keep your voice down," she said. "And keep your arm in the shadow. Whatever's up there can hear it. Everything up there can hear it."

---

The first thing the Bleach did to him was take the colour out of his hands.

He had gone over the line behind Vesper, into the shadow of a fallen column that lay across the street above it — a white column, white street, everything white — keeping low, keeping in the column's long thin shadow that pointed away from the Spire. And he had put his right hand down on the stone to steady himself, and his hand had been in the light for perhaps two seconds before he pulled it back into the shade.

He looked at it.

The back of his right hand was pale. Not white; pale, the brown of it gone a shade lighter, like a hand that has been in water too long. The hairs on it had gone white. The nails had gone white. There was a line across the back of his wrist where the shade had begun, sharp as a tide-mark, and above it his skin was its own colour, and below it his skin was the colour of a candle.

It did not hurt. That was the thing. It did not hurt at all.

He looked at it for long enough that Mags came back along the column and took hold of his sacked left arm and shook it, and he looked up, and she signed, one-handed, sharp:

*Don't look. Move.*

He moved.

They went up through the Bleach from shadow to shadow, the way you cross a river on stones. A broken wall, its shadow lying out across the street like a black plank. A fountain-basin, dry, its rim throwing a black crescent. A doorway, deep enough to stand in. Vesper went first, and she moved the way she had moved on the Mole-head, without a sound, from black to black, and she did not seem to need to look where the shadows were; she simply went where they went, the way water goes downhill. Mags went next, counting under her black wrappings, her lips moving; Kaelen could see them through a gap in the cloth. He went last, with the arm against his chest and the cracked ribs grating every time he went down on his belly, the shoulder he had wrenched in the tower at Wendmere locking each time he reached, and in every patch of light he crossed — and there were patches you could not help crossing, two strides, three, a bright white gap between one shadow and the next — he felt the light come onto him like hands.

Not on his skin. His skin it took, a little at a time, colour going out of whatever it touched. On the arm, it was different.

On the arm it *went in*.

---

He felt it the first time they crossed a gap of more than three strides: a broad street, a plaza's edge, the shadow of a statue on the far side and nothing between. Vesper went across it in a flicker. Mags went across in four long strides with her head down. And Kaelen went across it with his arm in its sack against his chest and the light came down on him from everywhere, sourceless, white, and the glass under the sack *drank*.

He had felt the glass drink before. It drank what it broke: hounds, Legionnaires, a ton and a half of drowned bell. This was not that. This was the arm drinking the way a dry sponge drinks: without breaking anything, without his doing anything at all, simply opening, every crystal in it turning toward the light like a field of flowers turning toward the sun, and pulling it in.

It was the light of the Dawning Spire. It was the light his arm was made of.

It came in through the sack and into the glass and up. Up the arm. Into the shoulder. Along the collarbone. He felt it go along the bone toward his throat in a single long cold pulse, the way the bell had gone in at the Chime, but softer, and worse for being softer, because it was not a blow. It was a tide. It was coming in.

And then he was across, in the shadow of the statue, and it stopped.

He crouched in the black with his back against the plinth and his right hand at his throat, feeling the edge of the glass. He could not tell. In the dark, with his fingers, he could not tell whether it had moved. An inch and a half. He thought it was still an inch and a half. He thought it might be less.

Vesper was looking at him from the next shadow along. She did not say anything. She did not have to.

*It wants you,* her eyes said. *This whole place. It wants you more than it wants us.*

He nodded, and did not look at the next gap of light, which was five strides wide, and crossed it.

---

He saw the Bleached in the old Glassmarket, on the fourth tier above the line.

Brenna Corliss had told them about it, in the chandlery, in a few flat words. *There's people still up there. From the morning. You'll see them. Don't touch them.* He had thought she meant the dead. He had seen the dead before; he had walked through Oakhaven while it went under a wood.

They were not dead. Or they were not *only* dead.

The Glassmarket was a long arcaded square under the shoulder of a dome, and it had been a market on the morning of the Fracture: you could see the stalls, the awnings, the trestles, the baskets. All of it white. White baskets of white fruit on white trestles under white awnings. And among the stalls, standing where they had been standing when the Light let go, the people.

They were the shape of people. That was all. A woman at a stall with her hand out, as if to take a coin. A man with a basket on his shoulder, mid-stride. Two old men on a bench with their heads together. A woman by the fountain with her face turned south — toward the Spire, toward the sound, toward whatever she had heard that morning that had made her turn — and something small on her hip that Kaelen looked at once and did not look at again.

They were white, and they were translucent. The light came through them. He could see the arcade behind the woman at the stall *through* her, through her face and her chest and her outstretched hand, the way you see a window through a paper lantern. Their edges were soft. They did not cast shadows, because there was not enough of them left to stop the light. They were seven years thin.

And they were standing. Seven years, in that white, unmoving, on the morning of the Fracture, still reaching for the coin.

"Don't touch them," Vesper said again. She said it without turning her head.

He did not touch them. He walked past the woman at the stall in the long thin shadow of the arcade, close enough to have put his hand through her, and as he passed he saw that her outstretched fingers were trembling.

Very slightly. The way the bubbles had trembled round him in the Drift at forty feet. As if something in her were still, faintly, after seven years, trying to finish the gesture.

He walked on. He did not stop walking. He told himself that it was the hum of the Unison in the stone, making everything shake.

---

The voice came out of his arm in the shadow of the dome, while Vesper and Mags were scouting the next street.

He had known it would. He had been waiting for it since the Mole. In the Tuning Room at the Malting it had come to him out of the glass, quiet and courteous and very tired, and told him that two pieces of one Spire are one thing in two places; and it had not spoken to him since. It had only listened. He had felt it listening, the way you feel somebody standing behind your chair.

Now, in the white heart of the city where the glass had been cut from, a thousand feet below the crack in the Spire it had been cut from, it spoke.

*You've come home.*

It came out of the glass and into the bone and into his ear from the inside, the way his own voice comes to him. An old man's voice. Very gentle. A doctor's voice, at a bedside, at the end.

*I wondered if you would. When the little one sang on the Mole and your arm answered it — I thought, he will go to her. He'll go to the soprano. He'll want to know what she heard.* A pause, and in it, very faintly, Kaelen heard breathing: slow, old, deliberate, the breathing of a man who has to think about each breath. *I'd like to know too. I've wondered for seven years. She is the only one of us who understood the question while it was being asked, and she has never once let me near her.*

Kaelen crouched in the shadow and said nothing.

*Say something, Kaelen. You needn't whisper. There's no one up there to hear you but me and the Unison, and they don't hear words.*

"You'll take her," said Kaelen. His voice came out low and rough. "Lyra. The Church's Restoration. You'll let them put her on the Altar."

*I won't let them do anything. I have no authority over the Church. Severin Ashe has never heard a god in his life, and he believes with his whole heart that the Light can be called home with a hymn. He is wrong.* The old voice was very kind. *He has been shown, he says, in prayer, that the Light will hear a living chord where it would not hear a pure one. He has been shown nothing. Someone in his household reads my correspondence and repeats it to him as revelation. I have never discouraged this.*

Kaelen shut his eyes.

*You know what will happen,* said Casimir Vaelen, gently. *You know better than anyone alive. The Light let go because it was told it could rest. It is ringing down now; it will go on ringing down for some years yet, and then it will be out. If your Cantor stands on Lumina on the first of Frostmonth and sings to it in the voice that sang a ten-thousand-year-old god a lullaby at Oakhaven, it will not come home. It will hear somebody ask it, very kindly, whether it is still there. And it will answer.* A pause. *The last thing any of us will see in this city is the answer.*

"You won't stop it."

*No. I'm sorry. I don't want her hurt, Kaelen. I want what I have always wanted, which is for them to be allowed to stop. If Severin Ashe wishes to do on Lumina what I have been planning to do under the Organum, I will not stand in his way. He'll be doing it with one god instead of eight. It will be a small mercy. But it will be a mercy.*

Kaelen opened his eyes. He was looking at his own right hand, pale as a candle from two seconds in the light.

"There's another way," he said.

*Then come and show me it.* The old voice did not change at all. *The offer stands. It has always stood. Come to the Organum and I will draw the glass out of you with my own hands, and you will live, and you will hear nothing ever again. Or come to the Organum and keep it, and stand next to me, and listen to all eight of them, and then tell me there's another way.* The breathing, slow. *Find the soprano first, if you like. Ask her what she heard. I would very much like to know what she says.*

And then, more softly, as if he had turned away from the bedside for a moment to say something to himself:

*Be careful of her, Kaelen. She has had seven years in the dark to think about you.*

---

Vesper was standing in front of him when he looked up.

He had not heard her come back. He never would. She was standing in the shadow of the dome with her black wrappings pulled down off her face and her violet eyes on his arm, and she had been there, he understood, for some of it. Long enough.

"I can hear him," she said. "Breathing. I told the scribe. Like an old man in a chair, asleep, with his mouth open." She looked at the arm. "Does he always talk to you like that? Like a priest at a deathbed?"

"Yes."

"I knew some things in the trench that talked like that." She pulled the black cloth back up over her face. "Mags has found the Unison. Two streets up. Come and look, and don't let that arm out of my shadow."

---

He heard them before he saw them, and he had been hearing them for twelve hours, and still he was not ready.

The note.

It was louder here. It had been a wire through a wall in the Umbral Tiers. Here, two streets from it, it was the wall. It came from everywhere, out of the white stone and the white air, a single high pure tone so loud and so even that it stopped being a sound and became a kind of pressure, as if the whole of the Bleach had been pumped full of it like a lung, and he felt it in his teeth and the roots of his hair and the hollow of his throat where the glass stopped, and the arm under its sack took it and *rang*.

Five hundred and twenty-eight.

He had heard it for three hundred mornings once, from a gantry halfway up the Spire. He had calibrated a bore to it.

They lay on their bellies on the roof of a white house, in the shadow of its white chimney, Mags and Vesper and Kaelen in a row, and looked down over the parapet into the street below, and there they were.

The Unison.

Forty of them. He counted. He did not mean to; it was the engineer, the clerk in the back room, totting up the column. Forty, in four ranks of ten, coming slowly down the white street toward the plaza at its end the way a procession comes, step and step and step, and singing.

They had been a choir once. He could see that. They wore what had been choir-white — the long robes of the Sky Choirs of Sol-Luminary, girdled at the waist, with the high stiff collars the sopranos wore — but the robes had gone the colour of the street, and so had the people inside them. Bleached. Not translucent, like the market-people; not yet. They were too solid for that, or the light did not take them as fast, because they were singing its note and a thing that is singing to the Light is a thing the Light is still listening to. But white. White faces, white hands, white hair cropped short or gone. White eyes. And in the throat of every one of them, glinting through the white skin under the high stiff collar, a line of copper.

The wire.

He knew it. He had seen the kit for it, in the depot at the Oakhaven quarry, in a velvet-lined case with a silver needle and a collar: Stage Nine. *Collar: standard. Wire: standard.* A copper wire threaded through the voice, through the fold of the vocal cord itself, so that the singer could not change pitch. So that the singer could only hold one note.

Forty of them. Seven years. One note.

And they were not forty. That was the thing he saw, lying on the white roof with the arm screaming its note against his ribs. They walked in step. Not *together*: in step, the way the parts of a single machine move in step. Forty heads turned at the same moment, to the same angle. Forty mouths stood open in the same shape. Forty chests breathed, but never two at once: the breaths went round them, staggered across forty bodies like the cylinders of an engine, so that the note never broke, never wavered, and went on.

One mind. Forty bodies. Singing the Light its own name, forever, so that it would not forget.

*Hunting the soprano who broke it.*

---

It heard his arm.

He knew the moment it happened. The procession stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped, forty bodies at once, mid-step, as if a hand had closed on the mechanism. And forty heads turned. In the same instant, at the same angle, all forty white faces with their forty open mouths and their copper-threaded throats came round, slowly, toward the house where the three of them lay in the shadow of a chimney on a white roof.

The note did not change. It never changed. But it *leaned*. He felt it lean, the way the tenor had leaned toward his arm in the Chime, the way Mote leaned south-east: the whole weight of that one sustained tone turning, very slightly, toward the place where something else on the roof was singing it back.

*They think I'm one of them,* thought Kaelen. *Or they think I'm her.*

The front rank took a step toward the house.

Mags had his collar. He felt her hand close in it. She was on her knees already, dragging him back from the parapet, across the roof, toward the trapdoor they had come up through. She could not hear the note. She could not hear anything. She had seen forty heads turn, and that was enough.

But the arm would not stop.

It sang. It sang louder the closer the Unison came — he could feel it, every step the front rank took toward the house, the glass ringing harder against his ribs, *answering*, the way it had answered the Pure Note and the Chime and the little pale thing on the Mole, helplessly, because it was made of this note and it could not hear its own note sung and stay silent. It was a lantern. It was a lantern in a dark room, and he could not put it out, and forty white faces in the street below were following it up the house like moths.

He heard them on the stair.

Not their feet. Their note, coming up through the house, through the floors, getting louder.

"*Vesper*," he said.

---

She did not ask him what he meant. She came across the roof to him in the chimney's shadow in a single movement and took hold of him.

She did it the way you would hold a man who is drowning: from the front, with both arms round his chest, the arm and all, pinning it between them — her body against his, chest to chest, her face against the side of his neck where the glass ended — and held on.

And the quiet came down over him like a lid.

He had felt the edge of it on the Mole, at a distance. He had not been inside it. It was not silence. It was the thing that silence is a shadow of: an absence, a thinning, the air round him forgetting how to carry. The roar of the Unison's note went away. Not quieter; *away*, to the far side of a wall a long way off. The ringing of the arm against his ribs went away. He could feel it ringing — he could feel it in the bone — but the ringing stopped at his skin, at her coat, at the edge of her, and went no further. He could hear her breathing, very slow and very controlled, an inch from his ear. He could hear his own heart, by bone, not by ear, the way Oona had said: a dull far thump in the skull.

He could hear nothing else in the world.

And under her coat, pressed between them, against his ribs, something soft and warm that purred.

They stayed like that. Mags knelt beside them with her back against the chimney and her pin across her knees, watching the trapdoor. And Kaelen, held in the arms of a woman he had known for three days, inside the edge of a silence that had come up out of the trench with her, looked over her shoulder and watched the trapdoor too.

It opened.

A white head came up through it. Then another. Then four, five — they came up onto the roof out of the house, slowly, one after another, the Unison, forty white bodies in their bleached choir-robes with their copper throats and their open mouths, and they spread out across the roof in a long line, all at once, all facing the same way, and their heads turned. Left. Right. Searching. Listening.

He could see their mouths moving. He could see the note in them — the cords standing out in their white necks, the copper glinting. He could not hear it.

They could not hear him.

They stood on the roof for a long time, forty of them, ten feet away, in the full white light, turning their heads, and Kaelen did not breathe and Vesper did not move and Mags did not blink, and the arm rang and rang against his ribs and none of it got out.

Then, all at once, they turned. Forty bodies, one movement. And went back down through the trapdoor, one after another, white heads going down into the dark, the way they had come.

---

Vesper did not let go of him for a long time after the last of them had gone.

When she did, she stepped back and pulled the cloth back up over the lower half of her face, and he saw that her hands were not quite steady.

"That's the trouble with being a lantern," she said. "Everything comes."

He could not answer her. His voice was not working yet.

They waited on the roof until the note had moved off — down the street, across the plaza, gone, a wall of sound receding until it was a wire again — and then Mags tapped his shoulder and pointed.

There was somebody else on the roof.

He had not seen her come. She was crouching at the far end of the parapet, in the white light, not in the shadow: a woman, or what had been a woman, in a bleached choir-robe with the high stiff collar, alone. She was thinner than the others. More translucent; he could see the parapet's edge, faintly, through her shoulder. She had not come up with the Unison. She had not gone down with them. She was crouching there with her white hands round her white knees, looking at them, and in her white throat, under the collar, there was a line of copper.

She opened her mouth.

He braced for the note. It did not come. What came out of her was a single sound — one pitch, cracked and thin, nothing like the clean wall of the Unison — and then she shut her mouth, and opened it again, and another sound came, a different pitch.

It took him a long time to understand that she was speaking.

One note at a time. One word to a note. Each one forced out of a throat with a wire through it that would only let it hold one pitch, so that to say anything at all she had to stop, and shut her mouth, and fight the wire for a different note, and open it again. It was like listening to somebody speak by striking a bell, one blow for each word.

*You —*

*— are —*

*— looking —*

*— for —*

*— her.*

Kaelen nodded.

The woman looked at the black sack over his arm for a long time, with her white eyes. Then at Vesper, at the shape under her coat, which had begun, very softly, to hum.

*She —*

*— is —*

*— on —*

*— the —*

*— sixth —*

*— chain.*

She stopped. He saw her throat work. The copper glinted. She fought the wire for one more note, and it came out of her higher and thinner than the rest, almost nothing, almost a breath.

*Tell —*

*— her —*

*— we —*

*— are —*

*— sorry.*

And then she got up off the parapet, a white woman in the white light, and walked away across the roof and down the far side of it, and Kaelen saw her go, and saw the light come through her as she went, and did not see her again.

---

The sixth chain left the rim of the city on the south-west side, nearest the Spire, at the top.

The very top. They came to it an hour before dawn, up through the last tiers of the Bleach where the light was so strong that they went from shadow to shadow at a crawl, on their bellies, and Kaelen's hands were white to the wrist and the side of Mags's face that had been turned toward the Spire for one bad moment crossing a bridge was the colour of a candle, and the arm had drunk so much light under its sack that he could feel the edge of the glass at his throat like a collar of ice and did not dare touch it to find out.

The rim was a wall. A great curved parapet of white stone running round the top of the inverted cone of the city, with the drop beyond it — the whole height of Sol-Luminary, and then the gulf, and then the peaks — and set into it, at intervals, the anchor-houses: squat towers of stone built over the great iron rings where the chains came down out of the sky and took hold of the city.

The sixth anchor-house had no roof.

The chain came down into it out of the white sky at a long slant, a kilometre of black adamantine link, each link as long as a man was tall, going up and up toward a ring set somewhere in the Spire's flank above them until the glare swallowed it. It was black. In all that white, the chain was black; the light did not take it, or could not. It came down into the roofless tower and through the great ring in its floor, and the whole city hung on it, and on eight others, and Kaelen could feel it as they came up to the tower's broken door — the long slow strain of it, singing very low in the stone underfoot, the note of a thing that has been holding a weight for a hundred years and is tired.

And in the tower, against the wall, in the angle where the chain came through the ring, there was a nest.

---

That was the only word for it. A nest. Built the way a bird builds, out of whatever could be carried up: choir-robes, white, folded and piled; scraps of gold leaf peeled off the dead domes; black blankets from the line, a great many of them, the Tiers' offerings, carried up from the edge of the shade night after night and laid down here in the angle of the wall in the deepest shadow there was — the shadow of the chain itself, a long black bar across the floor, the one piece of real dark at the top of the Bleach.

The child's drawing was there. Not the one from the line tonight. Others. Dozens. A black shape with wings, and a sun crossed out. Weighted with pebbles round the edge of the nest like a fringe.

There was nobody in it.

Mote came out of Vesper's coat.

It went across the floor of the tower in the chain's black shadow, soft and pale and quick, faster than Kaelen had ever seen it move, and up onto the piled robes, and into the middle of the nest — and stopped there, and curled, and lay very still, glowing violet in the dark.

And sang.

Not hummed. Sang. The note came out of it clear and high and full, louder than on the Mole, louder than at the Keel Docks — five hundred and twenty-eight, pure as a struck glass, sweet as nothing Kaelen had heard since the riser at the Foundry — and the arm under his sack answered it at once, helplessly, ringing with it, two voices on one note in the empty tower at the top of the world.

Vesper said, very quietly, "*Oh.*"

She was looking up.

---

She came down the chain.

Out of the white. Out of the glare above the tower, where the chain went up at its long slant into nothing. Kaelen saw her first as a mark against the light, a black fleck on the black chain far above, and then the fleck was moving, and was larger, and was coming down the links toward them the way a spider comes down a thread, fast and sure, head first.

She was the only black thing in the Bleach except the chain.

She landed on the rim of the roofless tower, on the broken top of the wall above the nest, and crouched there with the white sky behind her, and opened her wings.

He had been told. Brenna Corliss had said *with wings*. He had thought it was a way of speaking, the way the Tiers said *angel*.

They were wings. They grew from her shoulders through rents in what had been a soprano's white robe, and they were enormous, twice her height from tip to tip, and they were made of something he did not have a word for. Not feathers. Plates. Long curved blades, layered like the feathers of a bird but hard and thin and edged, and black — black the way the chain was black, a black the light could not get into — and down the edge of every one of them, a line of gold, molten, moving, like the edge of a coal. When she opened them, the light in the tower changed. Not darker. *Bent*. The white glare that came from everywhere at once curved round her wings like water round a stone, and where it curved, it went gold.

Under the wings, she was a woman. Tall and very thin, in rags of choir-white, with a cropped skull and a long throat and bare feet that gripped the stone like hands. Her face was the face he had seen once, for less than a second, going backward over the rail of the first riser with the fire behind her: the lead soprano, the girl who had held the note when the light turned. Seven years older. Not bleached. The only face in the Bleach that still had its colour, pale olive, with two lines cut down from the eyes to the jaw that might have been scars and might have been where tears had run for seven years in a place where tears burned.

In her throat, under the jaw, glinting, a line of copper.

She looked down into the nest. At Mote, singing in the middle of it.

And her face — he would remember this as long as he remembered anything — went from nothing to rage so fast that he did not see the change. It was simply there. Her lips drew back off her teeth. Her wings came up and forward over her head like a raised fist, and she came down off the wall into the nest in a single fall, and took Mote in both hands.

Not gently. Round its body, hard, the way you take a thing by the neck to wring it, her long fingers sinking into its soft pale sides, and lifted it, singing, up in front of her face.

"*You,*" she said.

---

Her voice.

It was two voices. That was the thing nobody had been able to tell him, because nobody who had heard it had come down from the Bleach to describe it. It came out of one throat and it was two voices at once, not in harmony — in *opposition*. One was copper: high, bright, metallic, a soprano's voice forced flat against a wire, every word rung out on the same hard pitch, the note, the one note, five hundred and twenty-eight. The other was under it, and it was not a voice. It was the absence of one. A dark low hollow thing that had no pitch at all, that sounded like the inside of the trench must sound, like the far side of Vesper's quiet, like a door opening onto nothing — and the words came out of both at once, the copper and the void, grinding against each other in her mouth.

"*You. You. Little — thing. Little —*"

Her hands closed.

Mote did not struggle. It hung in her grip, soft and folded and glowing, and went on singing her note up into her face — her own note, the note she had held on the three hundredth morning on the first riser at the Foundry while the light turned cannibal and ate her sisters' throats, the last thing she had sung as a woman before Ignatius Crane's fire threw her over the rail into the dark — and the copper half of her voice cracked.

"*Stop it,*" she said. "*Stop singing it. Stop — singing — it —*"

And Mote, in her hands, began to purr.

Kaelen saw it happen. He saw the little pale thing go warm in her grip, the violet in it brighten, the soft sides of it pressing up into her palms the way a cat presses into a hand that has started to stroke it. He saw it find her pain. He saw it reach for it — not her body, not her wings — the other thing. The seven years. The riser. The fire. The fall. He saw it take hold, very gently, of whatever it was in Solenne Aubade that hurt worst, and begin to eat.

And her hands stopped closing.

Her face did a thing he could not watch and could not look away from. Her mouth opened. The copper voice and the void voice came out of it together in a sound that was not a word at all. Her wings came down, slowly, round her and round the thing in her hands, closing over the nest like a black-and-gold tent, and the light bent round them and went gold, and under the wings, in the dark, Kaelen heard the soprano who had broken the Rite of Pure Unison begin to weep — and the little pale thing in her arms purring and purring and eating what it could.

"*I hate it,*" said Solenne, from under her wings, in both her voices. "*I hate it, I hate it, it's the only thing that —*"

She did not finish.

---

Vesper had her hooks out.

Kaelen had not seen her draw them. She was standing at the edge of the nest with one in each hand, the blades that were not there, the outlines of absence, and her face under the black cloth was very still. She did not move toward the wings. She did not say anything. She stood there with the hooks low and her weight on the balls of her feet and her violet eyes on the black-and-gold tent in the nest, and waited, the way you wait at the edge of a deck in a boarding action to see which way a man will jump.

"Give it back," said Vesper, very quietly.

The wings opened.

Solenne came up out of them onto her feet in the nest with Mote held against her chest in both arms, the way you hold a baby, and looked at Vesper, and at the hooks, and her face was wet and furious and very tired.

"*It's mine,*" she said. Copper and void. "*It was born on me. It was the first thing I heard in the dark, it was singing my note before I knew I was still singing it — it's mine —*"

"It's mine," said Vesper. "I carried it out."

They looked at each other across the nest, the two women who had come up out of the dark, one with wings and one with hooks, with a soft pale thing between them purring in the soprano's arms.

And the arm under Kaelen's sack, which had been ringing all this time with Mote's note, rang louder.

He felt it go. He could not stop it. Whatever Mote had taken out of Solenne, whatever it was eating there in her arms, the note it sang had changed — not the pitch, the pitch never changed; the *weight* — and the glass answered it, and it came up through the black canvas sack and out into the roofless tower, five hundred and twenty-eight, clean and cold, the note of the Dawning Spire, from the Dawning Spire's own glass.

Solenne's head came round.

---

She looked at him for a long time.

At the sack over his arm, and the light coming through it. At his open collar, and the cyan glass along his collarbone to an inch from his throat, lit from inside, singing her note. At his face.

He saw her know him.

He did not know how. He had been a man on a gantry halfway up the Spire, sixty feet above the riser, in a Conservatory engineer's leathers with a hood and goggles, one of eleven men on the bore-crew, for three hundred mornings. She had been on the first riser with a wire in her throat, singing, facing the Spire, never once turning round. She could not have seen his face. She could not have known his name.

But the glass was cut from her Spire, on her morning, out of her note.

"*You,*" said Solenne Aubade.

She put Mote down in the nest. Gently, this time. It curled there, purring, glowing, and she stepped out of the nest over it and came across the floor of the roofless tower toward him in the shadow of the chain, slowly, with her wings half-folded behind her and her bare feet silent on the stone, and stopped an arm's length from him, and looked up into his face. She was a head shorter than he was. She did not seem it.

"*The gantry,*" she said. "*Third platform. The man with the tuning-fork on a chain round his neck. You never stopped moving. Three hundred mornings and you never stood still, up and down the gantry with your fork and your gauges. We used to watch you. On the riser. You were the only thing in that whole place that moved.*" The copper voice cracked. "*I used to think you were checking on us.*"

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say.

"*And on the last morning,*" said Solenne, very softly, in both voices, "*when the Spire asked — when it asked us, when it said—*" her throat worked, and the copper wire glinted under the skin, "*— when it asked what it asked, and I was the only one on the riser who understood it, and I was holding the note because I didn't know what else to do — I heard you shout.*"

Kaelen's breath stopped.

"*From the gantry. Over the note. Over all forty of us. I heard a man's voice shout* stop. *Just once. Just that.* Stop." She looked at the arm. "*It was you.*"

"Yes," said Kaelen.

His voice came out of him as almost nothing. Five hundred miles east, an old man in a white room was listening to every word of it, and he did not care.

"Yes. It was me."

Solenne Aubade looked up at him with her wet furious face and her two voices and her copper throat, in the shadow of the chain that held up the city at the top of the world, with the white light bending gold round her wings.

"*Then I have been waiting seven years in the dark,*" she said, "*to ask you one thing, engineer.*"

He waited.

"*Why,*" said Solenne, "*did you wait until the three hundredth morning?*"

## CHAPTER ELEVEN
### The Calling-Song

> *The Vessel is to be addressed in kindness at all times. She is to be told that she is very fortunate, and reminded of it when she forgets.*
>
> — Rubric of the Sun-Altar for the Keeping of a Vessel, third article

*Sol-Luminary. Massday the eleventh of Ashfall, night, to Dawnday the thirteenth.*

---

The Basilica of the Pure Note was built across the line, and Lyra understood the moment she saw it that whoever had built it there had meant something by it.

It stood on the ninth tier, where the city stopped hanging and began to stand, and the hard straight edge of the Spire's light ran through the middle of it like a blade through a loaf. Below the line, in the shade, its west end was black: black stone, black doors, black glass in the high lancet windows, so that from the walkway it looked like a church cut out of the night. Above the line, its east end was white. Not painted. *Taken*. The great dome over the crossing had been gilded once; you could see the shape of the old gold in the ribs of it, the way you can see the shape of a face in a skull. It was white now, the white of the gull and the peaks and Brenna Corliss's cheek, and it stood up into the overexposed sky like a bone.

Between the two halves, across the whole width of the nave where the line fell, there was a wall. Black iron, floor to vault. And in the wall, a door.

"The Lightward Nave," said Seraphina, when she saw Lyra looking. It was the first thing she had said since the ladder. "The altar end. It has been sealed since the morning of the Fracture." She did not look at the door. "Nobody goes into it. Except me."

Lyra did not ask why. She thought she knew.

---

And under everything, the note.

She had heard it from the Keel Docks — everyone had, the Unison's long wire of sound coming down through the city — but this was not the Unison. This was nearer, and warmer, and it came from inside the Basilica: from the choir-stalls in the shade half of the nave, where nine men and women in white sat in three rows in the dark with their faces lit from below by shuttered lamps, and sang. One note. Held. When one of them ran out of breath, another took it up, and the note went on without a seam, the way the Unison's did, except that these were not wired and not bleached and not one mind. They were simply people, in shifts, singing the Church's note in the dark because the Church was preparing a Rite and wherever the Church prepares a Rite it holds the Pure Note over the place day and night until the Rite begins.

Five hundred and twenty-eight.

She knew it. Her body knew it before she did. She had heard it for five days over Oakhaven, coming down the Greenroot road under glass-fire and settling over the town like snow, never stopping, so that you slept in it and woke in it and after the third day could not remember what silence had been like. She had heard it in the white room where the two kind women fed her honey and raw egg and oiled her throat. She had heard it in the Root-vault, four hundred feet down, with a wire in a fold of her skin.

She stood in the nave of the Basilica of the Pure Note with Seraphina Aubade beside her in white armour, and her hands began to shake, and she could not make them stop.

Seraphina looked down at them.

She did not say anything. She looked at Lyra's hands for a moment, and then she did a thing Lyra did not expect: she turned, very slightly, so that she stood between Lyra and the choir-stalls. As if her armour could keep a sound off.

It could not. But Lyra noticed it, and wrote it down in the part of her head where she kept the things people did when they thought nobody was counting.

---

They gave her a room on the shade side of the iron wall, and two kind women.

She had known they would. She had known it on the ladder. She had known it, if she was honest, on the deck of the *Kestrel* at Port Cantabile with the chalk in her hand, writing *LET THEM TRY* on a slate for Vane, because she had been inside the Church's kindness once before and she knew exactly what it was and how it was done, and some part of her had wanted to go back into it with her eyes open, the way you go back into a burning house for a thing you left.

The room was small and clean and black-walled, with a cot and a basin and a chair and a hush-lamp on a hook whistling its small high note. The women were sisters of the Sun-Altar, in white, one young and one not, and they were kind. They brought warm water and washed her hands and face. They brought a bowl of honey with a raw egg beaten into it and held it to her lips. They brought a little flask of oil warmed in water, and the older one tipped her head back with two fingers under her chin, gently, the way you tip back a child's head to look at a sore throat, and let three drops of it fall onto the back of her tongue.

"There," said the older sister, softly. "There. You're very fortunate."

Lyra looked up at her.

It was not the same woman. It could not be. The two kind women at Oakhaven had been Reach women, Greenroot women, and this one was from the Heights; she had the long vowels of the Heights and the pale eyes. But it was the same voice. It was the same rubric, word for word. She understood, sitting on the cot with oil on her tongue and the Pure Note coming through the black wall, that somewhere in the Church there was a book — a printed book, with articles and numbered paragraphs — that said *she is to be told that she is very fortunate, and reminded of it when she forgets*. And that two women in Oakhaven had read it, and two women in Sol-Luminary had read it, and had both done exactly what it said, with exactly the same kindness, and believed it.

"Thank you," said Lyra, in what was left of her voice.

It came out of her as a ruined whisper, and the younger sister flinched at the sound of it the way you flinch at a dog that has been kicked.

"Oh," the younger one said. "Oh, your *poor* throat."

And Lyra, who had sung the funeral office of the Reach twice down a reed to a dead man two nights ago with blood in her mouth, sat on a cot in the Basilica of the Pure Note and was, for a moment, very nearly undone by being pitied.

---

A physician came, after the sisters. A thin quiet man from the Heights with a silver mirror on a stem — the only mirror she had seen in Sol-Luminary, which he held well away from the lamp — and he looked down her throat for a long time, and listened to her breathe, and pressed his fingers along the front of her neck, and said nothing until he had finished.

Then he said: "The cords are scarred."

"I know."

"On both sides. Old and new. Some of it is from a wire — a very fine wire, through the skin of the fold, not the fold itself — and some of it is from use. Overuse." He put the mirror away in a velvet case and snapped it shut. "You have been singing on a torn voice. More than once. Recently."

She did not answer.

"If you sing on it again at full voice," said the physician, "it will tear through. Not torn. Through. You will have a whisper for the rest of your life, if you are lucky, and nothing at all if you are not." He looked at her. He had a tired, honest, unhappy face. "I am telling you because I have been told to tell the High Patriarch whether you are fit, and I am going to tell him that you are not, and he is going to tell me that the Light will mend what the Light requires. And I want you to have heard it from somebody who does not believe that."

Lyra looked at him for a long time.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't." He picked up his bag. "I've done this before. There were forty of them, once. I told the Patriarch then that the wire would hold for a hundred mornings and not three hundred." At the door he stopped. "I was at the Foundry on the last morning. In the gallery, with the physicians. I was the one they sent for when the first of the sopranos began to bleed." He did not turn round. "Nobody sent for me after that. There wasn't time."

---

Severin Ashe came to her himself at the third hour of the night, alone, with a lamp, and sat down on the floor.

She had not expected that. She had expected — she did not know what she had expected. The High Patriarch of the Choir-Church of the Pure Note; the man who had sent Ignatius Crane up the Greenroot road with a warrant and a censer; the man who preached unison as holiness and every second note as sin. Something tall. Something cold.

He was old and small and soft-faced, with white hair that stood up round his head like thistledown and a white cassock that was too big for him, and he came into her black room with a shuttered lamp in his hand and set it on the floor and lowered himself down beside it with a little grunt, an old man's grunt, one hand on the cot-frame, until he was sitting cross-legged on the bare boards at her feet like a boy at a story.

"There," he said, a little out of breath. "That's better. I can't bear to stand over people. It makes me feel like a bishop."

Lyra looked down at him.

"You're Lyra," said Severin Ashe. "I'm Severin. I don't use the rest of it, mostly. You mustn't get up." He looked up at her with his soft, clear, delighted eyes. "Oh, I've so wanted to meet you. Do you know, I've prayed for you every night since Harvestmonth? Every night. Since Ignatius wrote to me from the Reach and said there was a Cantor there who could sing the tending notes of the orchards. The old notes. The real ones. I'd thought they were all gone, with the reeds."

She did not say anything. She did not trust what would come out.

"And then the Light showed me," said Severin Ashe, and his face did something that she would remember a long time afterward: it *lit*. The way a child's face lights when it tells you a secret it has been keeping all day. "In prayer. I've asked it for seven years — every morning, every night — *what do you want, how do we bring you home* — and it never answered, not once, not a word, and I thought it must be because I wasn't worthy, and I kept on asking. And then this summer, it *answered*." He leaned forward. "It said: *a living chord*. Not a pure one. Not one note. A living chord, from a living throat, out of the old craft. The Light will hear a living chord where it would not hear a pure one." He sat back. "I wept. I'm not ashamed to say so. I sat in my chapel at the Organum and wept like a baby."

He beamed up at her.

And Lyra, who had read the first book of the Reach's catechism at eight years old in the orchard-school and understood it at twelve and stopped believing it at nineteen; who had stood in the Root-vault four hundred feet down and heard a god singing itself a lullaby it could no longer finish; who had held a slate up to Kaelen on the deck of the *Kestrel* that said *THEN SO CAN HE* — Lyra sat on the cot in the Basilica of the Pure Note and looked down at the High Patriarch of the Church, and understood that he was telling her the truth.

He had heard a voice. It had told him something. He believed it was the Light.

He had never once in his life heard a god.

---

"I've brought you the score," said Severin Ashe. "I wrote it out myself. I wanted you to have it in my hand, not a copyist's."

He took it out of his cassock: a sheaf of good paper, folded once, tied with a white ribbon. He untied it on his knee with old careful fingers and handed it up to her, and she took it, and unfolded it, and held it to the lamp.

She read music the way she read a face. Quickly, and all at once, and then slowly.

It was a single line. One voice, unaccompanied, written out in the old notation of the Reach — the orchard notation, the shape-notes, that she had not seen written down since her father's school-slate — with a long sustained drone beneath it marked *Pure Note, the choir, throughout*. It began low, on the Root. It rose. It went up in a long, slow, falling-and-rising figure, a fifth and a fourth and a fifth again, reaching, the way a hand reaches into dark water for something it cannot see, and at the top it held.

She knew it by the fourth bar.

She had known it, she thought later, by the first, and spent three more bars refusing to.

"It's beautiful, isn't it?" said Severin Ashe, from the floor. "I don't understand it, of course. I'm no musician. But it was shown to me in that form, very clearly, over several nights, and I wrote it down as it came. And the moment I had it, I knew it had to be one of the old Cantors. Only the orchard craft could sing it." He smiled. "Do you know it?"

Lyra looked at the paper.

It was the calling-song.

The calling-song of the Reach. The one you sing at a bedside in a fever-year when a child has gone too far down — gone quiet, gone still, gone cold, gone out past the place where a voice can reach them in the ordinary way — and you have one chance left, and it is this: you sing to them loud enough to be heard on the far side, and you call them by name, and you ask them, in the shape of the song, *are you still there?*

And sometimes they hear it. And sometimes they answer. And they come back — for a moment; for a breath; their eyes open and they know you and they say your name — and then they go. All at once. Further and faster than if you had let them alone. Because you called, and they answered, and answering took everything they had left.

Every Cantor of the Reach knew the calling-song. Every Cantor of the Reach was taught, at twelve, in the orchard-school, that you sing it only when there is nothing else, and only once, and only if you are prepared to be the last thing they hear.

Her father had sung it in 417 for a fevered child in the Hollow. He had sung it too loud, above the Narrow, because the child was too far down to hear anything quieter. The child had lived. Something in the dark outside the Hedge had heard him too.

She had sung it herself, in the water-meadow at Oakhaven, for Pim.

"Do you know it?" said Severin Ashe again, gently, as if she might not have heard.

"Yes," said Lyra.

---

She told him.

She did not decide to. It came out of her the way blood comes up a torn throat, a little at a time, in the ruined whisper that was all she had, and he sat on the floor at her feet with his soft white thistledown head tilted to one side and listened to every word of it, and did not interrupt once.

What the calling-song was for. What it did. What happened to the ones who answered.

"They come back," she said. "For a moment. And then they go all at once." She held the paper out to him. "You're not calling the Light home, Patriarch. You're asking it if it's still there. And it's dying. It's been dying since the Foundry. It's ringing down like a bell, slowly, and it'll take years. If you ask it — if I ask it, in this — it'll *answer*. It'll come back for a moment. And then—"

She could not finish. Her voice had gone.

Severin Ashe looked at the paper in her hand for a long time.

Then he reached up and took her hand, paper and all, in both of his. His hands were dry and warm and very old, and they did not shake at all.

"My dear," he said. "Oh, my dear. You're frightened. Of course you are. They burned you at Oakhaven — no, I know; I know what Ignatius did; I've read his report. They put a wire in you." His thumbs moved on the back of her hand. "But you see, that's why it has to be you. You know what it's like to be called back. You'll sing it as if you meant it."

"It will *die*—"

"It won't. Not if we hold it." He was smiling at her. Kindly. Patiently. The way you smile at a child who is afraid of the dark. "That's what the Pure Note is for. The whole Church will be holding the Light, on Lumina, with the Pure Note — every voice we have, every choir from the Heights, two hundred throats — so that when it hears you and comes back, it will have somewhere to come back *to*. Something to hold it. It won't go out, my dear. We won't let it." He squeezed her hand. "That's what the Church is for. That's what the Church has always been for. To hold it."

Lyra looked down at him.

*The Light wants to be held,* she thought. The first lesson. Every child in the Reach could say it.

She looked at the score in her hand. At the shape-notes, in the High Patriarch's own careful old copperplate. And at the margin, beside the fourth bar, where the figure reached its height and held — where someone had pencilled, very small, almost too small to read, a single annotation in a different hand.

*Here. Hold until it answers.*

Small, square, careful letters. An engineer's hand, or a mathematician's.

She had seen that hand before. At the Mausoleum of Chords, by lamplight, in a grey folder Kaelen had taken out of his shirt and laid in her lap and made himself read aloud to her, every word. A cover-sheet. Eight months before the Dawning Spire fell. *The object of Stage Nine is not extraction.* And at the bottom, in exactly that small square careful hand: *Engineer V. is not to be told.*

Severin Ashe followed her eyes to the margin.

"Ah," he said happily. "Yes. That came with it. The last night, after I'd written the rest. I woke and it was there, beside the bar, in the morning, in my own pencil. I don't remember writing it." He patted her hand. "That's how I knew it was the Light. It even tells us when to stop."

---

Ignatius Crane came in the morning, and his fire was going out.

She smelled him before she saw him. Glass-fire has a smell: hot metal and honey and something sharp underneath like a struck flint, the smell of amber Aether-Glass burning in a censer's cage. She had smelled it for five days at Oakhaven, coming up the Greenroot road, so thick in the streets that the Hollow's children had learned to hold their noses. It came under her door at the first bell of Stasisday, thin, and she sat up on the cot and knew who it was.

He did not come in. He stood in the doorway in his crimson, with the censer hanging from its chain in his right hand, and looked at her.

He had aged. It had been twenty-six days since the Root-vault. He looked as if it had been ten years. His face had gone thin and the skin under his eyes was dark, and his left hand — the one the Bleach had taken on the morning of the Fracture, the one he kept in a white silk glove — was not in a glove. It hung at his side bare, and it was white. Not pale. White, and translucent at the fingertips, the way the Glassmarket's people were translucent: she could see the shadow of the bones through it in the light from the hall.

And the censer.

At Oakhaven, the glass-fire in Ignatius Crane's censer had burned white-gold and as high as a man's forearm, so bright that you could not look at it, so hot that the chain glowed. It had been the brightest thing on the Greenroot road. It had held back hounds. It had burned the Hollow.

It was burning now the way a candle burns at the end of a long night. Low. Yellow at the edges. Guttering — she watched it gutter, as he stood there, sink down into the cage almost to nothing and then, after a moment, with an effort she could almost see, climb back up.

"Cantor," said Ignatius Crane.

She did not answer.

"I took you into the protection of the Altar," he said. "At Oakhaven. Under my warrant. You'll remember." His voice was the voice she remembered: deep, formal, a preacher's voice, built for a nave. But there was something gone out of the bottom of it. "I did it to keep you from the Arbiterium's men. I believed it was the Light's will. I believed you would be safe."

"I was put on a wire," said Lyra.

"Yes." He did not look away. "I know. I put you there."

The censer guttered. Climbed. Guttered.

"Is it him?" said Ignatius.

She knew what he meant. Everybody on the Keel Docks would have been talking since sunset: a Consecrated, on the foredeck of the Free Captains' ship, with an arm of glass. And everybody in the Church would know whose arm it was, if they had read Ignatius Crane's report from the Reach.

She said nothing.

"On the Greenroot road," said Ignatius, "he told me something. Before the Rite. I had been blaming him for seven years — for the Foundry, for the Light, for all of it — because he was the only voice I heard shout. One voice, over the forty. *Stop*. I thought he was the one who broke it." The censer guttered almost to nothing. "And he told me, on the road, with the Pure Note over us and his arm keeping time with my censer — he told me it did not break. He told me *it let go because they held*."

She looked at him.

"I have not slept since," said Ignatius Crane. "I have prayed. I have prayed every night. And for the first time in thirty years, Cantor, the Light has not answered." He looked down at his censer, at the yellow flame crawling in its cage. "I thought, at first, that it was my sin. That I had burned the wrong people and it had turned its face from me. And then I thought — what if it never answered? What if every time I heard it, it was me?"

He looked up.

"Is it him?" he said again. "I'd only like to know. I would like to know if he's here."

---

She did not tell him.

But she did not need to. She watched him read it in her face — he had read faces in confessionals for thirty years; he was better at it than Oona — and something in him settled, the way a man settles when a blow he has been waiting for finally lands.

"Then the Patriarch will want him," said Ignatius, quietly. "On Lumina. Beside you. The Light's own glass, come home to stand at its Altar. He'll see it as a sign." He turned the censer slowly on its chain. "Everything is a sign to Severin. He has never once heard the Light and so he hears it everywhere."

"And you?"

He looked at her for a long moment.

"I came to tell you something," he said. "The Patriarch doesn't know it yet. He will in an hour. I will tell him myself." He drew a breath. "The Arbiterium has a column on the Tramway. The Dread Arbiter's column, out of the Reach, with a writ for the man with the glass arm and everyone who has sailed with him. It will be at the foot of the Heights in four days. And the Arbiterium has written to the Altar to say that the Church's claim on you was stayed, not lifted, and that the Restoration is to wait on the Supreme Arbiter's pleasure." He paused. "Severin will not wait. He's waited seven years. He won't let a Justiciar with a column take the Light's vessel out of his hands four days before he can sing her."

Lyra understood a moment before he said it.

"He'll move it," said Ignatius Crane. "To the first dawn he can. Tomorrow. Dawnday." The censer guttered and did not, for a long moment, climb back. "He'll sing you on Lumina in the morning, Cantor. And I'll be there with the fire, because that's my office. And I don't know any more what I'll be burning."

He went away down the hall.

She sat on the cot and listened to the smell of him go, honey and flint and hot metal, fainter and fainter, until it was only the oil on her tongue and the Pure Note through the wall.

---

At noon a bell rang. It rang down through the whole of the Umbral Tiers from the Basilica's black tower, a deep slow bronze note that Lyra felt in the cot-frame, and she heard the Tiers go silent beneath it, level on level, the way the Keel Docks had gone silent when Vane laughed.

Then a voice. A herald's voice, from the tower, very loud, the way nobody spoke in the Tiers. Reading.

*— by the High Patriarch, in council, the Restoration of the Light shall be sung on the Altar of Lumina at the dawn of Dawnday the thirteenth of Ashfall, the Cantor of the Reach being found by the Light's grace prepared —*

She put her hands over her ears. It did not help. Nothing ever helped.

*— and the Consecrated of the Dawning Glass, Kaelen Vance, engineer, being by the Light's grace returned to the city of his consecration, is called to stand at the Altar beside her, and any who know where he lies shall bring word to the Basilica, and be remembered at the Restoration —*

The bell rang again. Once. And the Tiers stayed silent for a long time after.

---

Seraphina came to her at the dead hour.

Lyra was awake. She had not slept. She had been sitting on the cot in the dark with the hush-lamp whistling and her father's reed in its case in her two hands, turning it over and over, and the score of the calling-song on the floor at her feet where she had dropped it, face down.

The knight did not knock. She opened the door and stood in it, and she was not in her armour. She wore a plain white shift and soft boots, and her fair cropped head was bare, and she looked, without the white plate, like what she was: a young woman of twenty-three, broad-shouldered, tired. She had the Sun-Blade. She always had the Sun-Blade. It hung at her back in its black-wrapped scabbard, and in the dark of the cell Lyra could see, very faintly, a thin line of light along the black cloth where it had singed through.

"You're awake," said Seraphina.

"Yes."

The knight came in and shut the door and stood with her back against it. She did not sit. She did not look at Lyra. She looked at the floor, at the score lying face-down by the cot.

"You told him what it is," she said.

"Yes."

"He told me. At supper. He was very moved. He said you were frightened and that it was natural and that the Light would steady you." Seraphina's face, in the hush-lamp's tiny glow, was the struck coin again: still, set, revealing nothing. "Is it true? What you told him?"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've sung it," said Lyra. "At a bedside. More than once. And I've watched what came back, and how long it stayed." Her whisper cracked. "And my father sang it. In the Hollow. Too loud."

Seraphina said nothing for a long time.

---

"On Lumina," she said at last, "I have a part. In the Rite."

Lyra waited.

"The Altar is a disc of white stone on the shoulder of the mountain, facing the Spire. At the back of it there is the Eye. A mirror. Bronze, polished, forty feet across — the only mirror in the Heights that the Altar permits — set on a frame so that at dawn it takes the first sun and throws it onto the Spire's face. Into the crack." Her voice was very even. "At the height of the Rite, when the Cantor reaches the top of the song and holds, I'm to stand before the Eye and draw the Blade, and hold it up in the light. So that the Light will see its own bone at the Altar and know where it is being called *to*."

She reached up over her shoulder, without seeming to think about it, and touched the black-wrapped hilt.

"It is a splinter of the god," said Seraphina. "Did you know that? The Church says it was given to House Aubade by the Light itself, in the First Cycle. A shard of its own body. It's why the Light doesn't take me. At the Foundry, on the last morning — when it turned, when it came down off the Spire like a wave and went through the risers and the gallery and the — it went round me. Parted round me, like water round a post. Because I was holding its bone." Her hand stayed on the hilt. "Everything else it touched, it took. I stood in it with the Blade in my hands and watched it take everything else."

"Your sister," said Lyra.

The knight's hand closed on the hilt.

"She was on the first riser," said Seraphina. "I was in the gallery. Fifteen feet away. Above her and to the left, where the Altar's guard stood. Three hundred mornings I stood in that gallery in white armour with the Blade, and she stood on the riser with a wire in her throat and held the note, and I never once looked at her. Not once. It was the rule. The guard looks at the Spire. The guard does not look at the Choir." Her voice did not change at all. "And on the last morning, when it turned, when the light came down and started taking the risers — I heard her. I heard her voice change. I heard her understand something. And I heard the fire come, Ignatius's fire, because he'd been told to burn it clean if it went wrong. And I knew if I looked I would have to go to her, and if I went to her I'd have to let go of the Blade to take her hand, and if I let go of the Blade the light would take me too."

She stopped.

"So I didn't look," she said. "I stood there and held the Blade and looked at the Spire, the way I'd been told, and I heard her go over the rail. And I have not looked up since."

---

Lyra got up off the cot.

She did it slowly. She was stiff from sitting and her throat ached from the oil and the egg and the night of silence, and she was afraid, if she was honest, of the woman by the door, who was a foot taller than she was and carried a god's bone on her back. She got up anyway and crossed the black cell in three steps and stood in front of Seraphina Aubade, close, close enough to have touched her, and looked up into her face.

"Look up," said Lyra.

The coin did not move.

"She's up there," said Lyra. "Right now. On the chains. The Tiers leave her bread on the line. There's a thing on our ship that was born on her note, and it's been leaning toward her for three days. I haven't seen her. But Kaelen went up into the white last night to find her. If anyone has, he has." Her whisper was going. She made it go further. "You can't go to her. I know that. I'm not asking you to go to her. I'm asking you to look."

Seraphina Aubade stood with her back against the door of the cell and said nothing for so long that Lyra thought she had lost her.

Then the knight turned round and opened the door.

---

She took Lyra through the iron wall.

There was a door in it. Lyra had seen it from the nave: black iron, man-high, studded, set in the middle of the great black wall that cut the Basilica in two along the line. Nobody went through it, Seraphina had said. Except me.

The knight unbarred it with one hand. Behind it there was a short passage, black, and at the end of the passage another door, and when Seraphina opened that one, the light came in.

It came in white and sourceless and total, the light of the Bleach, from everywhere at once, and Lyra flinched back from it with her arm across her face — and Seraphina said, very quietly, "No. Behind me. In my shadow. Stay exactly behind me," and stepped through into it.

And her shadow fell back through the door onto Lyra.

Not away from the Spire, the way every other shadow in the Bleach fell. *Toward* her. Toward the light. A long dark shape on the white floor, cast back from the knight's body against all sense, as if the light, coming down onto Seraphina Aubade from every direction, could not find her and went round, and left a hole the shape of her behind it.

Lyra stepped into it.

She walked through the Lightward Nave of the Basilica of the Pure Note in the shadow of a woman who carried a god's bone, close behind her, close enough to put a hand on her back, and the light did not touch her. She could see it, all round them, white and taking. She could see what it had taken. The altar end of the nave had been sealed on the morning of the Fracture with the Church's whole congregation of the eighth tier inside it for the dawn office, and they were still there. In the pews. In white, translucent, seven years thin. A man with his hand raised in blessing. A row of children in surplices — she looked once, and Seraphina said *don't*, and she did not look again. An old woman on her knees at the rail with her face turned up toward the dome, toward the Spire, toward the sound.

They went up a stair in the wall to the bell-gallery.

---

It was a narrow stone balcony under the white dome, open to the south-east, with a low parapet, and beyond it the whole of the upper Bleach fell away in white terraces to the rim of the city, and beyond the rim, the Spire.

Lyra had seen it from the deck. She had not seen it from here. It filled the sky. A mile high, white, the black crack running down from its summit a third of the way to its root, and the mile of wrong light round it humming. And coming down out of the glare toward the city from rings set in its flank, at their long slants, the chains. Nine of them, black, a kilometre each. And one more — thinner, lower, almost level — running out from the city's rim to the south-east, across the gulf, to a white shoulder of mountain on the far side where something round and bronze caught what little light there was in the dead hour and gave it back.

"Lumina," said Seraphina, without looking. "The Altar. The Pilgrim Chain." Her voice had gone very low. "Which one?"

Lyra pointed.

She did not know. She guessed. The south-west, the nearest the Spire, where the chain came down at its long slant into a squat roofless tower on the rim. She pointed at it, and Seraphina Aubade stood at the parapet of the bell-gallery of the Basilica with the Sun-Blade on her back and the Bleach bending round her, and — very slowly, as if her neck had rusted — lifted her head.

And looked up.

Lyra watched her face. She did not look at the chain herself. She did not need to. She watched the knight's face, in the white light that could not find it, and saw it look up along the chain from the rim of the city into the glare, link by link, a hundred feet, three hundred—

And stop.

Something was moving on the chain.

Lyra looked then. She could not help it. A long way up, five hundred feet above the rim, perhaps more, where the chain went into the white so bright that it hurt: a fleck of black. Not on the chain. Beside it. Hanging in the glare beside the links, and then — as they watched — opening. Spreading. Two long black blades of shadow unfolding on either side of it against the white, edged with something that caught the light and turned it gold.

Wings.

Seraphina did not move. She did not make a sound. She stood at the parapet with her face turned up toward her sister for the first time in seven years, and her face did not come apart the way it had come apart for a moment on the Keel Docks. It did not do anything at all. It simply stayed looking, as if it had forgotten how to stop.

Then, far above them, the black shape on the chain turned its head. And Lyra could not have said how she knew, at that distance, in that light — but she knew. It was looking down. At the Basilica. At the bell-gallery. At the one thing in the whole of the white upper city whose shadow fell the wrong way.

---

They stood there until the dark shape folded its wings and went back up the chain into the glare and was gone.

Seraphina did not lower her head for a long time after. When she did, she looked down at Lyra, standing in her shadow, and her face was wet. It had been wet, Lyra thought, for some time. The knight had not done anything about it.

"At dawn," said Seraphina Aubade, "I'm to stand before the Eye on Lumina and draw the Blade, and hold it up so the Light can see it."

"I know."

"She'll come." It was not a question. "If you sing the calling-song on Lumina at dawn with two hundred throats holding the Pure Note under you, she'll hear it from the chain. She'll come."

"Yes."

Seraphina looked down at her hands on the white parapet. They were very still.

"Then I'll have to choose again," she said. "Won't I."

Lyra did not answer. There was nothing true she could say that was also kind, and she had been told twice now by the Church that she was very fortunate, and she was not going to say anything to this woman that came out of a book.

So she put her hand on the knight's back, flat, between the shoulder-blades, just below the black-wrapped hilt of the Sun-Blade, the way she put her hand on Kaelen's glass. And held it there. And they stood together at the parapet in the Bleach, in a shadow that fell the wrong way, and watched the place on the chain where the wings had been, until the sky behind the Spire began, very faintly, to go grey over the shoulder of Lumina.

Below them, in the black tower, a bell began to ring the third hour, and the procession.

Under it, through the stone, the Pure Note went on.

## CHAPTER TWELVE
### Three Seconds

> *A struck fork held near a second fork of the same pitch will set it ringing. Held near a fork a little out of true, it will beat: loud, soft, loud, soft, the two notes falling in and out of step. No two forks were ever made exactly alike. Listen for the beat. It tells you everything.*
>
> — *The Vance Sequence*, introductory note to the first revision

*The sixth anchor-house, the Bleach. Stasisday the twelfth of Ashfall.*

---

"I didn't wait three hundred mornings," said Kaelen. "I waited three seconds."

Solenne Aubade looked up at him in the shadow of the chain.

He had not known he was going to say it. He had carried the answer for seven years and never once said it aloud to anyone — not to Lyra at the Mausoleum, when he told her everything else; not to the deck-beam where he wrote the names. He had said it to himself, in the hut outside the Hedge, at night, which meant the thing that wore Tobin Ashgrove's voice had heard it, and an old man in a white room had heard it. Nobody who could look at him had.

"I calibrated the bore to the specification I was given," he said. "I didn't ask what it was for. I was very good at not asking. I thought it was a draw — a deeper draw than any we'd done, a long slow siphon at the Anchor's own pitch, and the choir was there to hold the pitch steady while the bore took. That's what it said on the sheet. That's what I believed for two hundred and ninety-nine mornings." His voice was flat. "On the last morning I was on the third platform with the fork, checking the drift, and the Spire *changed*. I heard it change. Under your note. Something in it that had been — pushing, for three hundred mornings, the way a held thing pushes back — stopped pushing. And I heard it ask."

He stopped.

"And I knew what it was," he said. "I knew in the first second. I'm an engineer; I've heard a load come off a bearing ten thousand times. I knew it was letting go. And I spent the next two seconds being *sure*. Making certain. Checking the fork. Because if I shouted and I was wrong, I'd have stopped the Rite of Pure Unison on the three hundredth morning in front of the High Patriarch and the Supreme Arbiter and the Seven Chairs, for nothing." He looked at her. "Three seconds. Then I shouted. And by then it had already gone."

Solenne said nothing.

"That's all," said Kaelen. "That's the answer. I didn't wait three hundred mornings. I didn't know for two hundred and ninety-nine of them. I knew for three seconds, and I spent them making sure."

---

She laughed.

It was not a laugh he ever wanted to hear again. It came out of her two voices at once: the copper one, bright and flat and rung out on its one pitch like a cracked bell; and the other one, the void, a low hollow nothing underneath it that sounded like the inside of a well. The copper laughed. The void did not. And between them, grinding, the sound of a woman who has been waiting seven years for an answer and has just been given one that is worse than the one she wanted.

"*Three seconds,*" she said. "*Three.*"

"Yes."

"*I held it for three hundred mornings.*" The copper rang. "*Three hundred. With a wire through me. And it was three seconds.*"

She sat down on the edge of the nest, quite suddenly, the way Tobiah Marsh had sat down on the Salt Stair with a gaff-hole in him. Her black wings folded down round her on the robes and the blankets and the children's drawings, and Mote, which had been curled in the middle of the nest purring, unfolded and leaned toward her, and she put out one long hand without looking and laid it flat on the soft pale back, and the thing went still under her palm.

"*Do you want to know what it said?*" said Solenne. "*What it asked?*"

Kaelen's breath stopped.

He had wanted to know for seven years. He had wanted it more than he had wanted anything; more than he had wanted the glass out of him, more than he had wanted Lyra to forgive him. He had heard it ask. He had heard the shape of it, the rising inflection of a question in a voice the size of a mountain, under the note. He had never heard the words. He was not sure there were words. He had stood on the third platform with his fork in his hand and understood that a god was asking something, and had not understood what, and had spent three seconds being sure it was asking anything at all.

"Yes," he said.

Behind him, in the broken doorway of the anchor-house, Vesper had gone very still. Mags, who could hear nothing, had seen his face, and come in from the doorway, and stood beside him.

And five hundred miles east, he knew, an old man in a chair had lifted his head.

Solenne knew it too. He saw her look at the arm — at the sack, and the cyan light coming through it — and he saw her decide to say it anyway.

---

"*It asked,*" said Solenne Aubade, "*if it was us.*"

She was looking at the chain where it came through the ring in the floor beside the nest. Not at him.

"*Not like that. Not in words. It doesn't have words; you know that, you've had its glass in you for seven years. But I was on the first riser, with my face to the Spire, and I'd been singing its note to it for three hundred mornings, and I was nineteen and I'd been in the Sky Choir since I was six and all I had ever done in my life was listen. And I understood it the way you understand a dog, or a baby. Not the words. The* shape."

Her copper voice was very soft now. The void under it was steady.

"*It said:* is it you? *Like that. Like somebody who's been waiting at a door a very long time, and hears a step on the stair.* Is it you? Have you come? *And under that, so tired, so — glad. Ten thousand years it had been singing that note, by itself, holding up the light. Holding it up for everybody. And for three hundred mornings forty of us stood on a riser in front of it and held its note for it, perfectly, the way it had been holding it alone since the First Cycle. And it heard us. And it thought—*"

She stopped. Her hand on Mote's back had closed into a fist in the soft pale stuff. Mote did not seem to mind.

"*It thought we were the ones it had been promised,*" said Solenne. "*The ones who were coming to take a turn. So it could rest. And it asked us. Is it you? Have you come? Can I put it down now?*"

Kaelen did not move.

"*And I understood,*" said Solenne. "*I was the only one. The others were — they were singing; they were nineteen and twenty and twenty-two and they had wire in their throats and they were singing, they weren't listening. I was listening. I heard it ask. And I understood what it was asking, and what it would mean if we said yes, and I knew we couldn't hold it. Forty girls. Forty girls with copper in our necks. We couldn't hold up the light of the world. We couldn't hold it for an hour.*"

Her voice cracked. The copper half of it went up, very high, and broke.

"*And I held the note,*" she said.

"*I held it. That was my job. I was the lead, I was the first riser, I was the one they all took the pitch from, and I* held it. *Because I didn't know what else to do. Because if I stopped, all forty of them would stop, and the Rite would fail, and the Patriarch — and my sister was in the gallery — and the Spire was asking, so gently,* is it you, *and I* held the note. *And that was yes.*" She looked up at him at last. Her face was wet and she was not doing anything about it. "*Holding the note was yes, engineer. That's what it heard. It asked if we'd come to take it, and I held its note for it one more time, and it heard yes. And it let go.*"

---

Nobody said anything in the roofless tower for a long time.

The light lay on the broken walls round them, white and sourceless and humming. The chain came down through the open top of the tower at its long slant out of the glare, black, enormous, and went through the great iron ring in the floor beside the nest, and Kaelen could feel it through the soles of his boots, the long low strain of it, a hundred years of holding a city. Above them, a long way off, the Unison's note went on, a wire through the white.

*It let go because they held.* He had said that to Ignatius Crane on the Greenroot road. He had thought he understood what he was saying.

"Three seconds," said Kaelen, at last. "And three hundred mornings."

"*Yes.*"

"Between us."

"*Yes,*" said Solenne.

She was looking at him with her wet furious tired face. And Kaelen understood, standing in the shadow of the chain at the top of the Bleach with the light of the god he had helped to kill pouring into his arm, that she was not furious at him. Or not only. She had been waiting seven years in the dark to find the other one. The only other person alive who had heard it ask. Not to forgive him. Not to blame him. Just to say it out loud to someone who would know what she meant.

He sat down on the stone floor at the edge of the nest, because his legs had stopped wanting to hold him, and the arm in its sack lay across his knees ringing her note.

"It's still asking," he said.

She went very still.

"Under the ring-down. I hear it in the glass. At night, mostly. Very faint." He did not look at her. "*Is it you.* Over and over. Like somebody who's been told the step on the stair was the wrong house, and keeps on listening anyway."

Solenne Aubade put her face in her hands.

---

They stayed in the anchor-house through the day, because there was nothing else they could do.

The sun came up over the shoulder of Lumina an hour after Solenne's answer, and when it cleared the peaks the Bleach changed. The sourceless white that had lain over everything all night was joined by a second light — the ordinary sun, real light, with direction, with heat — and the two of them together were more than anything could stand in. Kaelen watched the line of the chain's shadow on the floor of the anchor-house narrow as the sun climbed, the black bar across the stone going thinner and thinner, and watched the four of them draw in toward it until they were sitting in a row along it like birds on a branch, Vesper and Mags and Kaelen on the floor and Solenne in her nest with her wings folded tight round her, all of them inside a stripe of shadow no wider than a door.

At noon it was the width of a man's shoulders.

He watched the edge of it come across the back of his right hand, and took the hand away, and put it in his lap, and sat very still.

Vesper and Solenne talked. Not much. He did not think they liked each other. They watched each other the way two cats watch each other across a yard. But they were the only two people alive who had been down there — into the dark under the world, the Hush, the thing outside the Octave — and come back up, and in the long white hours of the day, in that narrow stripe of shade, they talked about it the way soldiers talk about a war, in short sentences, without looking at each other, while Mote lay in the nest between them purring.

"It let you go," said Solenne, once.

"I think it let me go so I'd carry *that* out," said Vesper. She nodded at Mote. "I don't know what it let you go for."

"*It didn't let me go.*" The copper rang flat. "*I climbed.*"

Vesper looked at her for a long time.

"How long?"

"*I don't know. There isn't time down there. You know that.*" Solenne's long fingers moved on the blades of her folded wing. "*I fell all the way down, still singing. I kept singing. I didn't know how to stop; the wire wouldn't let me. And the dark — it* listened. *The way the Spire listened. It came close and listened to me sing, and something in it was born out of the listening, a little soft thing, and I hated it, and I climbed. Up. The way I'd fallen. I climbed with my hands in the dark until my hands were raw to the bone and something grew out of my back instead, and then I climbed with that.*" She opened one wing a little and looked at it. The gold along its edge moved like the edge of a coal. "*And I came out on the Spire. On the outside, in the crack. In the light. And it didn't take me.*"

"Why not?"

Solenne looked at her wing.

"*Because I'd been down there,*" she said. "*And the light doesn't know what I am any more. Nothing does.*"

---

The Unison came at the second hour after noon.

Kaelen heard them before anyone. He heard the note change — not the pitch, never the pitch; the *direction*. All day it had been a wire through the white, a long way off, everywhere at once. And at the second hour after noon it stopped being everywhere and started being somewhere, and the somewhere was getting closer.

He sat up.

Solenne was already on her feet in the nest. She had heard it at the same moment. Her wings came half open, and her face changed in a way he had not seen it change: not rage, not grief. Something much older and simpler than either. The face of an animal that has been hunted for seven years and hears the hunt.

"*They heard me,*" she said. "*This morning. When I—*"

When she laughed. When she wept. In her two voices, in a roofless tower, at the top of the Bleach.

"Or the arm," said Kaelen. "Or the animal. Or all three."

"Doesn't matter," said Vesper. She was up too, with her hooks out. "How many?"

Kaelen listened. He was very good at listening. He had made a career of it, once.

"All of them," he said.

---

He drew it for Tobiah afterward, on the back of a tank-chart, because Tobiah asked and because he needed to draw it to stop seeing it.

The anchor-house was a ring of broken wall forty feet across, its roof long gone, its walls standing between fifteen and twenty feet high and jagged along their tops. It stood on the very rim of the city, built out over the drop on stone corbels, so that the south-west quarter of its floor had nothing under it but the whole height of Sol-Luminary and the gulf. In the middle of the floor was the ring — iron, ten feet across, sunk into the stone — and through the ring came the sixth chain, down out of the white at its long slant from the south-west, filling the open top of the tower like the bar of a sundial, and going on down through the floor into the city's frame. Its shadow lay across the floor from south-west to north-east, a black stripe a yard wide at that hour. The nest was in that stripe, in the angle between the ring and the wall. On the north-east side, the city side, there was a doorway with no door, and beyond it a short ramp down to the white streets of the top tier.

They came up the ramp and over the walls at once.

Forty. He counted. Forty white bodies in their bleached choir-robes, coming up the ramp to the doorway in four ranks and at the same time coming over the broken top of the wall on every side, climbing it the way they had climbed the house in the Glassmarket, slowly, steadily, without hurry, forty white faces appearing along the jagged rim of the anchor-house all at once like a crown, and their forty open mouths singing the one note, and it was so loud inside that ring of stone that he felt it in the fillings of his teeth.

And then they struck it.

---

He had never heard them strike it. He had not known they could. All night and all day the Unison's note had been a held thing, sustained, endless, forty breaths taken in rotation so that it never broke. Now, in the instant they had the anchor-house surrounded, all forty of them took breath at once — he saw forty white chests rise together — and *struck* the note. Not held. Struck. Forty throats hitting five hundred and twenty-eight at the same instant, hard, like forty hammers on one anvil.

And his arm locked.

He felt it go. Every crystal in the glass falling into step at once. It had been locked three times before — by the proctors' presses at Whisper-Grove, by the Aegis's chord in the Cloister, by Sirena at forty feet — and each time it had been a single struck tone at the arm's own pitch, and each time it had been like this: the arm becoming a casting, a solid bar of light-glass from shoulder to fingertip, with no more give in it than a bell, ringing.

It locked cocked. He had been getting to his feet with it braced against the floor, and it locked there, bent at the elbow, the hand flat on the stone, and he was on one knee with a hundredweight of rigid glass bolted to his shoulder and the floor, and he could not get up.

The Unison was not looking at him.

Forty white faces, turned all at once, to the same angle. Toward the nest. Toward the woman standing in it with her black wings open and the light bending gold round their edges.

They struck the note again.

And Solenne Aubade's mouth opened, and the copper half of her voice — the half that had been wired, the half that could only hold one pitch — rang out of her with them.

---

He understood what they wanted then. He had not understood until he heard it.

They did not want to kill her. They had never wanted to kill her. The Tiers called it hunting and Brenna Corliss called it hunting, and neither of them had heard it from inside a ring of stone with the Unison's note in their teeth.

They wanted her back.

She had been the first riser. The lead. The one they all took the pitch from. Forty on the riser on the morning of the Fracture, and she had gone over the rail, and the rest — and the men the Church had wired after them — had been singing ever since without her, holding the note with nobody to take it from, a choir with no lead, a mind with a hole in it the shape of one woman. And for seven years they had been looking for her. Not to punish. To *finish* themselves.

They struck the note again, and her copper voice rang with them, and he saw what happened to her face.

It went smooth.

The grief went out of it. The fury went out of it. The wet tracks down her cheeks were still there but the face behind them went quiet and easy and empty, the way the Unison's faces were quiet and easy and empty, the way the market-woman's face in the Glassmarket was quiet, reaching for a coin forever. Her eyes went to the same angle as theirs. Her chest rose with theirs. The void half of her voice — the dark low nothing underneath the copper — went thinner, and thinner, drowned in forty-one throats of one bright note.

They were taking her in.

"*Solenne*—" said Kaelen.

It came out of him as nothing. The note swallowed it.

---

Vesper went into the ring.

She did not run. She walked. She walked out of the stripe of the chain's shadow into the white light at the middle of the anchor-house floor with a hook in each hand, between the nest and the nearest rank of the Unison, and stood there in the full light, and Kaelen saw the light come onto her and begin, at once, to take the colour out of her coat and her hands and the side of her face that was turned toward the Spire.

And her quiet went out round her.

He saw it go. Not with his eyes. With the note. Within ten feet of her — a circle of floor with Vesper at its centre — the Unison's note went *out*. As if somebody had laid a hand on that part of the air. And the seven or eight white bodies of the Unison who were standing inside that circle—

Stopped.

He saw it. He would see it for the rest of his life. Seven white faces inside Vesper's quiet, suddenly unable to hear the note. Unable to hear the thirty-three others. Unable to hear anything at all, for the first time since the morning of the Fracture. And their faces — their smooth, quiet, easy faces — *broke*.

Not into anything. Into themselves.

A woman of perhaps twenty-five, near the doorway, looked down at her own white hands as if she had never seen them. A man — there were men; the Church had made more after the Rite, out of tenors and basses from the cathedral choirs, and wired them too — a big man near the wall with a cantor's barrel chest put his hands to his throat, to the copper, and Kaelen saw his mouth open in a scream that the quiet swallowed whole. A girl, a very young one, sat down on the stone where she stood and put her arms over her head.

Seven people. Alone. Each of them alone in their own head for the first time in seven years, in a white ruin at the top of a dead city, with a wire through their throats.

And then the other thirty-three turned their heads — all at once, to the same angle — toward Vesper, and struck the note at her, and her quiet held, barely, and Kaelen saw her rock where she stood as if it had hit her like a wave.

She could not hold it for long. He could see that. She could hold seven of them out of it and not forty, not for long, not in the full white light of the Bleach with the colour going out of her by the second.

And in the nest, Solenne's face was still smooth.

---

His arm was ringing.

It had been ringing all along, locked and rigid against the floor, cocked at the elbow. And he heard, for the first time, under the Unison's struck note — because he was on one knee with his ear six inches from the glass and the clerk in the back room never stops — that it was not ringing the same note.

It was flat.

He had known that. It was the oldest rule of the glassed and he had learned it at Whisper-Grove with his face in an inch of lit water: *a tone struck at a glassed limb's own pitch locks it rigid, and leaves it ringing flat for days.* Flat. A hair flat. Not a different note. The same note, five hundred and twenty-eight, sung by something that had been pushed a fraction out of true and could not quite get back.

Not a different note. Almost the same one.

*A struck fork held near a second fork a little out of true will beat.* The first line of the first revision of the Vance Sequence. He had written it at twenty-two, in the Conservatory, to explain to first-year students why no two bore-heads ever drew the same. *Loud, soft, loud, soft, the two notes falling in and out of step.*

The Unison was not forty people. It was forty forks, struck together and held in step. Forty voices locked to each other because each of them could hear the other thirty-nine and pulled to match. Exactly the same note. Perfect unison. That was the whole of it — that was the thing that made them one mind instead of forty frightened people: that they were *exactly* in tune, and could hear that they were.

And there is nothing in the world that breaks a unison faster than a note that is almost, but not quite, the same.

He looked at the chain.

---

It came down through the open top of the tower out of the white at its long slant and went through the iron ring in the floor three feet from where he knelt. A kilometre of black adamantine link. Each link as long as a ship's boat. It held up a city. It ran from the rim of Sol-Luminary to a ring set in the flank of the Spire of Light, and from the Spire, through the city's frame, to the other eight chains, and through them to every stone and beam and tier of the whole hanging city.

It was the largest sounding-bar in the world.

He could not lift the arm. It was locked to the floor, cocked, rigid, a hundredweight of glass bolted to his shoulder. He could not swing it, could not strike with it.

But the floor was not the only thing it could touch.

He got his right hand on the iron ring. He got his boots under him. And he did the one thing a man can do with a locked limb, which is to move everything *else*: he put his whole weight into his right hand and his legs and *dragged* — pulled his body round the locked arm the way a door swings round a hinge, the shoulder-joint where flesh met glass screaming, the cracked ribs grating, the cocked glass scraping across the stone toward the ring — eighteen inches, two feet—

And laid the flat of the locked glass hand against the chain.

---

The arm went into it.

Not the hand. The note. Five hundred and twenty-eight, a hair flat, ringing out of a hundredweight of Dawning glass that could not stop ringing — into a kilometre of black adamantine link that had been singing its own low tired note under a city for a hundred years.

The chain took it. He felt the chain take it the way he had felt the Root take his hand in the quarry: something enormous turning, slowly, toward a touch. And then he felt it carry. Down the link, through the ring, into the floor, into the city's frame, out along every beam and buttress of the anchor-house — and up through the soles of forty pairs of white bare feet standing on that floor.

The Unison struck the note.

And the floor beneath them gave it back to them a hair flat.

He heard it happen. He would describe it to Tobiah afterward as best he could and fail, and Tobiah, who had stood on a rail at Oakhaven calling pitch numbers, would understand anyway and go white. The struck note went up from forty throats, perfect, exact — and the stone and the iron under them answered it with a note almost the same, coming up through the bone of their feet and their shins and their spines into their skulls, and the two notes met.

And beat.

*Loud.* Soft. *Loud.* Soft. A great slow throb, like a heart, like an engine missing on one cylinder, the two notes falling in and out of step with each other three times a second — *wah, wah, wah* — and every time they fell out of step, for the smallest part of a second, the forty voices could not hear that they were in tune.

They tried to hold it. He watched them try. Forty heads turning, all at once, at the same angle, searching for the pitch. Finding it. Losing it as the beat came round. Finding it. Every throat pulling at the note to match the others and the others not quite there, a hair off, because the floor under them had made them a hair off. The rotation of their breathing stumbled. Two of them breathed together. Three.

The note wavered.

It had not wavered in seven years.

---

And Solenne Aubade's face came apart.

He saw it from the floor with his glass hand on the chain. The smoothness went out of it all at once, the way ice goes off a pond in a thaw: a crack, and then everything. The grief came back. The fury came back. She stood in the nest with her wings open and her mouth open and her copper voice still ringing the note, helplessly, because the wire would let it do nothing else — but she was *in* her face again. She could hear the beat. She could hear the choir stumble.

And she did the only thing she had that the wire could not stop.

She sang with the other voice.

The void. The dark low hollow thing under the copper that had no pitch, that was not a note at all but the absence of one, the sound of the far side of the world. She had been singing over it, all this time; it had been underneath every word she spoke, a floor of nothing. Now she let the copper go — let it ring by itself on its one bright pitch — and put her whole breath, her whole body, into the other.

It came out of her and into the anchor-house and through the beating note like a black blade through white silk.

And the Unison broke.

---

Not all at once. A unison does not break all at once. It breaks the way a ship's hull breaks on a reef: a plank, and then the next, and then the sea is in.

The seven inside Vesper's quiet were already broken. Now the beat and the void took the rest of them. He watched it go round the ring of white faces like a wave going round a harbour wall — face after face after face losing the angle, losing the smoothness, coming apart into fear and grief and confusion and *themselves*. A woman clapped her hands over her ears. A man fell to his knees. Two of them turned, at last, not together, to look at each other — just two people, looking at each other — and he saw them see each other's faces for the first time in seven years, and he saw them recognise each other, and he saw the woman put out her hand.

The note stopped.

For the first time since the morning of the Fracture, at the top of the Bleach, in the white heart of Sol-Luminary, the Unison's note stopped.

The silence that came down afterward was not Vesper's quiet. It was not the Hush. It was just silence — ordinary, enormous, terrible silence, the silence of a room after a bell has stopped — and in it, forty people in bleached choir-robes with copper wire through their throats stood on the floor and the walls of a roofless anchor-house at the top of a dead city and looked at each other, and at their hands, and at the woman with black wings in the nest, and did not know what they were.

And his arm came free.

He felt it go. The lock broke as the note broke; every crystal of it falling out of step at once, the glass going from a casting back to an arm, and the weight of it came off the chain and he fell sideways onto the stone with it across his chest, ringing — still a hair flat, and would be for days; just ringing, a long fading shiver, like the last of a bell.

---

Some of them died.

He had not known they would. Nobody had. Sable said afterward, when he told her, that nobody had ever broken a Unison and so there was no entry for what happened after, and she sat with that for a long time and then wrote one.

Four of them. Out of forty. He saw the first one go — the big man with the cantor's barrel chest, who had put his hands to his throat in Vesper's quiet. He was standing by the wall when the note stopped, and he looked round at the silence, and he took a breath — on his own, out of rotation, the first breath of his own in seven years — and his face went very surprised. And then he sat down against the wall, the way Solenne had sat down on the edge of the nest, the way Tobiah had sat down on the Salt Stair, and his white chest did not rise again.

Seven years held up by a note. And when the note stopped, there was nothing left in him that knew how to go on without it.

Three more, in the next minute. A woman. Two men. They sat down, or lay down, or simply stopped, where they stood, quietly, without a sound. The others did not seem to understand what was happening to them. Neither did Kaelen. He lay on the stone with his arm across his chest and watched them go and could not do anything about it, and thought, very clearly, the way the clerk in the back room always thought: *I did that. With a beat. Three times a second.*

The rest ran.

Not all. Most. Twenty-six, he counted later, in the white streets below the anchor-house, scattering, alone, each one running a different way, in a different direction, at a different speed, out of the doorway and down off the walls and away across the white terraces of the Bleach like a flock of birds a hawk has gone through. He did not know where they would go. He did not think they knew. They had not been anywhere on their own in seven years.

Ten stayed.

---

They stayed in the anchor-house. They stood in a loose ragged half-ring at the edge of the chain's shadow, out in the white light, ten bleached people in their choir-robes, and they looked at Solenne.

She was standing in the nest with her wings half-folded and her face wet and her hands hanging at her sides. She was not singing. The copper half of her voice had stopped when the note stopped; there was nothing left for it to hold.

One of the ten came forward.

Kaelen knew her. He had seen her on the roof in the Glassmarket, the night before, alone, crouching at the parapet: the thin one, the translucent one, the one who could speak a word to a note. She came across the floor toward the nest, out of the light into the chain's shadow, slowly, and stopped in front of Solenne Aubade, and looked up at her.

She opened her mouth. Fought the wire for a note. Found one.

*Lead.*

Solenne did not move.

The woman shut her mouth. Fought the wire again. Another note, higher, thinner.

*We —*

*— are —*

*— sorry.*

And Solenne Aubade came down out of the nest onto her knees on the stone floor of the anchor-house, in the shadow of the chain that held up the city, and put her arms round the thin bleached woman with the copper in her throat, and folded her black wings round both of them, and the light bent gold round the edges of the wings and could not get in.

The other nine came into the shadow, one at a time, and knelt down round them.

Kaelen lay on the floor with his arm across his chest and did not look away. You stay for what comes after. His father had said that to him once, at seventeen, at the foot of a gallows in the Oakhaven square, and he had thought it was about the dead. It was not. It was about what you owed the people you had done a thing to.

He stayed and looked.

---

Vesper had gone white.

Not pale. White, along one whole side: the side she had turned to the Spire when she walked out into the light to hold her quiet over seven of them. Her coat had gone the colour of bone on that side. Her cropped dark hair had a white streak through it from the temple back. Her left hand — the one she had held out, with the hook in it — was white to the wrist, white as Brenna Corliss's cheek, and when she lifted it to look at it, the dark claws at its fingertips were the only colour left.

She looked at it for a while.

Then she put it in her pocket and came and crouched beside Kaelen on the floor and looked at his arm.

"Can you feel it?"

"Yes."

"Can you move it?"

He moved it. It moved. It hurt more than anything had hurt since the Chime, the whole shoulder a fire where flesh met glass, but it moved.

"You put it on the chain," said Vesper.

"Yes."

"The whole city just rang," she said. "Did you know that? Every stone of it. A hair flat. I heard it go out along the chain into the city and down through every tier, all the way to the bottom, for about four seconds. Like a bell under the floor." She looked down at the arm, at the cyan light coming through the sack. "Every Tier in the dark will have heard it. Every hammock. Every hush-lamp."

He had not thought of that.

"And him," said Vesper. She nodded at the arm. "He'll have heard that too. Your old man in his chair."

Kaelen shut his eyes.

He could feel it. Under the fading shiver of the glass, under the pain. A listening. Very close. Very interested. As if somebody five hundred miles away had leaned forward in his chair for the first time in seven years.

---

They went down at dusk. Kaelen and Mags.

Vesper did not come. She stood in the doorway of the anchor-house with her white hand in her pocket and Mote leaning against her shin, and watched them go, and said she would stay the night, and did not say why, and did not need to. In the nest behind her, in the shadow of the chain, Solenne Aubade sat with ten of her choir round her under her folded wings, and none of them was singing.

The line, when they reached it, had moved.

Kaelen saw it before Mags did. He had walked up from it the night before, and he knew where it fell on the ninth tier: across the walkway's end-ladder, just above the gutted house, where the Tiers left their offerings. It was not there now. It was lower. Not much. Two feet, perhaps, three. The bread and the cups and the children's drawings that had been in the shade at the line's edge last night were in the white now, and they had gone pale, and the paper of the drawings had gone thin as onion-skin and was coming apart in the evening breeze.

The Light was ringing down. A struck bell. And somebody had just rung the whole city like a bell, a hair flat, for four seconds.

He stood on the walkway and looked at it for a long time.

Then he went down through the Umbral Tiers in the dark, ladder by ladder, past the hammocks and the hush-lamps, and every face that saw him go by — every face in every cracked shutter, every father with a child on his chest — knew who he was. He could see it. They had heard their city ring. They had heard something read from the Basilica's tower at noon, too; he could see it in their faces, though he did not yet know what it was.

Nobody stopped him.

---

Vane was on the *Kestrel*'s deck, with Sable, and a slate.

They had been busy. He could see it in their faces before they showed him anything. They had spent the day in Brenna Corliss's chandlery and the Keel Docks and the lower Tiers, asking, listening, paying, and they had what Vane called the shape of it and Sable called the entries, and they gave it to him on the slate by the binnacle lamp, in Sable's small even hand, because there was no longer any point in not saying it aloud and Sable said aloud was less accurate.

*RESTORATION AT DAWN. LUMINA. THE PILGRIM CHAIN FROM THE RIM AT THE SEVENTH ANCHOR. PROCESSION AT THE FOURTH HOUR.*
*THE CANTOR GOES UP IN THE CAGE. SERAPHINA WITH HER.*
*TWO HUNDRED CHOIR ON THE ALTAR TO HOLD THE PURE NOTE. THE PATRIARCH. IGNATIUS AND THE FLAMBEAUX.*
*THE SCORE IS THE CALLING-SONG OF THE REACH. (CORLISS HAS A COPY FROM THE BASILICA COPYIST. 4 SOV.)*
*YOU ARE CALLED TO STAND BESIDE HER.*

He read it twice.

The calling-song.

He knew what it was. Everybody in Oakhaven did. Lyra had sung it in the water-meadow for Pim, at full voice, above the Narrow, with the Wearer whistling the falling fifth from the reeds, and he had seen her face the next morning and known what it cost her.

*Is it you?* he thought. *Have you come?* Asked again, on purpose, at dawn, by a Cantor of the Reach in front of two hundred voices holding the god's own note under her. And the god, ringing down, tired, still listening at the door for a step on the stair—

It would answer.

And Vaelen had known it would. Vaelen had written it in. Kaelen was as sure of that as he had been of anything since the third platform.

"Engineer," said Vane.

Kaelen looked up.

"You've got that face," said Vane. "The one from the Salt Stair. The one where you've already done the arithmetic and you don't like the answer." He was grey and stitched and swaying a little where he stood. "Say it out loud. Everybody else is."

---

So he said it out loud.

He said it on the deck of the *Kestrel* in the cradle at the bottom of Sol-Luminary, to Vane and Sable and Mags and Pennick and Hetty Bram, and to Tobiah propped up in the hatch, and to the Aegis Sentinel standing at the bow in the dark with the faint light in its helm-slot fixed on him; and to an old man five hundred miles east who was listening to every word, very closely, leaning forward in his chair.

He said that the Church had called him to stand at the Altar beside her, and that he was going to go and stand there.

Nobody said anything.

"They want the Light's own glass at its Altar," said Kaelen. "So the Light will see it. That's the Patriarch's reasoning. It's wrong, but it's his." He looked down at the arm. "It's also the only way any of us gets within a hundred feet of her on Lumina at dawn without two hundred choristers and a chapter of Flambeaux between us. So I'm going to walk up to the Basilica tonight and knock on the door."

"And then what?" said Vane.

Kaelen looked at him for a long time.

"I don't know," he said. "I didn't know on the third platform either. I'm going to stand next to her, and listen, and when I hear it start to answer I'm going to have three seconds." He looked at the arm. "I'd like not to spend them making sure."

Vane said nothing for a moment. Then he laughed — the short one, the one out of the side of his mouth.

"Well," said Vane. "That's a plan. That's very nearly a plan. Sable, write that down, it's the first time he's ever admitted not knowing something." He turned to the slate. "Right. Then the rest of us need to be at the bottom of that chain when you come down it. Because you're coming down it, Engineer. Both of you. Fast. With the Church behind you and the whole of the Bleach in front. And I've been looking at that chain all afternoon, and I'll tell you what I've counted—"

---

He left them counting.

He left Vane and Sable and Mags bent over the slate at the binnacle, and Pennick shouting softly about lift and weight, and Hetty Bram making tea nobody would drink, and he went to the bow where the Aegis stood in the dark.

It had not moved since Lyra went up the ladder. It stood exactly where it had stood when she held up her hand and told it *stay*: nine feet of riveted iron in the deep shade of the cradle's rim, its great hands at its sides, its helm turned toward the ladder. Inside its chest, very faintly, it was still chiming. The same few notes of the same hymn. The alto line. Over and over, the way a child hums to keep from crying.

He stood beside it for a while.

"I'm going to get her," he said.

The chiming stopped.

The helm turned. Slowly, with a long soft hiss of steam somewhere in its neck, the smooth curved plate with the slot across it came round from the ladder to him, and the faint light inside the slot fixed on his face.

He did not know what it understood. Sable said there was a child in there — a ten-year-old boy, an alto whose voice had not yet broken, whose number was stamped in the bronze beside its core and whose surname Sable's own hand had made unreadable, and who was waking up, slowly, inside iron. He did not know how much of the boy was awake. He did not know whether he could hear, or understand, or whether the thing that turned its head was him or only the logic-gates round him, stencilled on its chest with three words. *DEFEND THE SINGERS.*

"I'm going to get her," he said again. "Tomorrow. Down the Pilgrim Chain. The captain will be at the bottom with the ship. I want you there too. At the bottom of the chain, at the rim. When she comes down, you take her. You understand? Whatever's behind her. You take her and you get her to the ship."

The Aegis looked at him for a long time.

Then, very slowly, it lifted one enormous iron hand — and laid it flat on his left shoulder, on the place where flesh met glass, so lightly that he barely felt the weight of it. The way Lyra laid her hand on the glass. The way you lay a hand on somebody to show them you have heard.

Inside its chest, it began to chime again. A different line. Not the alto. Higher. He did not know it.

He found out later, from Sable, what it was. It was the first line of Hymn 9 of the Cloister of the Lost Choir — the one Sable had sung in a doorway to a ring of empty risers, the one that had brought it to its knees. The first line was the soprano's. It was the lead.

---

He walked up to the Basilica of the Pure Note alone, at the dead hour.

Up through the Tiers, ladder by ladder. Past the hammocks. Past the hush-lamps whistling their small high notes. Past the black tower, where the Pure Note came out through the stones as it had come out all day and all night, held by the Church's choristers in their shifts in the dark. Through the black square in front of the Basilica's west end, where the shade lay thick as wool and the great black doors stood shut under their black lancet windows.

He did not wear the sack. He had taken it off on the *Kestrel*, at the bow, and given it to the Aegis to hold. The arm hung at his side bare, Dawning glass from shoulder to fingertip, cyan, lit from inside, with the vein of sea-green moving through it like a river under ice; and the glass came up out of his collar along the bone to an inch from his throat; and in the absolute dark of the Umbral Tiers at the dead hour, he was the brightest thing in Sol-Luminary below the line.

Every shutter on the square was open a crack. He could feel them watching. He did not look.

He went up the black steps to the black doors, and lifted his right hand, the one that was still flesh, and knocked.

He waited a long time.

The doors opened. Not wide. One leaf, a few feet, onto the dark of the nave and the Pure Note pouring out of it like heat out of an oven. And in the gap, with a censer hanging from its chain in his right hand and his left hand white and bare at his side — with the glass-fire in the censer burning low and yellow and guttering, and climbing, and guttering again — Ignatius Crane looked at him.

They looked at each other for a long time, the Inquisitor and the engineer, in the doorway of the Basilica of the Pure Note, at the bottom of the line.

"*You,*" said Ignatius.

"Yes," said Kaelen.

The censer guttered.

"I was called," said Kaelen Vance. "To stand beside her. I've come."

## CHAPTER THIRTEEN
### The Eye

> *At the height of the song the Bearer of the Dawn shall stand before the Eye and draw, and hold the Blade up into the morning, and shall not lower it until the Light has answered.*
>
> — *Order of the Restoration of the Light*, written by the High Patriarch, rubric eleven

*Mount Lumina. Dawnday the thirteenth of Ashfall.*

---

The guard looks at the Spire. The guard does not look at the Choir.

Seraphina Aubade had learned that at fifteen, in the long white hall of the Altar's knights at the top of House Aubade, from an old woman with one eye who had stood guard at the Sun-Altar for forty years and never once turned her head. You learned it the way you learned to breathe. You stood in your white plate with the Blade at your shoulder and your eyes on the Spire, and whatever happened behind you or below you or beside you, you did not look. Because the guard is there for the Light. Not for the people who sing to it. They have their part, and you have yours, and if you look at them, you will want to help them, and if you help them, you will let go of the Blade.

She had been good at it. She had been the best of her year. She had stood in the gallery of the Siphon Foundry for three hundred mornings in white plate with the Blade at her shoulder, fifteen feet above and to the left of the first riser, and she had not looked once.

She had looked last night.

She walked up the Pilgrim Chain to Lumina in the fourth hour of Dawnday, ahead of the cage, in the full light of the Bleach, and the light parted round her and went on round her, and her shadow fell back the wrong way along the chain onto the black canopy of the cage behind her, and she did not look at anything except the Altar ahead.

But she could not stop seeing it. A black fleck on the chain, five hundred feet up in the glare, opening its wings.

---

The Pilgrim Chain was not like the other nine.

The nine were the city's bones. They came down from rings in the Spire's flank at their long slants and took hold of the city's rim and held it, and nobody walked on them who was not mad or a Tiers child on a dare. The Pilgrim Chain was a road. It ran almost level out from the city's south-east rim at the seventh anchor-house, across the gulf, a kilometre of black adamantine link rising very gently toward the white shoulder of Mount Lumina on the far side, and along its top, bolted into every link, there was a walkway: an iron deck six feet wide with a rail on either side at waist height, polished by four hundred years of pilgrims' feet. In the old days the city had walked it every dawn. You walked out over the gulf with the Spire on your right hand shining, and the peaks below, and the Altar ahead with the Eye throwing the morning back into the sky, and you sang.

Nobody had walked it since the Fracture, except the Church, and only under canopies.

The cage ran on the walkway behind her. A long low carriage of black iron with a black silk canopy over it on poles, pulled up the chain on a cable from the winch-house at the Altar end, moving very slowly, at a walking pace. Inside it, in the shade of the black silk, the people the Light was not allowed to touch.

The High Patriarch, in white, on a cushioned bench, with his white thistledown head bare and his hands clasped in his lap, smiling.

Two sisters of the Altar.

Lyra Ashgrove, in a white shift they had put on her at the third hour, with her hands in her lap and her father's reed in its case at her belt, which nobody had thought to take from her. She had not said a word since the bell-gallery. She sat very straight on the bench opposite the Patriarch and looked out through the gap in the black silk at the gulf going by beneath them, and Seraphina did not know what she was thinking and did not let herself guess.

And the Consecrated.

He walked. They had wanted him in the cage — the Patriarch had been distressed, had begged him, *the Light's own glass, my son, the Light must not touch you before the Altar* — and he had said, in a low flat voice, that the Light had been touching him for seven years and he would walk. So he walked behind the cage on the open deck in the full white glare, between two Flambeaux in crimson with their censers lit, with Ignatius Crane at his shoulder, and his left arm bare.

Seraphina had seen him on the gantry. Seven years ago, she had not looked at the Choir, but she had looked at the Spire, and the gantry was on the Spire, and there had been a man on the third platform who never once stood still — up and down, up and down, with a tuning-fork on a chain and a satchel of gauges — and she had watched him the way you watch the one moving thing in a still room.

She had not known his name. She had not known, until the Keel Docks, that he had lived.

He was walking in the Bleach with nothing over the arm, and the light was going into it.

She could see it. Everybody on the chain could see it. The arm was Dawning glass, cut from the Spire on the morning she had stood in the gallery, and the Bleach was the Spire's light, still ringing down; and the arm *drank*. It was brighter every time she looked back. Cyan, lit from inside, so bright now that it threw its own shadow — a shadow that fell away from the arm, toward the Spire, the wrong way, like hers. And along his collarbone, where the glass came up out of his open collar toward his throat, it was so bright she could not look at it.

He did not seem to notice. He walked with his eyes on the cage. On the gap in the black silk, where Lyra Ashgrove sat looking out at the gulf.

---

The Altar of Lumina was a disc of white stone two hundred feet across, cut into the shoulder of the mountain, facing the Spire across the gulf.

It had been white before. It was whiter now. Everything on Lumina was. The mountain stood a little over half a mile from the Spire's flank, well inside the mile of wrong light, and the Bleach lay on it the way it lay on the upper city, sourceless and total and humming. The great steps that went up to the Altar from the chain's end were white. The pillars round its rim, which had been gilded, were white. The old pilgrims' chapels cut into the mountainside above it were white, and the people who had been in them on the morning of the Fracture were still in them, at their prayers, seven years thin. She did not look at them. She had never looked at them.

The Church had built canopies. Rows of them, black silk on black poles, in rings round the Altar's rim, and under them, in the shade, the choir.

Two hundred. She had seen them go up in the night, in their shifts, in their white robes, up the Pilgrim Chain under moving canopies like a procession of black mushrooms. Every choir in the Heights. The cathedral choir and the Altar choir and the House choirs of all the old families, Aubade among them, and the children's choirs of the Basilica school. Two hundred throats, in ten rings, under black silk, and not one of them wired. They were holding the Pure Note. They had been holding it since the first hour, in rotation, the way the Basilica's nine held it, so that it came up off the Altar of Lumina like heat off a road, five hundred and twenty-eight, enormous.

They were paler than they had been when they went up. She could see that too. Even under the black silk. Their faces were lighter. Their hands. The edges of their white robes had gone a white that was not the white of linen.

The Church said the Light did not take those who sang to it. The Church had said a great many things.

And at the back of the Altar, on its frame of white iron, facing the Spire, the Eye.

Forty feet across. Bronze, once, and polished to a mirror so perfect that it had been the only mirror the Altar permitted in all the Heights; the only second light. It was white now too, mostly — a pale dull white like the inside of a shell — but at its centre, where the Altar's clerics had polished it every day for seven years with oil and ash, there was still a circle of bright bronze the size of a cartwheel. Facing the Spire. Tilted, on its great frame, to take the first sun as it came up behind Lumina, and throw it across the gulf into the crack.

The crack in the Spire's flank was black. It was the only black thing for a mile, except the chains, and her sister's wings.

---

They put Lyra on the Vessel's stone.

It was a round dais in the centre of the Altar, a step high and ten feet across, and on it there had always been a singer at the dawn office. A child, usually: the best treble of the Basilica school, chosen each spring, who sang the first line of the morning hymn alone before the Choir came in. Seraphina had stood on it herself once, at nine, before the Altar took her for a knight. She remembered the stone under her bare feet, cold even in summer, and the Spire in front of her shining so bright that the edges of everything swam.

Lyra Ashgrove stood on it barefoot in her white shift and looked at the Spire.

The Patriarch stood at the edge of the dais, below her, with his hands clasped and his face shining up at her the way a father's face shines at a child's first recital. He had given her the score at the cage. She had not taken it. He had folded it back into his cassock without seeming to mind.

"You won't need it, of course," he said, gently. "You know it. You told me so."

Lyra did not look at him.

And the Consecrated came across the Altar between his two Flambeaux, with Ignatius Crane at his shoulder, and the Patriarch turned and saw him and his whole soft face lit like a lamp.

"*Oh*," said Severin Ashe. "Oh, my son. Oh, look at you."

He held out both his hands. The Consecrated did not take them. He walked past the Patriarch as if he were not there, and stepped up onto the Vessel's stone beside Lyra Ashgrove, and stood there, at her left hand, with his glass arm at his side between them burning cyan in the white.

And Lyra, without looking at him, reached out and took it.

The glass hand. Not the other. Seraphina saw her do it. She saw the Cantor's small brown fingers close round the great cyan fingers of the arm, which were as long as her whole hand, and hold on. She saw his face. She looked away from it, the way you look away from a thing that is not yours.

"Beautiful," whispered the High Patriarch, and wept.

---

Ignatius Crane came and stood beside her at the Eye.

That was his place in the Order. Hers was before the Eye, at the centre, where she would draw. His was at her right hand, with the fire. *That the Light may know it is welcome,* the rubric said, *and that nothing else may come to the Altar while it answers.* The glass-fire was the Church's wall. Hounds would not cross it. Mimics would not cross it. Whatever came out of the dark to a song at full voice would meet the Flambeaux's fire at the Altar's rim and turn back.

His censer was guttering.

She had known Ignatius Crane all her life. He had been her confessor when she was a child, in House Aubade, before the Rite. He had been the brightest flame in the Church — they had said that of him, everyone, the Patriarch most of all: *Ignatius burns brightest*. She had seen his fire hold back a pack of hounds on the Lumen road in the Screaming Year, white-gold and as high as a man, so that the beasts went round the procession in a wide circle whining like dogs in a storm.

It was the colour of a tallow candle now. Low in its cage. It sank as she watched, almost to nothing; and he looked down at it, and she saw his lips move, and it climbed back, slowly, a little. Not as high as before.

"It's going out," she said. Quietly. Nobody else could hear them under the Pure Note.

"Yes."

"Why?"

He did not answer for a long time. He was looking past her, across the Altar, at the man and the woman on the Vessel's stone. At the arm.

"Because I can't remember what I believed," said Ignatius Crane, "on the morning I lit it."

---

The sun came up behind Lumina at the sixth hour, and the Eye took it.

She felt it before she saw it. A warmth on the back of her neck, through the plate — the first real warmth anybody on that mountain had felt since the Fracture, the warmth of the ordinary sun coming up over the peaks — and then the Eye, behind her, on its frame, *caught*.

The circle of bright bronze at its centre took the sunrise and threw it.

It went past her shoulder over the Altar in a single solid bar of gold — gold, real gold, the colour of the sun, the first colour Seraphina had seen in the Bleach in seven years — across the gulf, half a mile, straight as a ruled line, and struck the Spire of Light in the black of the crack.

The crack lit.

The two hundred voices under the black canopies swelled. They had been told this was the moment; the Pure Note came up off the Altar so loud she felt it in her teeth.

And the High Patriarch of the Choir-Church of the Pure Note turned to the Vessel's stone with his face full of the light and his eyes full of tears, and lifted both his hands, and said, in a voice that carried over all two hundred throats as if it had been made for nothing else:

"*Sing.*"

---

She did not know, then or afterward, why Lyra Ashgrove sang.

Sable told her, later, on the ship, that Vane had asked the same thing and Lyra had written on a slate that she did not know either. That she had stood on the stone with Kaelen's hand in hers and the gold bar across the gulf and the two hundred voices under her and she had thought about not singing, and about the Flambeaux at the Altar's rim with their censers lit, and about the ship in the cradle at the bottom of the city and the nineteen thousand in the Tiers; and she had thought about her father, in the Hollow, in 417, singing a child back from the dark too loud. And that she had opened her mouth because the song was in it, and once a Cantor of the Reach has the calling-song in her mouth over somebody who is going, she sings it.

She sang.

It came out of her low. On the Root. A hundred and seventy-four, the tending note, the note of the orchards and the Hollow and the birthing rooms, under the Pure Note's five hundred and twenty-eight like a root under a tree. Her voice was what Seraphina had heard on the Keel Docks: torn, cracked, ruined, a sheet ripped slowly down the middle. It did not carry. Under two hundred voices it should not have been heard at all.

It was heard.

Seraphina would not afterward have been able to say how. She was standing before the Eye with her back to the sun and her face to the Spire, and she heard the Cantor's ruined voice come up off the Vessel's stone and go out over the Altar and the gulf, under the Pure Note and through it, the way a thread goes through cloth. It was not loud. It was *clear*. It was the clearest thing on the mountain. It rose. It went up, slowly, in the long reaching figure of the score — a fifth, a fourth, a fifth again — the way a hand reaches down into dark water for something it cannot see.

And across the gulf, in the lit crack, the Spire *listened*.

---

She felt it in the Blade first.

It was on her back, in its black-wrapped scabbard, the way it had been every day of her life since she was sixteen. A splinter of the god. She had stopped noticing its weight years ago, the way you stop noticing your own hand. And as the Cantor's voice went up the figure of the calling-song, the Blade on her back went warm.

Then hot.

Then — she felt it through the scabbard, through the plate, against her spine — it began, very faintly, to ring.

She had never heard it ring. Not once in seven years. Not at the Foundry. It had been silent on her back through the whole of the Fracture, while the light came down off the Spire and went through the risers like a wave and parted round her. And now, on Lumina, at dawn, with a Cantor of the Reach singing the calling-song on the Vessel's stone, the bone of the god on her back began to hum, five hundred and twenty-eight, very low, like a struck bell a long way off answering another.

On the Vessel's stone, the Consecrated's arm was ringing too.

She saw him turn his head toward the Spire. She saw his face. She had seen it once before, from the gallery of the Foundry, on the third platform, seven years ago: the face of a man who has heard something and is listening, and making sure.

"*It's listening,*" said Severin Ashe, very softly, beside the Vessel's stone, with his hands clasped and his face streaming. "*Oh, it's listening. It hears her. It's coming home.*"

---

And under the Pure Note, under the calling-song, under the ringing of the Blade, Seraphina heard it.

She had never heard it. She had stood in the gallery of the Foundry for three hundred mornings and never heard anything but the Choir and the bore and the wind. She was not glassed. She was not a Cantor. She was not a soprano who had stood on the first riser and listened to a god for three hundred mornings until she could understand it the way you understand a dog or a baby.

But she was holding its bone.

It came up out of the Blade and into her spine and into her skull. Not a sound. A *shape*. Enormous, and slow, and very, very tired. A shape like the rising at the end of a sentence. Like somebody who has been sitting in a dark house a very long time, alone, listening to a step on the stair, and has heard the step stop outside the door.

*Is it you?*

She stood before the Eye and shook.

*Is it you? Have you come back?*

And across the gulf, the Light began to answer.

---

She saw it come off the Spire.

The way she had seen it on the last morning of the Rite, from the gallery, without looking: a change in the white. The light that lay on everything, sourceless, humming — it *gathered*. It drew together the way water draws together before a wave. All round the Spire, for a mile, the overexposed air thickened, and brightened, and the brightness began, very slowly, to move.

Toward Lumina.

Toward the Altar. Toward the calling-song and the Pure Note and the bar of gold the Eye was throwing into the crack. Toward the Blade on her back, which was ringing now so loudly that she could feel her teeth buzz in her jaw.

It came across the gulf like a tide coming in over a sand-flat. A wall of white, slow and enormous, a white so total that she could not see the edge of it, only the edge of what it had already taken: the peaks below the gulf going pale as it crossed them, the snow on them vanishing into the white, the rock vanishing, the shadows in the gullies thinning and failing like breath on a glass.

And behind it, as it came, she saw the city.

Sol-Luminary, hanging from the Spire's flank on its nine chains, half a mile to her right across the gulf. And the line across its face — the hard straight edge where the Bleach ended and the shade began, ruled across the city like a tide-mark — *moving*.

Down.

She watched it go. One tier. The hanging houses of the ninth tier going from black to grey to white as the edge of the light came down over them. Two tiers. She could see — she could not see, at that distance, she would never be sure what she could see — she thought she could see things moving on the walkways. Small dark things. Running. Down the ladders. Away from the line as it came down on them like a blind being lowered over a window.

*The Tiers,* she thought. *The hammocks.*

"*Yes,*" breathed the High Patriarch. "*Yes. Yes. Come home.*"

---

Her sister came down out of the white like a dropped knife.

Seraphina did not see her come. Nobody did. One moment the Altar was the Altar — the Choir under their canopies, the Flambeaux at the rim with their censers, the Patriarch at the Vessel's stone with his hands raised, the Cantor singing, the white tide coming across the gulf — and the next there was a sound like a sail splitting, very loud, directly overhead, and a shadow went across the whole of the Altar from the Spire's side to the mountain's, and the light *bent*.

She looked up.

She had looked up last night for the first time in seven years. It was easier the second time. It was not easy.

Solenne was above the Altar.

Her wings were open to their full span, twice her height, black, plated, edged with gold, and the white light coming across the gulf hit them and *curved* — went round them the way it went round Seraphina, the way water goes round a stone — and where it curved it turned gold, so that her sister hung over the Altar of Lumina at dawn in a ring of fire, with a long black shadow falling from her wings across the white stone.

The Choir saw her.

Two hundred faces under the black canopies turned up at once, and the Pure Note — for the first time since the first hour — *broke*. Not stopped. Broke, the way a wave breaks: a ragged foaming crest of voices going up into a cry, children's voices, high, from the Basilica school's ring. *The angel. The black angel. Look—*

"*Abomination!*"

The Patriarch's voice, cracking like a boy's. He had turned from the Vessel's stone with his hands still raised and his face, for the first time since Seraphina had known him, not soft. "*Ignatius! The fire — the Light is answering — the fire, Ignatius, before it reaches the Altar—*"

Beside her, the Inquisitor lifted his censer.

---

She saw his face as he lifted it. She would remember it as long as she remembered anything; longer than her sister's wings, longer than the Spire. Ignatius Crane, the brightest flame in the Church, who had swung his censer through three hundred mornings of the Rite and ordered the Choir burned when the light turned; who had thrown the soprano on the first riser over the rail into the dark with his own fire, because he had been told to burn it clean if it went wrong. Lifting the censer on its chain, the way he had lifted it ten thousand times, toward the black shape over the Altar.

And seeing her face.

Solenne was looking down at him. Not at the Altar. Not at the Cantor or the Patriarch or the Consecrated or the Choir. At him. From thirty feet up, in her ring of fire, with her black wings open and the copper glinting in her throat, she looked down at the man who had burned her.

The censer was in the air, at the top of its swing, on its chain.

The glass-fire in its cage went out.

Not guttered. Out. Seraphina saw it go: the low yellow flame sinking down into the cage, and down, and down, and not climbing back. A thread of smoke. The amber glass in the cage going dark, a dull lump of brown like a cold coal. The censer swung down on its chain past Ignatius Crane's knees, empty, and swung back, and hung.

He did not try to light it again.

He stood beside her before the Eye with the dead censer in his hand and his white bleached left hand hanging at his side, and looked up at the woman he had burned, and his face did nothing at all. Then he went down on his knees on the white stone of the Altar of Lumina, slowly, the way the market-people's hands moved, as if something in him had been trying to finish that gesture for seven years.

---

"*SERAPHINA!*"

The Patriarch. He had come across the Altar to her, stumbling, an old man in a white cassock too big for him, with his face streaming and his thistledown hair wild and his hands reaching for her — reaching for the Blade on her back. "*Now! Now, child, now — draw — it's coming, it's answering, the song's at the height — the Light must see its bone — the Order — DRAW—*"

She looked at the Vessel's stone.

Lyra Ashgrove was at the top of the calling-song.

She had reached it. The long reaching figure of the score had gone up — a fifth, a fourth, a fifth again — through the Pure Note and the broken cry of the Choir and the sail-crack of the wings, and arrived where the score said it should arrive, at the top, where the figure held. And she was holding. On the Vessel's stone, barefoot, in her white shift, with the Consecrated's glass hand in hers and her head thrown back and the cords standing out in her throat, the Cantor of Oakhaven was holding the top note of the calling-song of the Reach with a voice that had been torn twice and was tearing now, Seraphina could hear it tearing, a sound like a sheet ripping very slowly down its whole length—

*Here. Hold until it answers.*

And the white tide was halfway across the gulf, and the line on the city had come down three tiers, and the Blade on her back was ringing so hard she could feel it in the roots of her teeth.

She reached up over her shoulder and drew.

---

The Sun-Blade came out of its scabbard into the morning and the morning *went to it*.

She had never drawn it in the Bleach. Never once in seven years. She had carried it wrapped in black cloth because when it was bare the light came to it the way iron filings come to a lodestone, and she had not wanted to see that, and she had been told not to. Now she drew it, and held it up before the Eye in both hands, point to the sky — a long straight blade the colour of dawn, not metal, not glass, something translucent and gold and alive, a splinter of the god's own body — and the light came.

Not the white tide. Not yet. The light that was already on the Altar. The sourceless white that lay on everything — on the stone, on the canopies, on the Choir's faces, on her armour — *lifted* off it, all of it at once, and streamed toward the Blade in her hands like smoke going up a chimney. The Altar went dark round her. The canopies went dark. The faces of two hundred choristers went from white to their own colour, all at once, in the ordinary shade of an ordinary dawn, and every one of them gasped. And all of that light went into the Blade, and the Blade *blazed*, and across the gulf the white tide felt it and came faster.

*Is it you?*

*Here,* said the Blade. In her spine. In her skull. *Here. I'm here. Come.*

And above the Altar, in her ring of fire, Solenne screamed.

---

She screamed in both voices. The copper and the void. *STOP — stop it — stop asking it — STOP ASKING IT —*

And she came down.

Not at Seraphina. At the Vessel's stone. At the Cantor, at the top of the calling-song, holding — at the voice that was asking. Seraphina saw it all at once and could do nothing about any of it: her sister folding her wings and dropping out of the air like a stooping hawk, black and gold, straight at the small barefoot figure on the stone with her head thrown back and her ruined voice tearing. Talons — she had talons, her bare feet had grown hooked and black in the dark — reaching.

The Consecrated moved.

He did not let go of Lyra's hand. He turned on the stone, with her hand still in his glass one, and put his body between her and the falling shape, and brought his *other* arm up — the flesh one, the right one, the one the Bleach had taken to the wrist — and caught Solenne Aubade across the chest as she came down, and the two of them went over backward together off the Vessel's stone onto the Altar in a tangle of black wings and white light and his shout.

Lyra did not stop singing.

She stood alone on the stone with her hand still reaching out where his had been, and held the note.

And Solenne came up off the Consecrated in a single convulsive beat of her wings, and the beat took her up and back, and the edge of one wing — the gold edge, the moving molten edge — hit the bronze frame of the Eye behind Seraphina's shoulder with a sound like a bell struck with a hammer, and she spun. Seraphina saw it. Saw her sister's wing catch on the iron and fold wrong and her sister spin in the air and drop, crooked, out of control, past the Eye's frame, past the Altar's rim, over the edge of the white stone where the mountain fell away into the gulf.

Over the rail.

---

Seraphina had the Blade in both hands, raised, before the Eye.

She had been told: *and shall not lower it until the Light has answered.*

She had been told, at fifteen, by an old woman with one eye: *the guard looks at the Spire. The guard does not look at the Choir.*

She had been told, at sixteen, by nobody, by herself, in the gallery of the Foundry, with the light coming down off the Spire and her sister's voice changing on the first riser: *if you look, you will have to go to her. And if you go to her you will have to let go of the Blade to take her hand. And if you let go of the Blade, the light will take you too.*

The white tide was a hundred yards from Lumina.

Her sister's hand came up over the edge of the Altar's rim. Just the hand. Long-fingered, scarred, scrabbling at the white stone for a hold that was not there, and sliding.

Seraphina Aubade looked.

---

She let go of the Sun-Blade.

She did not throw it. She did not lower it. She opened her hands, both of them, at the top of the raise, the way you open your hands to let a bird go, and turned, and ran.

She heard it fall behind her. She heard it hit the white stone of the Altar with a sound like nothing she had ever heard, a high pure clean note like a struck glass, and bounce, and skid, and she heard the Patriarch scream. She did not look back. She had looked back once in seven years and she was not going to waste it on a sword.

Six strides across the Altar to the rim. She went down on her belly on the white stone at the edge with her armour ringing and reached over, both arms, as far as she could reach, and her sister's hand slid off the stone into the air—

And Seraphina caught it.

---

She had her by the wrist. Over the edge of the Altar of Lumina, with the gulf beneath them falling away for two thousand feet to the white peaks, and the mountain's face sheer below, and her sister hanging from her two hands by one wrist with her wings crumpled and broken-looking against the rock and her face turned up.

Seraphina did not have the Blade.

And the light found her.

She felt it come. The white that had parted round her for seven years — that had gone round her at the Foundry like water round a post — came onto her now as it came onto everything else: sourceless, total, taking. She felt it on the backs of her hands where they gripped her sister's wrist. She felt it on her neck. She felt it go into her, the way it had gone into the Consecrated's arm on the chain: not pain. Ease. A soft white loosening, as if all the colour in her were a held breath and she could let it out now.

She looked down at her hands.

They were going pale.

"*Sera,*" said Solenne. From below. In both her voices, the copper and the void. The first time she had heard her sister say her name in seven years. "*Sera — let go — the Blade — you'll—*"

"No," said Seraphina.

She did not let go.

---

Behind her, the light stopped coming.

She felt it before she understood it. The white on the backs of her hands — taking, soft, loosening — paused. Hung there. The way a wave hangs at the top before it breaks.

And over her head, over the Altar, over the white tide halfway across the last hundred yards of the gulf, over the Choir's broken cry and the Patriarch screaming at the Blade where it lay on the white stone shining, she heard the calling-song *change*.

She could not see the Vessel's stone. She was flat on her belly at the Altar's edge with her face over the gulf and her sister's wrist in her hands. She would only know afterward, from the Cantor's slate, on the ship, in a hand that shook so badly it was barely readable, what had happened behind her.

That Lyra Ashgrove had been holding the top note of the calling-song — *Here. Hold until it answers* — with her voice tearing through, and the Light coming across the gulf to answer, and the Blade blazing in the knight's hands to show it where. And that she had seen the knight drop the Blade and run. And that the Light, coming across the gulf toward the god's bone at the Altar, had felt the bone fall — had *lost* it, for one moment, the way you lose a voice in a crowd — and hesitated.

And in that hesitation, Lyra Ashgrove stopped asking.

She let go of the top note. She did not let it fall. She brought it *down*: a fifth, the falling fifth, the oldest interval in the orchard craft, the one she had whistled with Pim and sung in the water-meadow and sung four hundred feet down in the Root-vault until a god let go of a question and began its lullaby again from the beginning. She brought the calling-song down off its height onto that, and then onto the Root, a hundred and seventy-four, under the Pure Note, the tending note, the note you hum into a child's back at night when it has woken frightened and there is nothing wrong, nothing at all, go back to sleep.

And then she sang the second note.

Not five hundred and twenty-eight. Not the Pure Note. Not unison. A note *against* it — under it, close, a hair off, the interval the Church had called a sin for four hundred years, the second note, the one that does not match, that makes a chord instead of a line. She sang it under two hundred voices holding the god's own pitch, in a voice that was tearing through as she sang, and the two notes met, and the Pure Note and the Cantor's second note made something together that had not been heard on the Altar of Lumina since the First Cycle.

A harmony.

*Not yet,* it said. Seraphina felt it in the stone under her belly; she would swear to it. *Not us. Not now. You don't have to answer. Go back to sleep.*

---

The white tide stopped.

She felt it stop. Everybody on Lumina felt it. A hundred yards out over the gulf, the great slow wall of gathering light that had been coming across from the Spire like water over a sand-flat *stopped*, the way a wave stops at the top of a beach — hung there, enormous, white, shivering — and then, very slowly, went back.

Not all the way. It did not go all the way back. It drew back across the gulf toward the Spire the way the sea draws back, leaving the peaks it had crossed paler than they had been, the snow gone, the rock gone grey. It drew back off the city. Seraphina did not see that either; she would see it from the chain, later, and wish she had not. It drew back off Lumina too, off the Altar and the canopies and the choir, the way the sea draws back off a beach before it comes in again, so that for a little while the Altar lay in the ordinary light of an ordinary dawn. It would not last. It drew back, and settled, and lay down again round the Spire in its mile of wrong light, humming, ringing down, slowly, the way it had been ringing down for seven years; and already, at the Altar's rim, the white was creeping back.

The crack in the Spire went dark.

The Eye — behind her, she heard it — cracked. A long ringing split, like a bell breaking, as the bar of gold it had been throwing across the gulf went out and the bronze, after seven years of polishing and one dawn of the god's attention, gave way across its whole face.

And on the Vessel's stone, the calling-song stopped, and the Cantor's voice stopped with it, all at once, in the middle of the second note.

Not because she had finished.

Because there was no more of it.

---

Seraphina pulled.

She did not know where the strength came from. She would think about that for a long time. She was flat on her belly on the edge of a mountain in plate armour with her sister's whole weight hanging from her two hands over two thousand feet of air, and her hands were pale to the wrist from the light, and she had nothing in them. No Blade. No god's bone. Nothing to part the light round her or make her anything but a woman in the wrong armour at the edge of a drop.

She pulled anyway.

Solenne's free hand came up and found the rim. Her talons found the rock below it. One crumpled wing came up and over the edge, and then the other, and then she was coming up onto the white stone of the Altar of Lumina on her knees and her hands with her wings dragging behind her like broken sails, and Seraphina had her by both arms, and they were kneeling face to face at the edge of the drop, in the ordinary light of an ordinary dawn, with the gulf at their backs.

They looked at each other.

Seven years. Fifteen feet. A gallery and a riser. A hand that had not opened.

Solenne's face was wet. So was hers.

"*You looked,*" said Solenne Aubade. In both her voices. Very quietly.

"Yes," said Seraphina.

"*You let go of it.*"

"Yes."

Her sister looked past her, over her shoulder, at the Altar. At where the Blade lay on the white stone, shining, ringing very faintly, with the High Patriarch of the Choir-Church of the Pure Note on his knees beside it in his white cassock, weeping, with his old hands held out over it, not quite touching it, as if he were warming them at a fire.

"*They'll kill you for that,*" said Solenne.

Seraphina looked down at her own hands. They were pale. Not white; not the Bleached's white. A shade lighter than they had been. Like hands that had been in water too long. They were holding her sister's arms, and they were not letting go.

"Probably," she said.

---

Somebody was shouting behind them.

The Flambeaux. Seraphina heard it come back into the world all at once, the way sound comes back after a quiet — the Choir under their canopies in a hundred broken pieces, crying, singing, praying, children wailing in the Basilica school's ring; and over it, men's voices, crimson voices, the Inquisition's, at the Altar's rim with their censers lit — not guttering, *theirs* had never guttered — coming across the white stone.

*Seize them. Seize the angel. Seize the Bearer. The Blade — the Blade is on the stone — the Cantor — the Consecrated —*

She looked round.

The Consecrated was on his feet on the Vessel's stone with Lyra Ashgrove in his arms. She had gone down when her voice went; Seraphina saw that now. She was lying across his chest like a child, limp, her head back over his glass arm and her mouth open, and there was blood on her chin and down the front of her white shift, a great deal of it, and she was not making a sound. He was holding her and looking across the Altar at the Flambeaux coming, and his face was the face from the third platform.

Ignatius Crane was still on his knees before the cracked Eye with the dead censer in his hand.

And Solenne Aubade, kneeling at the edge of the drop with her broken wings dragging, looked past her sister at the kneeling Inquisitor, for a long time.

Then she got to her feet. Slowly. With Seraphina's help. And walked across the Altar of Lumina to Ignatius Crane, past the Blade on the stone and the weeping Patriarch, with the Flambeaux coming and the Choir crying under their canopies and the light lying back down round the Spire across the gulf; and stood over him, and looked down.

He looked up at her.

"*You,*" said Solenne.

"Yes," said Ignatius Crane.

She bent down and took him by the collar of his crimson robe — one long scarred hand, the talons at its fingertips dark — and lifted him to his feet as if he weighed nothing at all.

"*You're mine,*" she said. "*You're coming with me.*"

He did not resist. He let the dead censer drop from his hand onto the stone of the Altar, where it rang once, flat, like a cracked bell, and lay still.

---

Seraphina picked up the Blade.

She did not decide to. She was walking past it, behind her sister, toward the Vessel's stone and the Consecrated and the Cantor, with the Flambeaux closing from the rim and the Choir breaking and the Patriarch on his knees — and it was there on the white stone at her feet, shining, the colour of dawn, ringing very faintly, the bone of the god; and the old man's hands were held out over it, not quite touching, and his face turned up to her streaming with tears and utterly lost.

"*Why?*" he said. "*Why, child? It was answering. It was coming home. You let it go. You let it — go —*"

She bent and picked it up. It was warm. It rang in her hand, five hundred and twenty-eight, very low. The light round her — the ordinary light of an ordinary dawn — did not do anything at all.

"Because it was tired, Father," said Seraphina Aubade. "And so was she."

She put the Blade back in its scabbard, without the black cloth, bare. And turned toward the Pilgrim Chain, and the kilometre of black link running back across the gulf to the city through the white, and the Flambeaux between.

"Engineer," she said. "Can you carry her and run?"

The Consecrated looked at her over Lyra Ashgrove's head.

"Yes," he said.

"Then run," said Seraphina. "I'll walk behind you. The light still knows what I'm carrying, even if the Church doesn't." She looked at her sister — at her sister's broken wings, and her hand in Ignatius Crane's collar. "*Sol.* Can you fly?"

Solenne looked at her wings.

"*No,*" she said.

"Then we'll all go down the chain," said Seraphina, "the way the pilgrims used to. And we'll see who follows."

## CHAPTER FOURTEEN
### The Chain Run

> *Walk in the shade of the one ahead of you. If there is no one ahead of you, run.*
>
> — What the Umbral Tiers teach their children, as soon as they can walk

*The Pilgrim Chain, Sol-Luminary. Dawnday the thirteenth of Ashfall.*

---

The seventh anchor-house had a roof, and a winch, and two Flambeaux, and by the fifth hour of Dawnday it had Vane.

He had counted it all the afternoon before, from Brenna Corliss's chandlery and the Keel Docks and the top of the gutted house on the ninth tier where you could see the city's south-east rim if you put your head out of the black-painted window into the white for three seconds and pulled it back. He had put his head out eleven times. The left side of his face was the colour of a candle from the cheekbone to the ear, over the stitches, and Bettany had said nothing at all when she saw it, which was worse than anything she could have said.

The seventh anchor-house was the Pilgrim Chain's city end. A squat stone tower on the rim like the others, but whole, with a slate roof, because the Church had kept it. The chain came up into it out of the white across the gulf from Lumina, almost level, and went through the floor into the city's frame like the others; but on top of this one, bolted into every link, there was the walkway. Six feet of iron deck with a rail on each side, running out across the gulf into the glare for a kilometre to the shoulder of the mountain. And beside it, in the tower, the winch.

A great iron drum the height of a man, with a brake-wheel on its side like a ship's wheel and a lever as long as an oar, and round the drum the cable. The cable ran out across the gulf beside the walkway on iron rollers, a kilometre of it, to a second drum on Lumina, and the cage ran on it between them. Wind in here and the cage came to you; wind in there and it went. Let off both brakes and the cable ran free off both drums.

"So the cage is the shadow," Sable had said, at the binnacle, the night before, when he drew it.

"The cage is the shadow. It's the only roof on that whole chain. The Church walked their choir up under canopies on poles, but the poles are at Lumina now, at the Altar, holding up canopies over the choir." He tapped the chalk on the slate where the cage was. "Black silk on a black iron frame, eight feet by twenty. Anything under it, the light doesn't touch. And it's on a cable I can stop from here."

"Or start," said Sable.

"Or start," said Vane.

---

The two Flambeaux at the seventh anchor-house had been there all night, keeping the winch for the Church, in their crimson with their censers lit. Vesper took them at the fifth hour.

He did not see her do it. Nobody did. She went up the ramp to the anchor-house's door out of the white streets of the top tier — she had come down from the sixth anchor-house along the rim in the dark, alone, with one whole side of her gone white, and Mote in her coat — and went in, and a minute later she came to the door and stood there and nodded at him, and that was all.

They were sitting against the wall inside, side by side, with their censers still burning in their laps. They had not got up. There were two crossed cuts across each of their bellies and frost on the edges, and no blood on the floor at all, and the glass-fire in their censers went on burning white-gold in the cages, bright and high and untroubled, because their faith had not had time to go out.

Vane looked at them for a moment. Then he looked at Vesper.

"They were going to burn the ship," said Vesper. "If the Cantor didn't sing. I heard them talking about it on the ramp. Two hundred feet of black silk at the Keel Docks and a skyship in a cradle. They were looking forward to it."

He nodded. He did not say anything. He took the dead men's censers off their laps by the chains, carefully, and set them on the floor in the doorway where the light came in, and the glass-fire burned on in the white without a sound.

Then he went to the winch.

---

He had them where he wanted them by the sixth hour.

Sable at the winch-drum, with her hands on the brake-wheel, because Sable could hold a number in her head and act on it without flinching and he needed somebody who would put the brake on at the exact turn he called and not one turn sooner because she was frightened. Mags at the doorway, with her pin and one of the dead men's censers on its chain in her good hand, burning — because hounds would not cross glass-fire and something was going to come up out of the Tiers to the noise of what was about to happen; something always did. Vesper on the chain itself, thirty yards out along the walkway in the white, crouched at the rail like a cat on a wall, watching Lumina.

And the Aegis.

It stood in the mouth of the anchor-house where the walkway came in off the chain, nine feet of riveted iron, with Kaelen's black canvas sack still folded in its left fist the way he had given it to it at the bow the night before. Its helm was turned out along the chain toward the mountain. It had not moved since it came up the ramp. Inside its chest it was chiming the first line of Hymn 9, the soprano's line, over and over, very faint, the way it had been chiming since Kaelen talked to it at the bow.

Vane went and stood beside it in the doorway, in its shadow, which fell the right way, and looked out across the gulf.

A kilometre of black chain going out into the white. The walkway on its back like a thread of iron. The rollers and the cable beside it. And at the far end, small with distance, white on white, the shoulder of Mount Lumina and the round of the Altar cut into it, and the rings of black canopies round its rim like the dark centres of flowers, and at the back of it, on its frame, catching the very first grey of the dawn, the Eye.

He could hear the Pure Note from here. Two hundred throats. It came across the gulf like a wind.

"Right," said Vane, to nobody, to the Aegis, to the morning. "Now we wait."

---

He watched the sun come up behind Lumina.

He watched the Eye catch it and throw it, and the bar of gold go across the gulf straight as a ruled line past the anchor-house — fifty yards to his right, so close he felt the heat of it on the side of his face, the first heat he had felt in the Bleach — and strike the Spire in the crack. He watched the crack light.

He heard her start to sing.

He did not know how. He was a kilometre away, in a stone doorway on the rim of a city, with two hundred voices holding a note between him and her. He should not have heard anything. But he heard it come across the gulf under the Pure Note and through it, small and torn and clear, the way you hear one voice in a crowd that is saying your name: Lyra's voice, on the Root. Going up. The long reaching figure he had heard once before, at dawn, from four miles back, coming after the *Kestrel* over the green of Oakhaven in a voice that had been hers and was not.

Behind him, at the winch, he heard Sable draw in her breath.

And then he watched the light come off the Spire.

---

He would not tell it, afterward. Not to Mags, not to Vesper, not to the scribe for her Ledger, though she asked him once, very gently, a month later, and he looked at her until she put the pen down. He would only ever say one thing about the Restoration of the Light, and he said it once, on the *Kestrel*'s deck, at night, to Kaelen, and Kaelen did not answer, because there was no answer.

*I was on the rim. I could see the city.*

The anchor-house stood on the south-east rim at the top of Sol-Luminary, built out over the drop, and from its doorway, if you leaned out — and he leaned out, into the white, with the Aegis's shadow falling the other way and his face going pale and he did not care — you could see down the whole flank of the city. The white terraces of the Bleach going down tier by tier. And the line.

He saw the light gather round the Spire and begin to come across the gulf toward Lumina like a tide. He saw it from the side, the way you see a wave from the shore: a wall of white, slow and enormous, going out over the gulf away from him toward the mountain where she was singing.

And he saw the line on the city move.

It came down the flank of Sol-Luminary below him like a blind being lowered over a window. The ninth tier, where the gutted house was, where the Tiers left their offerings on the line — he watched it go from black to grey to white. The hanging houses under the walkways. The walkways themselves. The hammocks.

There were people in the hammocks. The Tiers slept in the morning; they lived at night. Rolled in their black blankets in rows like bats, under the walkways, over the gulf, in the deepest shade there was. And the shade went off them.

He saw them wake. He saw them start up in the hammocks and see the white coming down over the edge of the walkway above them — and run. Down the ladders. Along the walkways. Out of the hammocks onto the chains and down them hand over hand toward the dark. Hundreds of them. Fathers with children on their chests. He saw a woman throw a black blanket over a child and push it down a ladder ahead of her and come down after it with her back to the light. He saw a man on a walkway that the light had already reached stand still, and look at his hands, and sit down.

The eighth tier.

Behind it, the line had already gone down past the Basilica of the Pure Note, across its black west end, across the black tower where the Pure Note had come out through the stones all night, and he saw the black of it go grey and then go, and the black glass in the lancet windows go white, and he thought of the nine choristers in their shifts in the dark of the stalls, holding the note, and he did not think about them any further.

The seventh tier.

He did not count them. That was the thing he would remember. He stood in the doorway of the seventh anchor-house on the rim of the city with his face in the white and watched the light come down over the Umbral Tiers and the people of the Tiers running from it, and he did not count. For the first time since the trench off Cape Dissonance he did not count something that was happening in front of him. He could not make himself. The numbers would not come.

Then it stopped.

---

He felt it stop. He was leaning out of the doorway with one hand on the Aegis's iron arm for balance, and he felt the light *stop* — felt the white on his face, which had been pressing and taking and soft, go still on his skin — and across the gulf, at Lumina, the voice changed.

He heard it come down. A falling fifth.

He knew that one too.

And then something under the Pure Note that he had never heard in his life and never would again. Two notes. The Church's note, two hundred throats of it, and under it, close, a hair off, one torn voice singing something that did not match, that made the Pure Note into something else. Not a line. A chord. He felt it go out across the gulf and into the stone of the anchor-house under his boots, and into the chain, and into the whole hanging city on its nine links, the way the beat had gone into it the day before when the engineer laid his arm on the sixth chain.

*Go back to sleep.*

The tide stopped in the middle of the gulf. It hung there. And went back.

He watched the line on the city stop below the seventh tier, three tiers below where it had been at sunset, and hold.

And then the voice at Lumina stopped. Not at the end of a phrase. In the middle of the second note. As if somebody had closed a door on it.

He knew what that was too.

Behind him, at the winch, Sable made a sound he had never heard her make.

---

"*Captain.*"

Vesper, at the rail, thirty yards out on the walkway. Not loud — she was never loud — but the quiet round her carried her voice to him the way it carried everything, as if from a hand's breadth away.

"Captain. They're coming."

He brought the glass up.

Lumina. The Altar. The rings of black canopies, and under them the choir — broken, he could see that; the white robes scattered, people standing, people running, the Pure Note gone ragged and thin and stopping and starting. The Eye on its frame with the bar of gold gone out of it and a long black line across its face. The Vessel's stone at the centre.

And at the Altar's rim, where the Pilgrim Chain came in, people coming onto the walkway.

He counted them. He could count again; the numbers had come back as soon as the light stopped, all at once, like a tide coming in over a flat. *One*. A man, tall, carrying something across his chest, with his left arm bare and lit from inside so bright that it threw its own shadow on the walkway. *Two*. Something white in his arms. Limp. A woman, in a white shift. *Three*. Behind them, a tall figure with something black and huge dragging behind her on the deck like a pair of broken sails, and a man in crimson in her grip, by the collar, stumbling. *Four*. *Five*. And last, walking, not running, between them and the Altar, in white plate with the light *on* her: the knight.

They were on the walkway. In the open. In the full white light of the Bleach, with a kilometre of chain to come, and no roof.

And behind them, at the Altar's rim, crimson.

He counted that too. Six. Eight. Flambeaux, coming onto the walkway after them with their censers lit, white-gold, burning high, and the light parting round the glass-fire the way it parted round the knight, so that each man in crimson came along the chain in a little moving bubble of ordinary shade.

And between the Flambeaux and the knight, on the walkway, coming down the chain under its black silk canopy, with the Church's winch-men on Lumina letting off the brake behind it—

The cage.

---

"Sable," said Vane. "The brake. Now. Full."

The archivist put her whole weight on the wheel. The drum groaned. Out across the gulf, he watched the cable on its rollers go taut, and stop.

The cage did not stop.

He saw it through the glass. Half-way down the first hundred yards from the Altar, rolling on its iron wheels along the walkway between the rails, black silk canopy rippling on its frame — and no cable through its grip at all. They had uncoupled it. The winch-men on Lumina had knocked the cage's grip off the cable and let it go, and it was rolling free, on its own, down the long slow gentle slope of the Pilgrim Chain toward the city: half a ton of black iron on a six-foot walkway with a rail on either side, and nothing to stop it, and nothing ahead of it but five people running.

And it was full of crimson. He could see them under the canopy. Six, eight Flambeaux, crouched in the cage with their censers, riding it down.

It was gaining.

"How fast?" said Sable, from the winch, without turning round. Her hands were still on the brake.

He did the arithmetic. The slope. The weight. The wheels on the deck. The distance.

"Faster than they can run," he said. "It'll catch them a little over half-way."

He looked at the cable, taut on its rollers, under Sable's brake. At the drum. At the lever as long as an oar, that knocked the brake-band off.

"Sable," he said. "Change of plan. Let it off."

---

She did not ask why. That was the thing about the scribe. She looked at him over her shoulder for one beat, with her grey face and her ink-stained fingers on the brake-wheel, and he watched her see it — the cable, the loop, the cage uncoupled and riding free on the walkway with nothing between it and the runners except four hundred yards of downhill deck — and she put both hands on the lever and threw it, and the brake-band came off the drum with a bang.

The drum spun.

Out across the gulf the cable went *slack*. The whole kilometre of it, on its rollers, along the side of the walkway, going loose all at once as the drum at the city end let it run, and the drum at the Lumina end — with the Church's winch-men on it, who had just let their own brake off to send the cage — spinning too, with nothing holding either end.

And a kilometre of loose steel cable, under its own weight, began to sag.

It sagged off its rollers. It came down off the side of the walkway in long loops, swinging, the way a ship's cable comes off a capstan when the pawl slips — fast, everywhere at once, a long writhing line of steel falling across the walkway between the rails in coil after coil.

Across the walkway in front of the cage.

He watched it through the glass. The cage, rolling free down the chain, black silk rippling, half a ton of iron at the speed of a galloping horse, with eight men of the Inquisition crouched in it — hit the first coil of loose cable lying across the deck. And the second. And the cable went under its front wheels and *in*, wrapping the axle, the way a fouled line wraps a propeller, and the cage stopped.

Not slowly.

It stopped the way a horse stops at a wall. The front of it came up, the back of it came over, the black silk canopy tore off its frame and went up into the white like a flung cloak, and the cage went over sideways against the walkway rail and hung there, tilted, with its wheels in the air and the cable wound round its axle like a fist, and eight Flambeaux spilled out of it across the deck and against the rail and — two of them — over it.

He saw them go. Two men in crimson, falling into the gulf, with their censers still lit on their chains, two little white-gold sparks going down and down through the white toward the peaks two thousand feet below.

The others got up. Behind them, the ones on foot had pulled up short at the overturned cage lying across the walkway, and did not climb it.

---

"*Run,*" said Vane. Out across the chain. He did not shout. He did not need to. Vesper was on the walkway thirty yards out with her quiet round her, and she turned her head toward the runners, and he knew that what he said in the anchor-house doorway went out along the chain in the quiet like a voice in a speaking-tube. "*Run. Don't stop. The cage is down. Run.*"

They ran.

He watched them come. Four hundred yards. The engineer in front with the Cantor in his arms, running flat out, his bare glass arm round her and lit so bright he could barely look at it. The woman with the wings dragging, and the priest in her fist, stumbling, falling, hauled up again by the collar without breaking stride. The knight behind, walking backward now, facing the cage, with the light on her.

And the Flambeaux behind the knight. Six of them, out of the wreck of the cage, picking themselves up off the deck and coming on with their censers swinging.

And the light.

He saw it on them. It was on them the whole way. The Bleach lay on the Pilgrim Chain the way it lay on everything, sourceless, white, total, and they were running in it with no roof, and he could see it taking them. The engineer's right hand, flapping as he ran, white to the elbow. The Cantor's bare feet, hanging over his arm, going pale. The woman with the wings — the light did not touch her face or her wings, but it touched the priest in her grip, his crimson going pink, going white along the shoulder that faced the Spire.

Three hundred yards.

He could not do anything about it. There was no roof. The cage was the roof and the cage was lying on its side against the rail with its canopy gone.

Two hundred and fifty.

"*Captain,*" said Vesper.

The knight had stopped.

---

She had stopped walking backward. She had turned, on the walkway, to face the Flambeaux coming down the chain behind her — six men in crimson with their censers swinging, in their moving bubbles of shade, coming at a run — and she had drawn the Blade.

He saw it come out through the glass. It came out of its scabbard into the white the colour of a sunrise, and the white *went to it*.

All of it. Everything on the chain. The light that lay on the walkway and the rail and the cable and the runners — lifted off them, all at once, the way mist lifts off water, and streamed back up the chain toward the knight and the Blade in her hands like smoke going up a chimney. The engineer, running, went from blazing white to his own colour in a single stride. The Cantor in his arms. The priest. The walkway behind them went dark — not shade; *ordinary*, the ordinary grey of an ordinary dawn on a black iron deck — for two hundred yards, the whole stretch of chain between the knight and the anchor-house.

The Blade blazed in her hands like a struck sun.

And the Flambeaux stopped.

He saw them stop. Six men in crimson, twenty yards from her, with their censers swinging and their glass-fire burning white-gold — and the light coming up the chain past them toward the Blade, all the light of the Bleach, *pulled*, so bright now round her that he could not look at it through the glass and took the glass away from his eye. They could not go forward into that. Nobody could. They stood on the walkway with their arms across their faces, and the knight stood between them and the city with the bone of the god held up over her head in both hands, drawing every scrap of the light on the chain into herself, and the light went *round* her — the light parted round her, the way it had at the Foundry, the way it had for seven years — and did not take her.

It did not take her. But it went past her. On both sides. Back up the chain.

Onto the Flambeaux.

---

He did not look at that through the glass. He had seen a gull. He did not need to see six men.

He looked instead at the runners, coming up the last two hundred yards of chain in the ordinary grey shade that the knight had made for them, and he went out to meet them.

He went out of the anchor-house onto the walkway, past the Aegis, past Vesper at the rail — and Vesper came with him, without a word, at his shoulder, on his bad side — and they ran down the chain toward the engineer coming up it.

He met them at a hundred yards.

Kaelen did not stop. He did not slow. He came up the walkway with the Cantor across his chest and his face the colour of paper and his eyes on the anchor-house, and as Vane came alongside him he said, not breathing, "*She's not — she's breathing. She's not making a sound. There's blood.*" And kept running.

Vane looked at her as he turned and ran beside them.

She was white to the lips. Her head was back over the engineer's glass arm and her mouth was open, and her chin and throat and the front of her shift were red, a great deal of it, and she was not making a sound. Her eyes were open. They found him. He saw them know him.

Her hand moved. On the engineer's chest. Two fingers, very slightly. A sign. The *Kestrel*'s hand-speech, the one Mags had taught the whole ship.

*Here.*

He ran.

---

The woman with the wings was behind them. He had not seen her up close. He did now, as she came up the walkway with the priest in her fist, and Vesper went past him back down the chain to her — Vesper, who did not like her, who had held her quiet over seven of the Unison the day before while this woman sat in her nest with her face smooth and empty — and got her shoulder under one of the dragging wings and *lifted*, and the woman with the wings looked at her once, sharp, and let her.

She was taller than Vane. Her wings were broken. He could see it: one of them bent wrong at a joint near her shoulder, folded the way a bird's wing folds when a dog has had it, the long black plated blades of it dragging and scraping along the iron deck behind her, the gold along their edges flickering and going dim. The other was whole but she was not using it. She had the priest by the collar in one hand and her face was set like a woman carrying water up a hill.

The priest was walking. Just. He had a white hand and a white streak up the side of his face where the light had found him, and his crimson had gone pink along one shoulder, and he did not seem to know where he was. When he stumbled, she hauled him up. When he slowed, she dragged him.

"*Don't,*" she said, when Vane put out a hand toward him. Two voices, at once, copper and dark. "*He's mine.*"

Vane did not argue.

---

Fifty yards. The anchor-house doorway. The Aegis in it, nine feet of iron, with its helm turned down the chain toward the Cantor in the engineer's arms, and its great hands lifting.

Kaelen ran up the last yards of the walkway into the shadow of the doorway and stopped in front of it.

He did not say anything. He held her out. And the Aegis Sentinel took Lyra Ashgrove out of his arms the way you take a child out of a cart — both hands, under her back and her knees, slowly, so slowly — and brought her in against its iron chest, into the curve of its arm, and folded the black canvas sack it had been holding all night over her like a blanket.

The chiming in its chest stopped.

Then it started again. Not Hymn 9. Something Vane had heard every night on the *Kestrel* for three weeks, through the deck, from the hold, from the cot where the Cantor slept when she slept at all: four notes and a falling fifth. The tending note of the Reach. The one you hum into a child's back.

Kaelen stood in the doorway with his arms empty and his glass arm hanging at his side, burning, and looked at it.

Then he turned round and looked back down the chain.

---

The knight was still out there.

She was coming. Walking. Backward, up the walkway toward the city, with the Blade raised over her head in both hands, slowly, step by step, and the light streaming to the Blade from everywhere and parting round her and going on past her down the chain onto whatever was behind her. There was nothing behind her now. Vane did not let himself think about that. There was a long stretch of iron walkway and the overturned cage against the rail and the cable lying in coils, and nothing else on the Pilgrim Chain between the knight and Mount Lumina except the light, and she was holding it.

He saw her arms.

They were shaking. They had been raised over her head for five minutes, in plate, with a sword in them, and they were shaking, and the Blade in her hands was shaking, and every time it shook the light round her shook with it — the parting of it round her body flickered, the way a candle flickers in a draught — and he saw the white come onto the edge of her armour and go off again.

She could not keep it up. Not all the way. A hundred and fifty yards, walking backward, with her arms over her head.

"She's going to drop it," said Kaelen.

"Yes."

"If she drops it before she's in the shade, the light comes back onto her. All of it. Everything she's been holding off." He did not look away from her. "It'll take her like the gull."

"Yes," said Vane.

He looked at the cable.

It lay on the walkway in its long slack loops, from the anchor-house all the way out to the overturned cage, where it was wound round the cage's axle like a fist. A kilometre of loose steel cable, run off the city drum, lying along the chain.

He looked at the winch. At the drum, with its brake-band off, and its lever, and the big iron crank on its other side that you turned to wind the cable in, which took four men.

He looked at the Aegis, with the Cantor in its arms.

"Kaelen," he said. "The winch. Wind it in."

The engineer looked at him. Then at the cable. Then at the cage, half a kilometre down the chain with its axle wrapped in steel, lying across the walkway behind the knight like a—

"*Like a roof,*" said Kaelen.

He was already moving.

---

It took two of them. The engineer and Vane. They could not have done it with four ordinary men in the time they had. But the engineer had a hundredweight of glass on his left arm that had drunk the light of the Bleach all the way up and down the Pilgrim Chain and was burning so bright it threw its own shadow, and when he put it on the winch-crank and turned, the crank *went*.

The drum wound. The cable came in off the walkway, coil by coil, loop by loop, singing as it tightened, the whole slack kilometre of it drawing up straight along the chain toward the anchor-house — and at the end of it, half a kilometre out, wound round its axle like a fist, the cage.

It came off the rail.

He saw it, between turns, through the doorway. The overturned cage, dragged by its fouled axle, scraping, groaning, came off the rail where it had been leaning and rolled back up onto its wheels — crooked, battered, one side of its frame bent in — and started to come along the walkway toward the city on the end of the cable. Slowly. Then faster. Its canopy was gone. But the frame was still there, the black iron frame on its poles, and on the frame, tangled, shredded, the rags of the black silk.

Behind the knight. Coming up the chain behind her as she walked backward toward the city. Faster than she was walking.

"*Seraphina!*" Kaelen shouted. Down the chain. Not in the quiet; he did not have the quiet. He shouted it with his whole voice, and his voice came out of him ringing — Vane heard it, they all heard it, the glass at his throat taking the shout and giving it back with the note under it, five hundred and twenty-eight — and it went down the chain like a bell. "*Seraphina! Behind you! Get under it!*"

The knight turned her head.

She saw the cage coming up the walkway behind her on its cable, with the rags of its black silk streaming. She did not lower the Blade. She stepped to the side of the walkway, against the rail, with her arms still over her head, shaking, and let it come up to her—

And as the black iron frame rolled past her, she stepped *in*. Under it. Into what was left of its shade.

And lowered the Blade.

---

The light came back onto the Pilgrim Chain all at once.

Everything she had been holding off. It came down off the Blade and out from round her and back onto the walkway and the rail and the cable and the gulf, sourceless and total and taking, white, and Vane watched it come along the chain toward the anchor-house like a wave along a beach and threw himself flat behind the Aegis in the doorway with his arms over his head.

It came over the doorway and stopped at the Aegis's shadow.

He lay on the stone and breathed.

When he looked up, the cage was coming up the last of the chain on its cable into the anchor-house, with the engineer winding, and the rags of black silk on its frame were thin as tissue, gone white, coming apart in the light like ash off a fire, strand by strand.

And under them, crouched in the cage's battered iron frame on its floor with the bare Blade across her knees and her arms hanging, alive, the knight.

---

They were all inside the anchor-house when the Aegis fell.

He did not see why at first. It had been standing in the doorway with Lyra in its arms the whole time — through the cable and the cage and the light coming back — not moving, its great iron body between the Cantor and the white outside, chiming the tending note. When the cage came in off the chain and the knight climbed out of it and Kaelen let go of the crank, it turned. Slowly. With a long hiss of steam somewhere in its neck. Toward the inside of the anchor-house, and the stair down to the city, and the way home.

And went down on one knee.

He heard it go. A long grinding iron sound, like a ship going aground. Its left knee hit the stone and stayed there, and its right leg locked straight, and it stayed kneeling with the Cantor still in its arms, held against its chest, wrapped in black canvas, and did not let her go.

Then he saw its back.

It had been standing in the doorway with its back to the chain. To the light. For the whole of the run. Twenty minutes in the doorway of the seventh anchor-house with its face toward the inside and its back toward the Bleach, with the Cantor in its arms, between her and the white. And the light had been on its back the whole time.

The iron was white.

Not painted. Taken. The riveted plates of its back, its shoulders, the backs of its great arms and legs, had gone the white of the Glassmarket and the gull, and they were thin. He could see it. The edges of the plates had gone soft, translucent, like paper held up to a lamp, and where they met there were gaps, and through the gaps, inside the iron, very faintly, there was a light. Not cyan. Not white. A warm, dim, rosy light, like a lamp behind a curtain. Like the light a hand makes when you hold it over a candle.

The core.

He stood there and understood what he was looking at. Sable had told him. Everyone on the *Kestrel* knew it, the way you know a thing nobody says. Inside the Aegis Sentinel, in a glass core behind its iron chest, among the logic-gates that said *DEFEND THE SINGERS*, there was a child. A boy of ten, an alto, WG-41,005 on the bronze beside his core, Unnamed in the harvest of 416, with a surname nobody could read because the scribe's hand had shaken when she wrote it. Waking up, slowly, inside iron.

And the iron round her had gone thin as paper, holding the light off a Cantor for twenty minutes, and the light was coming in.

---

Sable was on her knees beside it before anyone else moved.

He did not see her go. One moment she was at the winch, with her hands still on the brake-wheel from the cable, and the next she was kneeling on the stone floor of the anchor-house in front of the Aegis with her grey skirts spread round her and both hands flat on its chest, on the stencil, *DEFEND THE SINGERS*, and her face very close to the iron.

It was still holding Lyra. It would not let her go. Its helm had come down, slowly, until it was bowed over the Cantor in its arms; and the faint light in its slot was going faint. Fainter.

Its chest was chiming. Very slowly now. Further apart. The tending note of the Reach, and then a long silence, and then the next note, as if it had to remember each one.

"I know your name," said Sable.

She said it very quietly, with her face against the iron. Vane could only just hear it.

"I have always known your name. I wrote it down. I was in the doorway when they pressed you, and I was counting, and I wrote it in the book, and my hand shook, and I made your surname so that nobody could read it." Her voice did not change at all. It was the voice she used for entries. "I have spent three weeks trying to read it again. I have looked at that line every night. I couldn't. I had made it so that I couldn't." She was quiet for a moment. "I can now."

She did not open the Ledger. It was under her arm, in its oilcloth, where it always was. She did not take it out.

"I know it because I remember the doorway," said Sable. "Not the book. I remember you. You were the last one in the line. You had a red cloak on, and it was too big for you, and you'd turned the cuffs back twice. And when they asked you your name, so that it could be entered, you said it as if you were proud of it. Nobody else in that line said their name as if they were proud of it. And the little one behind you, Annet, had hold of your hand, and would not let go of it, not even at the door."

The chiming stopped.

---

He did not know what it cost her. He would learn, later, from the scribe herself, one night on the *Kestrel* at the binnacle, in a voice that did not shake: *my mother's hands. Braiding my hair. I can't remember what they looked like any more. I can remember that I had them.*

What he saw was Sable, on her knees on the floor of the seventh anchor-house of Sol-Luminary, with both hands flat on the iron and her face against it, saying:

"Whisper-Grove, forty-one thousand and five. Wren Halloway. Ten years old. Alto. Of the Cloister of the Lost Choir. Son of Jem Halloway, carter, and Isobel Halloway, who sang in the parish choir and taught him the alto line of Hymn 9." A breath. "Taken for parts. Wrongly. Returned."

And then, not in the voice for entries — in another voice, very low, that Vane had never heard come out of the archivist and never heard again:

"*Wren. That's you. You're Wren.*"

---

For a long moment, nothing happened at all.

Then the light in the helm-slot came back.

Not the faint steady glow it had always had. Brighter. Warmer. The colour of the light behind the thinned plates of its back: rosy, dim, like a hand over a candle. It came up in the slot until the whole of the curved riveted helm was lit from inside like a lantern, and the helm lifted. Slowly. Up from where it had been bowed over the Cantor. Toward Sable's face.

And the Aegis Sentinel spoke.

Not a chime. Not a hymn. Not words, either, not quite. It came out of the vents in its neck where the steam hissed, and out of the grille across its chest where the chiming came from, and out of the iron itself, all at once, the way a church organ speaks out of the whole church. A sound. A long, low, uncertain sound, like somebody who has not used their voice in three years trying to remember where it is. A child's voice, through iron.

An alto.

It did not say anything that anybody in the anchor-house could understand. It did not need to. It held a single note, low and wavering and too loud, the way children hold a note when they are not sure of it yet. And then it found the next one. And the next.

It was the alto line of Hymn 9.

Sable put her face against its chest and did not move for a long time.

---

They went down through the Tiers in the morning with the Aegis walking.

It could walk. Slowly, with a long grinding in its right knee and steam hissing out of the thinned plates of its back at every step. It would not give the Cantor to anyone else. It carried her down every ladder of the Umbral Tiers one-handed, holding the rungs with the other, with her wrapped in black canvas against its chest and its helm bowed over her, humming. Sable walked beside it with one hand on its arm the whole way. Nobody suggested she let go.

The Tiers were awake.

He had expected them to hide. He had expected shutters closed and ladders pulled up and the Narrow, the way the Tiers had been for three days: a cave hung upside down, silent, watching. They were not hiding. They were on the walkways. Hundreds of them, in their black, with their children, standing on the walkways and the ladder-heads and the edges of the hanging houses as the company came down, and they were not silent.

They were humming.

He did not know what. He did not know if they knew. It was not the Pure Note. It was not anything he had ever heard. It was low and wavering and every one of them was humming it a little differently, a little off from the person beside them, so that it was not a note at all but a kind of murmur, a rough unsteady chord, coming up out of the whole underside of the city as the Aegis carried the Cantor down through it.

It took him three tiers to understand that they were humming the second note.

The one she had sung on Lumina. The one that did not match. They had heard it come across the gulf at dawn with the light coming down over their hammocks, and they had heard the light stop. And they had worked out — the way people do, the way the Tiers had worked out to walk in each other's shadows — which note had stopped it.

At the third tier, a woman stepped out onto the walkway in front of the Aegis with a black blanket in her arms, and held it out. Not to the Aegis. To Sable. Sable looked at it, and at her, and took it, and put it over the Cantor on top of the black canvas, and the woman stepped back, and the humming went on.

At the second tier, Brenna Corliss stood in the door of her chandlery under the sign that said ROPE in black on black, with the white half of her face turned away from the lamp, and watched them go past, and did not say anything, and lifted one hand.

---

At the Keel Docks the harbourmaster had pulled up the ladders.

All of them. Every ladder from the second tier to the cradles. The Church packet with the white sun-disc on its envelope swung in its cradle with nobody aboard it and no way to get aboard it, and the harbourmaster in her black veil stood at the head of the one ladder still down — the one to the fourth cradle, where the *Kestrel* hung — with her two men with their long poles, and as the Aegis came down the last walkway with the Cantor in its arms, she stepped aside.

She did not kneel this time. She looked at Kaelen, at the glass arm burning at his side, and then at the knight behind him with the bare Blade on her back and her hands pale to the wrist, and then at the woman with the broken wings and the priest in her fist.

"Forty-one," she said, under the veil, to nobody. "On the seventh and eighth and ninth. Forty-one we know of, so far."

Kaelen stopped.

"The line came down," said the harbourmaster. "Three tiers. Forty-one of ours were above it when it stopped." She did not say anything else. She did not need to. She stood aside from the ladder and looked at the deck of the cradle at her feet.

Then she said, very quietly: "It would have been all of us. We heard it coming. We heard it stop." She lifted her veiled face to the knight. "We heard what stopped it. Go. Before the Basilica rings."

---

They lifted out of the fourth cradle of the Keel Docks of Sol-Luminary at the eighth hour of Dawnday, and Vane flew her.

One-handed. With his face stitched in eleven places and the left side of it white from the cheekbone to the ear, and his left hand splinted against his chest, and the wheel under his right. Pennick at the tank-valves, with his face set. Mags at his left shoulder, reading his lips. The city's shadow lay over the gulf to the north-west at that hour, long and black, swinging slowly round as the sun climbed, and he took the *Kestrel* out of the cradle and down into it and along it, the inside of the wedge, counting — the swing of the edge across the peaks below, the narrowing, the wind — and did not let her drift once.

Behind them, as they cleared the city's shadow at its far end and came out into ordinary daylight over ordinary mountains with colour in them, the Basilica's bell began to ring.

He did not look back. He had stopped looking back at things a long time ago. But Vesper was at his left elbow, on his bad side, in her quiet — he had not heard her come; he never would — and she looked back for both of them.

"The line's still down," she said. "Three tiers. It isn't coming back up."

"No."

"It's not going any further down either."

"No," said Vane. "Not today."

---

He counted them, that night, at the wheel. It was what he did.

The engineer, asleep for the first time in three days, on the foredeck against the press, with his glass arm across his chest burning a soft steady cyan and the edge of the glass at his throat a finger's width from the hollow of it. Bettany had measured it at sunset with Lyra's knotted string and taken the number down to the hold, and Lyra had written it on her slate and sent the slate up, and underneath it, *NOT YET*, and he had looked at it for a long time.

The Cantor, in the hold, on her cot, with Bettany and Hetty Bram sitting either side of her. She had no voice. Not torn. *None*. Bettany had looked down her throat with a candle and a spoon and come up the ladder afterward and sat on the hatch-coaming with her hands in her lap and said nothing to anybody for an hour. Lyra had written on the slate, when she woke: *DID IT STOP?* And when Bettany told her yes, she had written: *HOW MANY?* And when Bettany told her that too, she had turned her face to the hull and not written anything else.

The knight, at the stern rail, alone, with the Blade bare in her lap and her hands, which had gone a shade lighter, holding it.

The woman with the broken wings, in the lee of the tank-cradle, with her wings folded wrong round her and the priest sitting against the bulkhead beside her with his white hand in his lap and his eyes open. She had not let go of his collar since Lumina. She had not said anything to him since *you're mine*. He had not asked her anything.

The archivist, in the hold, sitting on a crate in the dark beside the Aegis, which had folded itself down against the bulkhead like a great iron dog and would not move; with her hand on its arm. It was humming. The alto line. Sable had one thing left of herself now. The smell of the Veil's scriptorium on a winter morning. He had heard her say it to herself, at sunset, in the voice she used for entries, the way you count your coins.

Pennick at the tanks. Tobiah in his hammock with his side bound, awake, watching the knight. Mags on the windlass with a line across her knees.

And at his left elbow, in her quiet, Vesper, with one whole side of her gone white, and something under her coat that leaned toward the woman with the broken wings, and purred.

Thirteen, counting the one in iron. And a thing in a coat. And a priest who had been the brightest flame in the Church, in a winged woman's fist. And four hundred and thirty-two people in the hold, saying their names in the dark.

He held the *Kestrel* on her heading — north-east, away from the Heights, toward the Tramway and the Reach and the war that was coming, nine weeks away now, eight, seven — and did the arithmetic again, and did not like it, and did it again.

"You're counting," said Vesper.

"I'm always counting."

"I know." She was quiet for a moment. "What's the number?"

He thought about it.

Forty-one, on the seventh and eighth and ninth. Two Flambeaux off the chain. Six more in the light. Four of the Unison on the floor of the sixth anchor-house. A woman's mother's hands. A Cantor's voice. A knight's oath and a priest's fire. Three tiers of a city, gone white, not coming back.

One god, ringing down. Still. Not today.

"Not yet," said Vane.
