## CHAPTER FOUR
### The Drowned Chime

> *Article the Sixteenth. What lies below the tide-mark belongs to whoever brings it up. The Harbour takes a fifth. The sea takes what it likes, and no court on this coast will hear a complaint against it.*
>
> — Articles of the Free Captains of Port Cantabile

*Port Cantabile and the Sunken Carillon. Stasisday the fourth of Ashfall, night, to Memoryday the seventh.*

---

The bottles came down the Salt Stair at ten o'clock that night in a net-bag carried by one of Oona Salt's people, who set them on the chandler's counter one at a time, very gently, as if they were eggs, and would not stay to be thanked.

"Harbour-Queen's compliments," he said from the door. "Found on the sixth landing. Harbour property under the Twelfth. She says they're for the Captain, and she says he'll know why."

Then he went back up into the dark as fast as a man can go on wet steps.

Lyra was in the back room with Tobiah when they arrived. She had been there since four o'clock, on a stool by the trestle with a lamp, because Bettany had to sleep sometime and because the boy's breathing had a catch at the top of every breath that she wanted to keep listening to, the way you keep a hand on a pot you think might boil. A catch at the top was a rib. A catch at the bottom would have been a lung. She had been counting them. Two hundred and eleven, so far, all at the top.

She heard the bottles go down on the counter through the curtain. Four. Glass on oak, each a little higher than the one before, as if the hand setting them down got more careful as it went.

When she came through, Sable was standing in front of them with a lamp, reading.

The archivist did not touch them. She bent from the waist with her hands clasped behind her back, like a woman in a museum, and read each label in turn by holding the lamp close, and then she straightened up and stood quite still, and Lyra saw her lips move once without sound, which was something Sable did when she was entering a thing into the part of her head where the Ledger lived.

Lyra came round the counter and looked.

Four blue vox-bottles, palm-high, waxed at the neck. Four paper labels, in four different hands: one a clerk's copperplate, one a big careful print that had been done slowly by somebody who did not write often, one in brown ink that had run in damp, one in pencil.

Every one of them said the same name.

**VESPER. FIRST MATE, *WAVE-CUTTER*. LOST OFF CAPE DISSONANCE, 414.**

Somebody had chalked a price on the wax of each. Four. Four. Six. Nine.

*Nine,* Lyra thought, and did not know why that was the one that turned her stomach, and then did: somebody had paid nine sovereigns for this, or been meant to. Somebody had been charged more because they had wanted it more.

The street door opened and Vane came in out of the Lantern Yard with rain on his shoulders, and stopped.

He stood there in the doorway for a while. Lyra could hear the Yard behind him: a woman calling a child in, a cart, the harbour note flat under everything, and far out past the Cay the Chime ringing in its threes. Then he shut the door, and the Yard went away, and he walked over to the counter and looked down at the bottles without picking any of them up.

"Well," he said. "That's tidy."

Nobody answered him.

He put his hand inside his coat — not the outside pocket, where he kept his flask and his matches and the eleven useless things a captain carries, but the inside one, over the heart, which Lyra had never once seen him use — and took out a fifth bottle, and set it at the end of the row.

Same blue glass. Same wax. The label was done in the slow big print.

"Second landing," said Vane. "Spring of '17. Four sovereigns." He touched the chalk on the wax of the second bottle on the counter, the one done in the same hand, and his finger left a smear. "Same fellow. He must have liked the work."

"Have you opened it?" said Sable.

"No."

"In two years."

"I'm not a fool, scribe. You open one of those and you don't know what comes up the neck." He was looking at the bottles and not at either of them. "And I'm not a brave man either, which is the other reason, if you're entering this somewhere."

Lyra took the slate off her belt and wrote on it, and held it up.

*IS SHE IN THEM?*

Vane read it. He did that thing he did, the half-laugh, the one that came out of the side of his mouth when something hurt.

"Cantor," he said, "she's on the *stair*."

---

Here is what the Rust Coast knows about vox-bottles, which Lyra learned that night from Hesper, who had come down to the chandler's to see the Oakhaven women and stayed for the rum.

They started in the Screaming Year, the same as the quiets. Somebody — the stories disagree who — took a bottle out to the lip of the trench off Cape Dissonance on a calm day and held it upside-down over the water with the stopper out and counted a hundred, and stoppered it, and brought it back, and when it was opened in a kitchen in the Lantern Yard a drowned man's widow heard her husband say her name.

"Once," said Hesper. "Just the once, and then it was empty. And she paid the man who brought it everything she had, and then she went and *told* people."

So there was a trade. There had been a trade for six years. Boats went out to the trench lip and came back with cases of bottles, and the bottles were labelled from the harbour's lists of the lost, and sold up the Salt Stair to anybody who could pay.

"And are they real?" Nell Carder asked.

Hesper considered her rum.

"Some," she said. "That's the trouble with it, love. If none of them was real it'd be a cheat and we'd have hanged the lot of them years back. If all of them was real it'd be a mercy. It's *some*." She drank. "And some of them, you take the stopper out and it's not your husband says your name. It's something that learned your husband's name off the water. And then there's the other sort, the sort the *Nancy* had, where you take the stopper out and nothing says anything at all, and then you can't hear your own kitchen."

Lyra wrote on the slate and turned it to her.

*WHERE DO THE VOICES COME FROM?*

Hesper read it twice. She was a big woman with a soft face, who ran four houses on the Lantern Yard and took in wreck-orphans on Driftwood Cay and had buried, she said, more children than most women on the Coast had borne, and she had the kindness of a person who had stopped expecting anything back for it.

"From down there," she said. "Where else? It's the trench. Things go down, and something comes back up." She looked at Lyra's throat, and at the blood-dark crust at the corner of her mouth from the morning. "You'd want to keep that quiet, near the water. You know that. The Chime's been listening to this harbour for six years."

---

There was a rule aboard the *Kestrel* now, which nobody had ever said aloud, because saying it aloud was the problem.

It had started at the Mausoleum, after Kaelen told them what his arm could hear and who might be listening at the other end of it. Glass cut from one Spire is one thing in two places. Anything said within reach of that arm, a hundred and forty-year-old man in a white room five hundred miles east could hear as plainly as if he stood in the doorway. Kaelen had told them so himself, in a flat voice, on the deck of the *Kestrel* at night, and then he had gone forward to the bow and sat with his back to them so that he could not see their faces while they decided what to do about it.

What they did about it was write.

They wrote plans on Lyra's slate and on the backs of Pennick's tank-charts and on the wall of the hold in grease-pencil, and when they needed to talk, somebody would say, *Engineer, would you go and see about the—*, and Kaelen would go and see about it. He did not argue. He went, every time, with his collar up. He did not need to be told what they were doing. He knew the exact sound of a room that stopped talking when he walked into it, and he had heard it more in eleven days than in the seven years he had lived alone outside the Hedge.

That night, when the chandler's back room had the dive to plan, Vane said, "Engineer, would you go and see if the bellows are any good," and Kaelen got up from the floor by Tobiah's trestle and went out into the rain, and Lyra watched the curtain fall closed behind him and felt it like a hand pressed flat on her chest.

They planned it on the counter, beside the bottles, in grease-pencil on a sail-bag.

*TWO DIVERS. K. — ARM IS BALLAST, ARM HEARS THE BELLS. 2ND: ?*

"Teague," said Vane. "He's asked. He's dived the edge of the Chime twice for the Harbour, and he knows the *Gale*, and he's got nothing to do this week but sit on a bollard."

*HE BURIED COLM TODAY.*

"Yes," said Vane. "That's why he's asked."

---

Coll Madder was not buried that day, because the Rust Coast has no ground to spare.

Lyra had not known that. She learned it at the top of the Long Mole at sunset with Ruth Madder standing beside her holding the baby, while a Harbour man explained in a careful, embarrassed voice that on this coast the dead go to the water — sewn in sailcloth with a stone at the feet, off the end of the Mole at the turn of the tide, with a bell rung once — because the town is cut into rock and every foot of flat ground is a house or a yard or a cistern, and the old burial caves above the Salt Stair had been full since before anybody's grandfather.

"No," said Ruth Madder.

"Missus, it's—"

"He's never seen it. He'd never seen it till today. You are not putting him in it."

The man looked at Lyra, as if she might be in charge of this, and she shook her head.

In the end it was Hesper who settled it. There was a burying-ground on Driftwood Cay, she said, on the high rock above the orphan-house, because the Cay was older than the Coast's customs and the Cay's people had been farmers once. Coll could go across with the Oakhaven folk when they went, and lie in earth, among the children. Ruth held the baby and nodded and did not say anything else, and the Harbour men took Coll Madder back down off the Mole on a hand-cart, under a sail, and laid him in the cool of the Harbour office to wait four days for a boat.

Lyra sat with Ruth afterward on the steps of the chandler's in the dark. Neither of them said anything. Lyra did not trust her voice and Ruth would not, and they sat in the Narrow of it, a murmur of rain and a baby's breathing, until the baby woke and needed feeding, and Ruth went in.

Coll's daughter did not have a name yet. They had been waiting to name her until they could do it in the Hollow, properly, under the apple trees, with Lyra to sing it.

---

The dive-lighter was called the *Patience*, and she had been built for exactly this: a broad-beamed open boat, thirty feet, with a short stubby mast and a derrick on it, two double-action bellows-pumps amidships, coils of rubbered air-pipe in tubs, and a speaking-tube lashed along each pipe so that a man forty feet down could put his mouth to a brass cup inside his helmet and be heard at a brass trumpet in the boat.

"That's new," said Teague, of the speaking-tubes. "Since the Hollis brothers. Dunnet had them fitted. Thought it'd help." He coiled a pipe. "It didn't."

They went out from the Mole on Dawnday at the slack of the morning tide, with the sky low and grey and the sea flat as a plate under it, and the Aegis Sentinel sitting on the bottom-boards in the bow like a piece of cargo nobody had been able to fit anywhere else.

It had come aboard last. Nobody had asked it to. Lyra had seen it at the head of the Mole steps in the half-dark, standing very straight with its great iron hands at its sides, and when she came down the steps it had followed her down, one tread at a time, and the *Patience* had gone down eight inches by the stern when it stepped in, and the four Harbour men at the pumps had moved, very quietly, to the far end of the boat.

It was why Oona had agreed to the job at all. A thing nine feet tall that did not tire and did not breathe could stand at the derrick and haul a man up from forty feet hand over hand, as fast as a winch, for as long as it took.

Vane had the tiller. He sat with it under his arm and his eyes on the Chime-buoy ahead, and Lyra, watching him across the boat, saw that he never once looked over the side. He looked at the buoy, and at the sky, and at the pipes, and at the back of the Aegis's head. He did not look at the sea. When spray came over the bow he wiped it off his face with the back of his wrist like a man wiping off something worse than water.

The Chime-buoy was a barrel painted red on a chain, a mile and a half south-west of the Cay. Under it, Teague said, the reef fell away off a shelf, and on the slope of the shelf, in the Screaming Year, the old fishing town of Wendmere had slid into the sea in a single afternoon with its church and its bell-tower and four hundred people, and the tower had come to rest standing up.

"Belfry's at forty feet," said Teague. "Base is at seventy-five, in the dark. Town's below that, down the slope. Nobody goes into the town." He nodded at the water. "And the *Gale* came down on top of it all in the storm of '16 and broke her back across the parapet, so her hull's lying in the belfry with her tanks in her belly, and her mainmast's sticking up out of it like a pin out of a cushion. Top of the mast's at eight feet on a low tide. You'll see it. You go down the mast."

"Like a ladder," said Kaelen.

"Like a ladder that's got a current on it you won't believe," said Teague, "so you go down it with both hands and you don't let go of it for anything. That's how the Hollis brothers went. They let go."

Lyra was looking at the water.

It was green, the green of glass bottles held up to the light, and so clear in the flat morning that she could see down into it a long way, and she could see — she was almost sure — something standing up out of the deeper green beneath them: a dark line, a single straight dark line going down and down until the green swallowed it. The *Gale*'s mast.

And she could hear the bells.

Not with her ears. Or not only. She felt them in the bottom-boards under her knees, three notes, slow, a long gap, three notes. The gap had a shape to it. She knew that shape the way she knew the shape of a held breath in a birthing room, the space before the next push. Something was ringing the Chime, and between the threes it was listening.

---

They dressed Kaelen in the Harbour's biggest suit and it did not fit his left side at all.

Teague and two of the Harbour men cut the sleeve off the canvas suit, and laced a leather gauntlet-collar round his arm at the shoulder with tallow and twine, and stood back and looked at it, and everyone in the boat understood at the same moment that the glass was going to be in the sea bare.

Then they cinched the weight-belt round his middle, and Lyra saw him go white and say nothing, because it sat directly across the ribs that had gone from mended to not on the Salt Stair three days before. Bettany had strapped them. Bettany had also said, flatly, that a man with cracked ribs had no business at forty feet, and Kaelen had agreed with her, and dressed.

"Will it—" Teague started.

"It's been in worse," said Kaelen.

He did not say anything else. He had said very little since the Salt Stair and none of it was about Coll. Lyra had watched him for two days. He ate when Bettany put food in his hand and slept when Pennick took the lamp away and the rest of the time he stood somewhere with his back to a wall and his collar up and looked at nothing. He had looked at her once, on the Mole, across the width of Coll Madder's sailcloth, and she had looked back at him, and she had not known what her face was doing, and neither of them had moved.

They put the helmet on him: copper, twelve-bolt, with three round windows like the eyes of a startled thing. They screwed the faceplate home. Mags Tennant took the handle of his pump with her good hand and looked at Lyra and nodded once, and Lyra took the brass trumpet of his speaking-tube in her two hands and put it to her ear.

The bellows began, *hush-ah, hush-ah*, the oldest sound in the world, a slow bellows breath, and inside it, through the tube, she could hear Kaelen Vance breathing in a copper box.

He went over the side backward on the ladder, the way Teague showed him. The water closed over the top of the helmet without any fuss at all.

Teague went after him. Pell and Mabb took Teague's pump.

---

*Mast,* Kaelen said, through the tube. His voice came up the pipe small and hollow, as if from the bottom of a well. *Got it.*

Lyra wrote it on the slate as he said it so that the boat could see. *MAST. GOT IT.*

*Current's — strong. Teague's right.* A pause; breathing; the bellows. *Going down it.*

She knew where he was by his breathing. That was her craft, the only one she had: she had spent her life in birthing rooms and fever-rooms reading the distance and the pitch of other people's breath, and she could hear the pressure of the water coming onto him in the way the breath shortened, three feet, six, ten. At twenty feet he said something she did not catch, and then, more clearly:

*Lyra. My breath. It doesn't go up.*

She wrote: *BREATH DOESN'T RISE?* and Teague's trumpet, at Pell's end of the boat, gave out a short hard laugh in Teague's voice that was not a laugh.

"Told you," said Pell. She had the other trumpet. "Told him. It's the Drift. Down there the sea's forgotten which way's up for bubbles. You breathe out and it just — stays. Hangs about you like a cloud of frogspawn. By the time you've been down an hour you can't see your hand for your own old breaths."

Lyra pressed the trumpet to her ear.

*Everything I breathe out,* said Kaelen, very quietly, forty feet under the sea, *stays.*

---

They went down the mast in nine minutes, and at forty feet, Kaelen said: *Bells.*

And then nothing for a while.

She heard them through the tube before he said anything more: the Chime, close. Not three notes now — they had stopped ringing the moment he reached the belfry, and what she heard instead was the bells' *hum*, the long after-ring of eight great bronze throats that had been struck and were not being struck now, eight notes laid over each other and dying very slowly in cold water, and over that, in the tube, Kaelen's breath going short and careful.

*Eight bells,* he said at last. *In a frame. Big. The* Gale*'s through the middle of them, through the frame. Her hull's split along — there's a hole. I can see the tanks. Two of them. The other two I can't see.*

*IS ANYTHING THERE?* Lyra wrote, and held it up for the boat, and then remembered he could not see it and put her mouth to the trumpet and whispered it with what was left of her voice. "Is anything there?"

A long pause.

*No,* said Kaelen. *They're just ringing.*

"They're not ringing," said Lyra.

*No. Not now.* Another pause. *My arm is.*

She did not need to be told. She had heard it start, in the tube, under his breathing: a high faint clear tone, glass-thin, five hundred and twenty-eight, the note that she had heard every day for six weeks without wanting to, coming off the bare glass of his arm into the sea. And she had heard, very faintly, the tenor bell of Wendmere — which had been tuned by some long-dead founder to the Velocity Anchor's six hundred and thirty-nine — lean toward it.

Not ring. Lean. The way a dog's head turns.

---

They got the first tank up at eleven.

It took two hours. The *Gale*'s drift-tanks were brass cylinders as long as a man and twice as thick, slung in iron cradles below her main deck, and they had been under the sea for three years with the Drift scouring round them, and the cradle-bolts had grown into the cradles. Kaelen worked them with his right hand and a spanner and then, when the spanner would not do it, with the flat of the glass, very carefully, in short controlled pushes, and Lyra lay on the bottom-boards of the *Patience* with the trumpet against her ear and listened to every one of them land.

He talked to Teague through the water by touch. He told the surface almost nothing. Lyra knew why. Every word he said in that helmet, he said with the glass bare and a hand's breadth from his mouth.

At eleven he said: *Slings on. Haul.* And the Aegis Sentinel stood up in the bow, and took the derrick-line in its two iron hands, and began to pull.

It pulled the way a tide comes in. Hand over hand, without hurry, without stopping, with a faint ticking from somewhere inside its chest like a clock in another room. The *Patience* heeled four strakes to the line and the Harbour men threw their weight to the high side, and the brass cylinder came up out of the green under them like a drowned thing surfacing, streaming, enormous, and the Aegis swung it inboard and set it down on the bottom-boards so gently that it did not even ring.

And then it rang anyway.

Drift-glass is Aether-Glass that has been tuned to lift: Velocity-glass, pale green as sea-ice, packed in brass with a reed-valve at one end. A tank is full of song. This one had been three years in the dark with its valve stopped, and when the air reached the reed it began, very softly, to sing — a sour, cold, drowned little note, six hundred and thirty-nine and flat — and every man in the boat stopped working to listen to it.

Pennick's tanks had sung like that once, Lyra thought. Before the hops and the gaskets and the eleven days. *That is what we are here for,* she thought. *That note. That is four hundred and thirty-two people in a hold.*

"One," said Vane, from the tiller, not looking at the sea. "One and a half if Pennick lies to me."

They brought Kaelen and Teague up the mast and out at noon, grey-lipped and shaking, with the cold in them so deep that Bettany would not let either of them near the brazier for an hour for fear of their hearts. Teague had a cut across his palm from a cradle-bolt and did not seem to know it was there. Kaelen sat in the bow with a blanket over his good shoulder and the bare glass arm across his knees, and the arm was still ringing, very faintly, five hundred and twenty-eight.

The Chime did not ring again all afternoon. They rowed back to the Mole in a silence that Lyra did not like at all.

---

She lay awake that night in the chandler's loft, under the roof, with the rain stopped and the window open on the harbour and the Chime ringing again far out past the Cay, and she counted it.

Three notes. Gap. Three notes. Gap.

She had been counting for most of an hour before she understood what she was counting, and when she did she sat up in the dark so fast that her head swam.

It was not three notes. It had never been three notes. It was a phrase, played slow — so slow that each bell's note had time to die before the next was struck, so that the ear heard them as separate things — and she knew the phrase. She would have known it anywhere. She had sung it on the Long Mole a day and a half ago, over a dead boy, in front of ninety strangers, in a voice like a torn sheet.

The first part of the office. The opening of the Cantor's funeral song of the Reach, which no one on the Rust Coast could possibly know, being rung back to her across a mile and a half of black water by eight drowned bells.

Every rigging-chime in Port Cantabile had answered her. She had thought that was the harbour.

She went down the ladder in the dark in her shift and found Sable, who did not seem to sleep, sitting at the chandler's counter with the five blue bottles and a candle, and wrote on the slate so hard that the pencil squealed.

*THE CHIME HAS MY OFFICE.*

Sable read it. She looked toward the open door, and the dark harbour beyond it, and listened. Lyra watched her face as she found the phrase in the slow bells, and it did not change, which was how Lyra knew she had found it.

"Then it has your voice," said Sable. "Or the shape of it. That is how they learn. You sang near the water, and something below the water was listening, and now it has a little of you." She paused. "Hesper said it had been listening to this harbour for six years."

*THEN IT HAS EVERYONE'S.*

"Yes," said Sable. "I imagine so."

---

They went out again on Driftday morning at the slack and it woke up.

It did not happen all at once. That was what made it bad. If it had come up out of the green in a single shriek, the way a hound comes through a wall, the boat would have known what to do; you can be brave at a shriek. Instead it came the way the cold comes, a little and then more, and by the time anyone in the *Patience* understood that something was happening, it had already happened to Pell.

Kaelen and Teague had been down an hour. They had the cradle of the second tank half-free. The Chime had been silent while they worked, as it had been the day before, and Lyra lay with her ear to the trumpet listening to Kaelen's breath and the ring of his arm, and on the other side of the boat Pell and Mabb were working Teague's pump, *hush-ah, hush-ah*, and Mags was working Kaelen's with her one good arm and her face like a closed door.

And the tenor bell of Wendmere rang once, very softly, forty feet beneath them.

Lyra felt it in the bottom-boards. One note. Not a struck note. A note that had been *leaned on*, the way you lean on a door to see if it will give.

Then the treble. Then the fifth. Then all eight, very quietly, slowly, in a phrase she did not know.

And in the bells, there were voices.

---

She could not afterward say what anyone else heard. People told her, later, some of them; most of them would not. Mabb heard his mother, who had been dead nine years, calling him in for supper off the Lantern Yard in exactly the tone she had used when he was seven and late. One of the Harbour men heard a woman laughing who was not anybody he would name. Vane, at the tiller, heard something that made him take his hand off the tiller and put both hands over his ears and sit like that, rocking very slightly, with his eyes shut — and when Lyra asked him about it, a week later, he looked at her for a long time and said, *the hawser, Cantor,* and nothing else, ever.

Lyra heard herself.

She heard her own voice. Not as it was now. As it had been, before the Root and the wire and the meadow, before the six weeks of blood in her mouth: her whole voice, the voice that had sung four hundred births in the Hollow, clear and warm and low, singing the funeral office of the Reach over the open sea, perfectly — better than she had ever sung it in her life — the way she had always heard it in her head and never once got out of her throat.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard.

It came up out of the water on the starboard side, from under the boat, soft, and it was singing *her*, it was singing for *her*, the second part of the office, the part for the one who is left, and it wanted her to come and listen properly. That was all. Just to put her face down near the water where she could hear it better.

She was on her knees at the gunwale when Mags Tennant hit her.

---

Mags could not hear it.

That was the whole of it, afterward, when they tried to work out how anyone in that boat had lived. Mags Tennant had been deaf from birth. She had grown up on Hesper's houses in the Lantern Yard reading lips and hands and faces, and she had come aboard the *Wave-Cutter* at nineteen because a deaf girl on the Rust Coast can work rigging in a gale as well as any boy and Vane had been the only captain on the Mole who did not care, and she had followed him off the *Cutter* onto the *Kestrel* when there was nothing left of the *Cutter* to follow. She had been the one of the crew no voice could lure for ten years, and she had never once in all that time had a reason to be glad of it.

What Mags saw, on the *Patience*, was a boat where everybody had stopped moving.

She saw the Harbour men go still at the pumps. She saw Vane put his hands over his ears. She saw Mabb let go of his handle and stand with his mouth open, and the pump stop, and Teague's air-pipe go slack on the bottom-boards. She saw Lyra crawl to the gunwale on her knees with her face open like a child's.

And she saw Pell get up.

Pell put down the handle of Teague's pump and stood and stepped up on the thwart with a look on her face of such enormous relief that Mags, who had stood beside her at the pumps for two days and never once seen her look anything but tired, would remember it longer than anything else that happened in that boat. Pell stepped up onto the gunwale. She stood there for a moment, balanced, in her boots, smiling down into the green.

Then she stepped off, the way you step off a quay into a boat that you know is there.

She did not splash. The water took her the way it had taken the helmets: without any fuss at all.

Mags was across the boat by then. She could not get to Pell; Pell was gone. She got to Lyra instead, and took her by the back of the neck with her good hand and dragged her off the gunwale and hit her across the face, open-handed, as hard as she could, and Lyra came back into her own body with her ears ringing and her lip split and Mags's face eight inches from hers, signing, one-handed, furious, so fast that Lyra could barely follow.

*PUMP. PUMP. TEAGUE HAS NO AIR. PUMP.*

---

Lyra got Mabb's handle. Mags went back to Kaelen's.

Two pumps, two women, one of them with a torn throat and a split lip and the other with one arm, *hush-ah, hush-ah*, while the bells rang in the green beneath them and the Harbour men sat at their stations with their faces slack and their hands in their laps, listening to whatever they were listening to.

The voice was still singing. Lyra could still hear it. She worked the pump handle and kept her eyes on the wood of it, on the grain, on a knot in the grain the size of her thumbnail, and she did not look at the water, and the voice sang the office for the one who is left in a voice that was hers and was beautiful and was not hers, and with every stroke she said to herself, in her head, in her own ruined voice, *that is not me. That is not me. That is a thing that has learned me.*

She was thinking it on the fortieth stroke when she understood that she could hear something else in the trumpet.

Kaelen.

She dropped onto the bottom-boards with the trumpet against her ear and her right arm still working the pump-handle above her head, and heard him breathing very fast and hard and, under the breathing, swearing — steady, monotonous, foul, one word after another, the way a man swears when he is holding on to something with all his strength and needs a noise to hold on with.

*— got him — got his pipe — he let go — he let go of the — Lyra. HAUL. Haul us both. Now. NOW—*

She came up on her knees and screamed it.

It came out of her as nothing — a rasp, a whisper, a sound like a nail on slate, the blood coming up into her mouth with it. Nobody in the boat heard it. Nobody in the boat was listening to anything that was in the boat.

Except the Aegis.

It had been sitting in the bow the whole time with its iron hands on its knees. It turned its head. It had no face; it had a smooth curved helm of riveted plate with a slot across it, and inside the slot, very faintly, a light. Lyra did not know what it heard. She did not know if it heard the bells, or her, or Kaelen in the pipe; she did not know if it heard anything at all, or whether there was somebody inside it who could. She only saw it stand up, all nine feet of it, and take the derrick-line in its hands where Kaelen's and Teague's lifelines ran over the sheave together, and pull.

It hauled them up from forty feet in a minute and a half.

They came out of the water in a tangle, two copper helmets and a mass of canvas and pipe and rope, Teague limp and Kaelen with his right hand locked into the neck-ring of Teague's helmet so hard that they had to cut the glove off him later to get it open. Teague had let go of the *Gale*'s mast, Kaelen said afterward, in a dull voice. Just let go. Opened his hands. And the Drift had taken him off it sideways, very fast, the way a thrown stone is taken, out over the drowned town — and Kaelen had seen him go and had let go himself, with one hand, and caught the pipe as it whipped past.

And the glass had held them. Kaelen had jammed the arm through the bell-frame of Wendmere's tower to the shoulder, behind the tenor bell, and hung off it, one-handed, with Teague streaming out on the end of his pipe in the current like a flag, until the line came taut.

"He was talking," said Kaelen. "In the pipe. The whole time. Teague was." He was sitting in the bow with the blanket round him, shaking. "He was telling Colm he was sorry."

---

They did not get the second tank on Driftday. They did not get anything. They rowed back to the Mole in the early afternoon with Teague breathing in the bottom of the boat and Pell's pump-handle lashed down where she had left it, and the Harbour men at the oars with their heads down, not speaking, not looking at each other, and the Chime silent behind them.

Oona Salt was on the Mole. She had seen them coming. She looked at the boat, and counted, and her face did not change.

"Pell," she said.

"Yes," said Vane.

Oona stood for a moment. Then she said, to nobody in particular: "I never knew her other name. Six years she worked my Mole, and I was asked for it on the fourth, and I didn't know it. I've been meaning to find out." She looked at the water. "Well. I'll find it out now."

She went back up the Mole. Lyra watched her go — fifty-six, built like a capstan, in an admiral's coat, walking slowly, with her hands clasped behind her back as if she were holding one of them still with the other.

---

That night they planned the third day on the chandler's counter in grease-pencil, and when it came time to plan it, Vane looked across the room at Kaelen, and opened his mouth, and Kaelen got up before he could say it and went out.

Lyra wrote the plan herself. She wrote it in capitals, on the back of the sail-bag, and she wrote it well, because it was the one thing in the room she knew more about than anyone else: what a voice does, and who it does it to.

*IT SINGS THROUGH THE BELLS. IT SINGS YOU WHAT YOU WANT MOST.*
*WAX IN EVERY EAR IN THE BOAT. WAX AND WOOL AND A CAP TIED DOWN.*
*MAGS HAS THE PUMPS. MAGS IS THE ONLY ONE IT CANNOT HAVE.*
*EVERYONE ON A LINE TO THE MAST. TIE THEM THERE. SHORT LINES.*
*I DO NOT WEAR WAX. SOMEONE HAS TO HEAR THE TUBE.*
*TIE ME SHORTEST.*

Vane read it over her shoulder. He read it twice.

"No," he said.

She wrote: *YOU WILL BE WAXED. YOU WILL NOT HEAR ME.*

"Cantor—"

*IT HAS MY VOICE ALREADY,* she wrote. *IT CANNOT TAKE IT TWICE.*

That was not true, and she knew it was not true while she was writing it, and so did Sable, who was watching her from the end of the counter with a candle and five blue bottles and an expression Lyra could not read. A trick works on a mimic once; everybody in the Reach knew that. Nothing in the Reach had ever said the same thing about a person.

Vane looked at the sail-bag for a long time.

"Teague won't go," he said finally. "Bettany says he'll be lucky to stand by Nullday. So the engineer goes down alone."

*YES.*

"And there's two tanks still in that wreck, and I need both, and I'll get one of them if I'm lucky."

*YES.*

"And if it does what it did today, with the engineer alone at forty feet and nobody in the boat able to hear a thing but you—"

*THEN I WILL HEAR IT,* Lyra wrote, *AND I WILL KNOW WHAT IT IS, AND I WILL NOT GO TO IT.*

She looked up at him. He looked back.

He tied her himself the next morning. He used a bowline round her waist and a round turn and two half-hitches on the *Patience*'s stubby mast, a fathom of line between, so that she could reach the trumpet and the bottom-boards and nothing else, and he tested it twice with his whole weight, not looking at her face. Then he put wax in his own ears, and wool over that, and a knitted cap tied down under his chin, and took the tiller.

---

It was Memoryday. The weather was coming. They could see it over the western horizon, a dark band that had not been there at dawn, with the light under it the colour of a bruise.

Kaelen went down the *Gale*'s mast alone at the slack of the morning tide with Mags on his pump and the Aegis at his line and Lyra tied to the mast with her ear to the trumpet, and for the first forty minutes, nothing happened at all.

He reached the belfry. He reached the hull. She heard his arm begin to ring, five hundred and twenty-eight, and heard the tenor bell lean toward it, and lean, and not ring. She heard the spanner on the cradle-bolts, and the slow careful pushes of the glass. She heard his breath, short and steady. She heard him, once, start to hum — very low, under his breath, inside the copper — the Root note she had taught him at eleven years old for when things hurt, a hundred and seventy-four, and then stop himself, because the glass was bare and an old man five hundred miles away could hear it.

At forty-one minutes he said: *Slings on the second. Third's right behind it. I can get both. Give me twenty minutes.*

She had the slate in her lap. She wrote *BOTH. TWENTY MINUTES* and held it up, and the boat saw it — Mags at the pump, Vane at the tiller, the Harbour men with their capped heads — and nobody could cheer, because nobody could hear anybody else, so Vane lifted one hand off the tiller and made a fist of it, and that was all.

At fifty-three minutes the bells began.

Not softly, this time. Not leaning. All eight of them at once, struck, hard, a great broken bronze roar coming up out of the green under the boat so loud that Lyra felt it in her teeth and her breastbone and the bottom-boards jumped under her knees, and the Harbour men in their wax felt it too, because she saw every one of them turn his head.

And in the trumpet, Kaelen said: *Lyra.*

She pressed the brass to her ear so hard it hurt.

*Lyra. Listen to me. Don't haul.*

The bells rang. The voice in the tube was his. It was exactly his: the low dry engineer's voice with the faint crystalline ring under it that she had known since he was eleven, the voice he used when he had already made up his mind, flat and quiet and very tired.

*There's something in the bells. It's got hold of the line. If you haul, it comes up with me. Into the boat. Into all of you.* A breath. *Leave me. Let go of the line. Cut it, Lyra.*

She knelt on the bottom-boards with her ear to the trumpet and the bells roaring and could not move.

She did not know. That was the thing that would wake her for the rest of her life: that she did not *know*. The voice was his, and it was saying what he would say. It was saying the one thing in the world she would have bet her life Kaelen Vance would say, forty feet down, alone, with something in the bells — *leave me, cut it, let me carry it* — the thing he had been saying in one form or another since he was seventeen. A mimic says what it has learned. A man says what he is. And she could not hear the difference, because they were the same.

She looked up.

Across the boat, Mags Tennant had stopped looking at the pump.

Mags had Kaelen's signal line in her good hand: the thin tarred cord that ran down beside the air-pipe to his wrist, for when the speaking-tube failed. She was holding it the way Lyra had seen Bettany hold a wrist to feel for a pulse, palm-up, the cord across the pads of her fingers, her eyes half-closed, her face entirely still. She could not hear the bells. She could not hear the tube. She could not hear anything in the world.

She was reading the line.

Lyra saw the cord twitch across Mags's fingers. Once, twice, three, four — short and hard and fast — a man's hand, forty feet down, jerking at a rope. Four pulls. Haul. Haul fast.

Mags opened her eyes and looked at Lyra across the boat.

She took her hand off the line and signed, one-handed, a single word.

*HAUL.*

## CHAPTER FIVE
### Forty Feet

> *Down there you have three friends: your pipe, your line, and the man on the pump. Trust them in that order. Trust nothing you hear.*
>
> — Cantabile divers' saying

*The Sunken Carillon. Memoryday the seventh of Ashfall. Fifty-three minutes down.*

---

At forty feet in the Drift, the sea had forgotten which way was up, and Kaelen Vance was standing inside his own breath.

It had taken him the first morning to get used to that and he had not got used to it. Every breath he let out of the helmet's exhaust-valve went out in a gout of silver and then *stopped*, an arm's length from the copper, and hung. It did not rise. It trembled a little in the current, the way a cobweb trembles in a draught, and stayed. Fifty-three minutes of breathing at a breath every four seconds made eight hundred breaths, near enough, and all eight hundred of them were still here, in a loose bright drifting cloud around him in the green, so that he worked in a room whose walls were his own exhalations and which grew smaller every minute he stayed.

He could see perhaps nine feet. Beyond that the cloud closed, silver on green.

Inside the nine feet was the work.

He was on the *Seraph's Gale*'s main deck — or what had been her main deck, which was now a slope of grey planking tilted thirty degrees down to starboard and furred with weed — at the place where her back had broken across the parapet of Wendmere's tower. The hull split under him in a long dark wound along the starboard side, and through the split, in the black of her belly, the third drift-tank lay in its iron cradle where the second one had been until four minutes ago. He had the slings round the second one and it was hanging now from the derrick-line above him in the green, a brass shape going slowly up into the silver, out of sight. Up to the boat. Up to Lyra.

Around him and above him and below him, the belfry.

He had drawn it on the back of a tank-chart on the second night because he could not stop drawing it, and he knew it now the way he had once known the inside of a bore-head. Wendmere's tower was square and forty feet on a side at the belfry stage, with tall louvred openings in each wall that had long since lost their louvres, so that the belfry was a stone box open on four sides to the sea. In the middle of it stood the bell-frame: a great cage of iron-strapped oak twelve feet high, in which the eight bells of Wendmere hung in a ring, mouth-down, the treble the size of a bucket and the tenor the size of a cart. The *Gale* had come down through the frame from the north-west in the storm of '16 and stopped there, her keel across the west parapet and her bow in the frame, and three years of her weight on the oak had bent the whole cage over toward the south-east like a tree in a wind.

The bells hung crooked in it. Seven of them, a little crooked. The tenor badly.

The tenor was nine feet from him, through the frame, to his left. He had his left arm through the frame beside it. That was how he held station in the Drift: arm through the oak to the shoulder like a bolt through a hasp, the glass hooked behind the frame's main brace, so that the current could pull at him all it liked and only had a hundredweight of Light-glass to argue with. The arm had been ringing for fifty-three minutes, very faintly, five hundred and twenty-eight. The tenor had been leaning toward it the whole time, the way a dog's head turns.

And then all eight bells were struck at once.

---

It went through him like a blow to the chest.

Underwater, sound does not come from anywhere. It is simply everywhere at once, inside the copper and inside the bone, and a peal of eight bronze bells struck together at a range of nine feet is not a sound at all but a weight — he felt it land in his breastbone and his teeth and the back of his eyes, felt the helmet ring with it like a struck pot, felt the arm in the frame take it and *answer*.

Five hundred and twenty-eight, at full voice.

And the cloud moved.

That was how he saw her, in the end. Not with his eyes — there was nothing to see with eyes, no shape in the green, no body. With his breath. Eight hundred silver breaths hanging in the water around him, and every one of them shivered at once to the stroke of the bells, and *arranged*. The way sand arranges on a struck plate. The way iron filings stand up along a lodestone's lines. The bubbles slid and gathered and thinned along lines of pressure he could not see, and for as long as the bells rang, the cloud that had been his breath took on the shape of the thing that was ringing them.

It was very large.

It filled the belfry. It was in the frame and around the frame and through the frame, in among the bells, a shape like a woman drawn by somebody who had heard women described but never seen one: long, too long, a head that was mostly mouth, arms that went on past where arms should end and became lines of pressure running up the inside of each bell's bronze throat, so that she wore the bells the way a hand wears rings. Her face was nine feet from his. It was made of his breath. It had his breath's eyes.

She sang.

He did not hear it with his ears either. He heard it in the glass. The bells rang, and under their ringing, very precisely, she took the note his arm was making — the note he had come down here carrying, the note that had been ringing off his bare glass into her water for three days — and she sang it back at him. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Clean and exact and sustained, in the voice of forty copper-wired throats on a riser at the Siphon Foundry at the three hundredth sunrise. She had learned it off his arm. She was giving it back.

And his arm locked.

---

He knew what it was the instant it happened, because it had been done to him twice before — by the proctors' hand-presses in the plaza at Whisper-Grove, tuned to seven hundred and forty-one, and by the Aegis's own chord in the Cloister — and twice had been more than enough. A tone struck at a glassed limb's own pitch locks it rigid. Not stiff: *rigid*. Every crystal in the glass falls into step with the note and stops, and the arm becomes a single solid thing, a casting, with no more give in it than a church bell, and the man it is attached to becomes a man attached to a church bell.

His arm was through the bell-frame to the shoulder.

He was not holding station in the Drift any more. He was bolted to it.

He pulled. He put his boots against the oak and his right hand on the brace and pulled with everything in his legs and back, and the arm did not move a quarter-inch, and the current took the rest of him and laid him out sideways along the frame like a flag on a pole, and the helmet banged against an iron strap, and the cloud of his breath, which was her, leaned in.

*Kaelen,* said Lyra, in his helmet.

---

It came out of the speaking-tube. Out of the brass cup by his mouth, where for three days he had heard Lyra's breathing and her ruined whisper and, once, very faintly, her humming him the Root note through forty feet of pipe when she thought he had stopped listening.

It was not her ruined whisper now.

It was her voice. Her whole voice. The voice she had had in the Hollow before the Root took it, before the wire and the meadow and the six weeks of blood: warm and low and clear and unhurried, the voice that had sung four hundred births, coming up out of the brass into the copper around his head like warm water into a cold bath.

*Kaelen. Love. You can stop now.*

She had never in her life called him that. He knew it. He knew it the way he knew the load on a bolt. And it did not matter at all, because it was her voice, and it was what he had wanted her to say for twenty-three years.

*You've carried it far enough,* said the voice in the tube. *Let me have it. Take the glass off your face so I can see you. Just for a moment. It's only water. I'm right here.*

The bells rang. The breath-shape leaned in, nine feet, seven.

*Take the glass off, love.*

He had his right hand on the faceplate's wing-nut before he knew it was there.

---

Here is the thing about a mind built for failure analysis, which Kaelen Vance would have told you, if you had asked him, was the only thing his head had ever been good for.

It does not stop. Not for grief, not for fear, not for the most beautiful voice it has ever heard asking it to take a copper faceplate off at forty feet. It goes on, behind and underneath everything else, coldly, like a clerk in a back room totting up a column while the house burns, asking the only question it knows how to ask: *where is the load going?*

His hand was on the wing-nut. Lyra's voice was in the tube.

And the clerk in the back room said: *the tube runs both ways.*

It was a speaking-tube. A brass cup at his mouth, a brass trumpet at hers, forty feet of oiled linen hose between, lashed to the air-pipe. Sound went up it and sound came down it. The thing in the bells was singing into the tube. It was singing *down* the tube to him in Lyra's voice.

Which meant it could sing *up* the tube to Lyra in his.

He took his hand off the wing-nut.

He knew what it would say to her. That was the thing that went through him colder than the water: he knew exactly. It had been listening to him for three days in this helmet with the glass bare. It had his voice. And it would say to her what he would say, because what he would say was the one thing it had heard him say, over and over, in one form or another, every day since he was seventeen years old.

*Leave me. Let go. Cut the line. Let me carry it.*

And she would not be able to tell. Not because she was a fool. Because it would be *true*. Because a mimic says what it has learned and a man says what he is, and for twenty years he had been teaching everyone who loved him that those were the same thing.

He did not speak into the tube.

There was nothing he could say into it that the thing in the bells could not say better in his own voice. Anything he said, it would have. Anything he shouted, it would shout louder. Words were poisoned. Every word he had was already in the water.

So he did not use a word.

He reached up past the tube with his right hand, past the air-pipe, to the thin tarred cord that ran down beside them to the lanyard on his wrist — the signal line, the oldest thing in the dive, older than tubes and bellows, a piece of string from a man to a boat — and he took it in his fist, and he pulled it.

Once. Twice. Three times. Four. Short, hard, as fast as he could.

*Haul. Haul fast.*

And then he held on to it, and waited, and for a long moment nothing at all came back down the string.

---

The air went thin.

He felt it at once. The bellows had a rhythm — *hush-ah, hush-ah*, a push of cool air across his face every two seconds that he had stopped noticing on the first morning, the way you stop noticing your own heart — and it stopped. The helmet's air went still and close and began, almost at once, to taste of his own breath. He had perhaps three minutes in the copper. Teague had told him that the first morning. *If the pump stops, you've got three minutes, and the first one's fine, and the second one you'll think is fine, and the third one you won't think anything.*

Somebody up there had let go of the pump.

*Mags,* he thought. *Mags has let go of the pump to read the line.*

The voice in the tube said, in Lyra's whole voice, so tenderly: *There. You see? They've let you go. It's all right. You can rest now.*

He hung on the bell-frame of Wendmere with his arm locked to the shoulder and his air going stale and the breath-woman leaning in through the frame toward the faceplate, five feet, four, and held the signal line in his fist and counted, because counting was a thing to do with the part of his head that wanted to scream.

At eleven, the line came taut.

Not a pull. Not the jerk of a hand answering. The long, slow, absolute tautness of a weight being taken up — the line going hard as a bar from his wrist up through the silver cloud into the green above — and he knew the hands on it, though he could not see them, because no human crew had ever hauled like that. Hand over hand, without hurry, without stopping. Like a tide.

The Aegis had the line.

And at fourteen, the bellows came back. *Hush-ah.* Cold air across his face. *Hush-ah.*

He breathed it in and nearly sobbed.

---

The haul took him by the wrist and the waist and tried to lift him off the frame, and could not, because his arm was a casting bolted through two-hundred-year-old oak, and something had to give.

He felt the load go into him.

It went into the ribs first. The haul came on at the waist, through the belt, straight across the place where they had gone from mended to not on the Salt Stair, and he felt the cracked ends grate, and the copper filled with a sound he was glad nobody could hear.

He felt it the way he had felt the Salt Stair's stanchion: not as pain, though there was pain, the shoulder-joint where flesh met glass shrieking as the haul pulled one way and the lock held the other — but as a *path*. A line of force running from the Aegis's iron hands down through forty feet of tarred cord, into the lanyard, into his wrist, through his body, out along the locked glass arm, into the bell-frame, into the brace behind the tenor bell. He could feel exactly where it went. He could feel exactly where it stopped.

It stopped at the tenor's headstock.

He saw it as clearly as if he had drawn it. The tenor of Wendmere hung from a beam of black oak by two iron gudgeons, one at each end, sitting in brass bearings in the frame. Three years of the *Gale*'s weight on the north-west corner of the cage had bent the whole frame south-east, and the tenor's beam with it, so that the tenor's weight — a ton and a half of bronze, more — no longer hung down through both bearings but sat almost entirely on the south-east one. One gudgeon. One bearing. Three years of the Drift scouring round it, and a broken ship leaning on the frame, and a ton and a half on a single iron pin as thick as his wrist.

And his locked arm lay along the brace directly beneath that pin, like a pry-bar laid under a stone.

All it would take was a lever and a load.

He had a lever. It was locked to his shoulder and it would not bend.

He had a load. It was nine feet tall and ticking and hauling on his wrist from forty feet above like a tide.

The thing in the bells sang five hundred and twenty-eight in his arm and breathed at him through the faceplate, three feet, two, a mouth made of silver, his own eyes.

Kaelen Vance stopped resisting the haul.

He let it take him. He let it take his whole body up and off the frame, every ounce of the Aegis's pull going through him into the locked arm, and he turned his hips as it took him so that the arm — rigid, bolted, with no more give in it than a church bell — rotated in the frame like a crowbar on its heel, and drove its upper edge straight up into the underside of the tenor's south-east gudgeon with a hundredweight of glass and the whole of a nine-foot automaton's back behind it.

The pin sheared.

---

He heard it. Everyone in Port Cantabile heard it, people told him later: a single enormous crack out of the sea, like a mountain clearing its throat, that rolled across the harbour and up the Salt Stair and set every rigging-chime on the Long Mole jangling.

The tenor of Wendmere dropped out of its frame.

One end first, the sheared end, so that the great bronze cup swung down and round on its remaining gudgeon and struck the frame's lower rail mouth-first, and *split* — he saw the crack open in the bronze, a black line jumping across the lip — and then the second gudgeon tore out of its bearing and the bell went, a ton and a half of cracked bronze tumbling end over end out of the bottom of the cage, down through the stone floor of the belfry where the floor had rotted, into the dark of the tower beneath, toward seventy-five feet.

And at the instant the bell split, his arm was against it.

The glass drinks what it breaks. He had known that since the clamps outside Oakhaven, since the first hound; it was the whole law of his life now. A glassed limb that breaks a Cacophony drinks the sound the thing is made of. He had drunk hounds. He had drunk a Legionnaire's bell with a girl's name in it. He had never in his life put the glass against something that big when it broke.

The tenor was where she lived. The deepest voice in the peal. The throat she rang the others with.

The arm drank it.

---

He did not, afterward, have the words. He tried, for Lyra, a week later, with the slate between them, and gave up.

It was not like the hounds. Hounds went into the glass like a burn going into a hand: a shock, and then heat, and the tideline climbing a little toward his collarbone in a creep of frost. This was not a little. This was a ton and a half of bronze that had rung for two hundred years over a fishing town, and four hundred people who had gone under with it in a single afternoon in the Screaming Year, and the Hollis brothers, and Dunnet's crew, and everyone who had ever spoken at the waterline in Port Cantabile for six years, and Pell — he felt Pell, he would swear to it, a woman's relief, a single step off a quay — all of it, the whole bottom of the thing she was, coming up through the glass into him at once like the sea coming in through a broken seacock.

He felt the tideline move.

Not creep. *Move*. He felt it go along his collarbone like a cold hand laid flat, toward his throat, and stop there — two fingers, two and a half, a cold that went in and in and settled under the bone like a stone on a grave — and he felt the arm, for the first time in seven years, sing *two* notes at once. Five hundred and twenty-eight, high and cyan and holy. And under it, cold and green and moving, six hundred and thirty-nine.

The lock broke.

It broke because the note that held it broke. The thing in the bells could not sing five hundred and twenty-eight with no throat to sing it in, and the arm came out of step with her all at once, every crystal at once, and came free in the frame like a bolt coming out of a hasp.

And the breath-cloud around him, eight hundred silver breaths, fell out of her shape and hung there, just bubbles, trembling in the current. He looked through them at nine feet of green water and the crooked frame and the seven bells left in it, hanging silent, and there was nobody there.

Nobody at all.

He would never know whether he had killed her or only taken her voice. Sable said, afterward, that for a Cacophony those might be the same thing. Sable also said she had no entry for either.

---

Then the line went down.

It happened very fast, and he understood it only as it was happening: the tenor's broken headstock had taken his lifeline with it.

The line ran from his wrist up through the frame to the boat, and he had been hauled up and across the cage when the pin sheared, and somewhere in the half-second of the bell's fall a bight of tarred cord a yard above his wrist had dropped across the headstock's iron strap, and the bell had taken it. A ton and a half of bronze falling down a seventy-five-foot tower, with his line round its neck and him on the last yard of it like a second weight on a plumb-line.

The line snapped taut and went *down*.

It took him off the frame. It took him down through the belfry floor after the bell, wrist-first, into the black of the tower, and the silver of his breath went past him upward like a flock of birds — and then stopped them both with a jerk that wrenched his shoulder half out of its socket, eight feet into the dark, the bell hanging on the line beside him and him hanging upside down on the yard of cord below the snag, and something, far above, holding the whole of it.

Something was holding it from above.

He hung there and felt it. The lifeline was a bar from his wrist up through the belfry floor into the green, and it was holding a ton and a half of falling bronze, and every ounce of that was going up the line to forty feet above, to the *Patience*'s derrick, to a thirty-foot open boat with a stubby mast and a four-strake freeboard on a sea that was beginning, he could feel it in the line, to get up ahead of the weather.

The boat would go over. He could feel that in the line too: the heel, the way the line was coming in from the sheave at an angle now, not straight down. A ton and a half was more than the *Patience* could carry on one side.

Somebody up there was going to have to cut it.

He knew who would be nearest the line. He knew who had a knife in his belt. He had watched him for three days not looking at the water.

*Cut it,* he thought, upside down in the dark. *Vane, cut it. Your ship. Cut it. It's the right call.*

The line held.

He counted. He did not know why he counted; it was what he did. One. Two. Three. The line held. Four. Five. Somewhere up there the *Patience* was going over on her beam with a ton and a half of bronze on her derrick and a man with a knife and wax in his ears beside the line. Six. Seven. The line did not even tremble. Eight. Nine.

At ten, Kaelen Vance understood that it was not going to be cut, and that he had better do something about it himself.

---

He came up the line hand over hand with his right hand and his left, and the left was free and it was a hundredweight of glass that rang two notes now, and he did not need it to be gentle.

The bell hung beside him in the dark of the tower, twisting slowly on the line. He could not see it. He did not need to see it. He could hear exactly where the line was fouled, because the bell was ringing — a dull cracked tremor running up the cord into his hand — and the note changed where the cord crossed iron.

He reached for it, upside down in a black shaft with his breath going past him in the wrong direction, and found the headstock strap with his right hand by feel, and the loop of cord across it, and the shackle-pin of the strap where the iron had rusted thin, and put the heel of his glass hand against it and *struck*.

Once.

The shackle broke. The bell went down into the dark of Wendmere's tower and he felt it go, a long falling tremor through the water and the stone and his boots, and then, a long time later, far below, a sound like a door closing in another house.

The line went slack in his fist for half a second.

And then it went taut again, very fast, and took him up.

---

He came out of the water into a world that had tipped over.

The *Patience* was on her beam-ends. That was the first thing he saw through the faceplate when his helmet broke the surface: the sky where the sea should have been, and the boat lying on her side with her starboard gunwale under and green water pouring in over it in a sheet, and the stubby mast canted out over the waves at forty-five degrees with the derrick at its head and the Aegis Sentinel braced against the foot of it, all nine feet, its iron feet jammed into the bottom-boards and its back against the mast-step and both hands on his line. Then the line went slack and the boat came back up — slowly, wallowing, half-full — and the Aegis hauled the last of him in over the side like a fish, and he came down on the bottom-boards in eighteen inches of water with somebody already at the wing-nuts of his faceplate.

Mags. Mags, one-handed, her face white and furious.

The faceplate came off. Air. Real air, wet and cold and stinking of weed and tar and the coming weather, and noise, so much noise — the wind and the sea slapping and the boat groaning and somebody retching and the rigging-chimes of the whole Long Mole still jangling a mile and a half away from the crack of the bell — and Lyra's face, close, closer than it had been in six weeks, with her lip split and blood at the corner of her mouth and a rope round her waist, and her hands on either side of the helmet.

She did not say anything. She could not have. She looked at him.

He said, "It's me."

She kept looking.

"It's me, Lyra. It had my voice. It was in the tube. I didn't say any of that." His teeth were going. "Whatever it said. I didn't—"

She put her hand over his mouth.

She held it there. He could feel her palm shaking. Then she took it away and wrote on the slate in her lap, without looking down, so that the letters went off the edge:

*I KNOW. MAGS KNEW. YOU USED THE STRING.*

---

Vane was not at the tiller.

He had left it when the boat went over. He was on his knees by the mast-step, forward of the Aegis, in water to his thighs, with the wax still in his ears and the knitted cap still tied down under his chin, and his left hand wrapped in the lifeline where it came down off the sheave — between the derrick-head and the Aegis's iron fists, the one place in the whole boat where a knife would have set her free. He had taken a turn of it round his own fist. The tarred cord had cut through his palm to the meat and there was blood running down his wrist into his sleeve, and the knife was in his belt.

The knife was in its sheath. Lyra would tell Kaelen later — on the slate, on the *Kestrel*, that night — that she had watched him draw it. When the boat went over, with the line going down into the sea like a bar and the gunwale going under, the Harbour men shouting soundlessly at him through their wax, pointing at the line, *cut it, cut it,* she had watched Vane draw the knife and put the edge on the cord.

And hold it there.

She did not know how long. She had not been counting. Long enough. And then, she said, he had put the knife back in his belt, quite slowly, like a man putting a thing down that he would never pick up again, and wrapped the line round his fist instead, and sat there with the sea coming in over the gunwale and held on.

Kaelen looked at him across the half-swamped boat.

Vane looked back. He was grey to the lips. He could not hear anything; he was waxed; he could only see. He looked at Kaelen, out of the helmet, alive, and his face did a thing Kaelen had never seen it do, and then he laughed — the loud one, the one from the side of his mouth, the one the whole crew knew meant he was frightened half to death — and turned and was quietly, thoroughly sick over the side into the sea he had not looked at in five years.

---

They brought two tanks into Port Cantabile that afternoon ahead of the weather: the one from Dawnday, and the second, which had gone up on the derrick-line four minutes before the bells and had hung in the green under the *Patience* through the whole of it, swinging, untouched, singing its sour little drowned note to itself.

The third and fourth were still in the *Gale*'s belly at forty feet. Nobody was going back for them. The weather was coming over the Cay by four o'clock, a black wall of it with the rain walking on the water in front, and the Chime was silent behind them, and stayed so.

Seven bells hung in the frame of Wendmere. The tenor was at the bottom of the tower in the dark. And nobody, then or afterward, ever heard any of the seven ring again.

---

Oona Salt met them on the Long Mole in the first of the rain with her coat buttoned to the throat, and looked at the two tanks on the stones, and at Teague's empty place in the boat, and at Vane's hand, and at Kaelen.

Kaelen had come up the Mole steps with his collar open, because it no longer made any difference. Everyone on the Mole could see the glass now. It came up out of his shirt in a sheet of pale cyan along the whole length of the collarbone toward the hollow of his throat — Bettany would measure it that night at six and a half fingers from the point of the shoulder, two more than on Stasisday, an inch and a half from the throat — and where it had crossed the collarbone that afternoon there was a new thing in it, a thread of colder colour running through the cyan like a vein through marble: green, the pale moving green of sea-ice, of drift-glass, of the water off Wendmere.

Oona looked at it for a long time.

"Two," she said.

"Two," said Vane. He was holding his bandaged hand against his chest. "Your two. I'll have the third off you in some other life."

"You'll have it off me now." She did not look at him. She was still looking at the green in Kaelen's glass. "Take one. I'll take one. Bench free, debt cleared, and Pennick can strip the Dawnday tank for the parts he wants and I'll have the rest back."

Vane said nothing for a moment.

"That's not what you said on the fourth."

"No," said Oona Salt. "On the fourth I hadn't watched a man come up out of the Chime with the sea in his arm." She looked at Lyra then, and at the slate on Lyra's belt, and at the ship on the tower behind them all with its patched envelope swinging in the wind and its hold full of four hundred and thirty-two people who said their names at night. "And I've a price besides. It's not money. You'll pay it before you leave my harbour, or you won't leave it."

"Name it."

She took her hand out of the admiral's coat. There was a bottle in it. Blue glass, palm-high, the wax at the neck unbroken, and the paper label legible in a Cantabile scribe's careful hand.

**MERRET SALT. HARBOUR PILOT. LOST OFF THE CHIME, 414.**

"Some of them are real," said Oona. "Hesper will have told you. Some of them are real, and you can't know which till you take the stopper out, and if you take the stopper out wrong you get what the *Nancy* got." She held it out — not to Vane. To Lyra. "You've got a Cantor. You've got a press on your foredeck that the Catalogue made for taking a person out of themselves and putting them in glass, and I'm told your scribe has been learning to run it backward. And you've got the only man on this coast who's gone down into the Chime and come up again."

Lyra looked at the bottle in Oona's hand. She did not take it.

"My brother taught me to swim off this Mole," said Oona Salt. "He was the best pilot on the Rust Coast and he went out to the Chime in '14 to bring in a boat that had no business being there, and I sold the man who bottled him four sovereigns' worth of licence a week for five years." The rain was coming down harder now. She did not seem to notice it. "If he's in there, I want him out. If he's not, I want to know what is. That's my price, Cantor. Not tonight. Not before you're ready. But before you leave my harbour."

Lyra took the bottle.

She held it in both hands. It was cold from the admiral's coat and heavier than it should have been, and when she held it close to her ear — Kaelen saw her do it, he would remember it, she did it the way she held a hand to a fevered child's chest — she went quite still.

She did not write anything on the slate. She did not need to. He could see her face.

Something in the bottle was breathing.

## CHAPTER SIX
### The Top of the Stair

> *The subject should be addressed by name throughout the procedure. It calms them, and it is the last occasion on which the name will be of any use to them.*
>
> — Office of the Catalogue, *Harvest Schedule*, fourth revision. Drafted by the Veil scribe designated Sable.

*Port Cantabile. Nullday the eighth of Ashfall.*

---

Entry. The eighth of Ashfall, 419. Nullday. The Long Mole at Port Cantabile, first light, wind south-west and rising. The first blow had gone through on Memoryday night and stood off now over the Cay, gathering for a second.

Two of Hesper's boats lay alongside the Mole steps with their sails half up. The *Gannet*, which was hers, and the *Fair Return*, which was her sister's, and which nobody on the Mole would call by its name that morning, because it seemed to them all a cruelty.

Sable stood at the head of the steps with a pencil and an ordinary notebook bought in the Lantern Yard for a copper, and wrote down the people who went into them.

She did not use the Ledger. The Ledger lay closed in its oilcloth under her arm. The Ledger kept names safe, and it charged for that by weight, and she had two things left of herself to pay with and four hundred and thirty-two people in a hold who had a prior claim. This was not that. This was a list. It cost nothing. She had been a cataloguer for nineteen years and she knew the difference precisely: a list is a thing that costs you nothing, and that is how you know it is only a list.

She wrote them anyway. Somebody should.

*Ruth Madder, of the Hollow, Oakhaven. Twenty-nine. With her daughter, not yet named, twenty-three days old.*

*Coll Madder, of the Hollow, Oakhaven. Thirty-four. Deceased. To be buried on the high rock at Driftwood Cay, in earth.*

*Lissa Merrow, of the Weavers' Row. Without hands since Rootday of the last week of Harvestmonth. Walked down the steps unaided and refused the arm offered.*

*Bettis Renn. Recovered from the harvest-house at the Malting. Has not spoken a word since the Cloister road, and has stopped screaming. Carried.*

Nine children. Sable wrote down all nine. Two of them had not spoken since the Greening, and she noted which two, because someone on the Cay would need to know it, and she noted the names they had come aboard the *Kestrel* with and the names they answered to now, which in one case were different, and she noted that the smallest of them, a girl of five called Tansy, held on to Lyra's skirt at the top of the steps for so long that in the end Hetty Bram had to take her fingers off it one at a time.

Lyra did not help. She stood with her hands at her sides and let it be done, and did not say anything, because she did not trust what was left of her voice, and because, Sable thought, writing it down, she did not trust what her face would do if she moved it.

A kindness, everybody on the Mole kept saying to each other in murmurs. A kindness. Hesper's people would have them. Hesper took in the Coast's wreck-orphans and had for six years. They would have a roof, and a rock to grow things on, and the money Vane had put into Hesper's hand the night before, which was every sovereign left in the *Kestrel*'s strongbox. They would be out of it. They would be off the ship that everyone was hunting, and out of the war that was coming, and away from the man with the glass arm and the singer with no voice and the press on the foredeck.

It was a kindness. Sable wrote that down too, because it was true. And then underneath it she wrote the other true thing, which was that every person going down those steps knew perfectly well that they were being put ashore so that the *Kestrel* could go and do something they would not survive, and that not one of them had been asked.

Hetty Bram did not go. Hetty Bram stood on the Mole with her arms folded and said, "I have buried a town, and I will not be put on a rock like a parcel," and nobody had argued with Hetty Bram in Sable's hearing, or in anybody's hearing, since the Root. Bettany did not go, because Tobiah Marsh had a gaff-hole under his rib and Teague had a cold in his chest that might turn, and somebody had to sit with them. Nell Carder stood at the top of the steps with her shawl over her head for a long time, looking at the boats and then back at the ship on the tower, and in the end went down to the *Gannet* with the children, because, she said, they would need somebody who knew their names. *For now,* she said. Sable wrote that down exactly as it was said.

The boats cast off at a quarter past seven.

Nobody on the Mole sang anything. It was not that kind of coast, and it was not that kind of morning, and the only person who might have was standing at the top of the steps with both hands pressed flat against her mouth.

---

Entry. The same day, mid-morning. The chandler's back room.

Sable had stolen the daybook on the night of the fourth. She did not record that in any official place.

It had been on a shelf under the counter of the last bottle-dealer's shop on the sixth landing, behind a tin of chalk, and she had taken it while the Harbour's people were rolling the two dead men off the stone onto sailcloth, and put it under her coat beside the Ledger, and walked down six flights of the Salt Stair with it. Harbour property, under the Twelfth. Theft from the Harbour's own pocket, which carries the drop. She had recited the Article to eight armed men an hour earlier in the voice of a woman reading a will. She had noticed the irony at the time, and catalogued it, and stolen the book anyway, because it was a book, and because nobody else in Port Cantabile was going to read it.

She had been reading it for three nights. It was not hard. It was only dull, and she was very good at dull.

A dealer's daybook. Six years of it, in a neat clerk's copperplate — the same hand, she had noticed on the first night, as one of the four labels on the counter. Purchases on the left, sales on the right. The purchases were all from the same three or four boats, and against each was a date and a mark, *C.D.L.*, which Sable had decided by the end of the first night meant *Cape Dissonance lip*. A case of twenty. A case of forty. In the Screaming Year, at a sovereign a bottle; by '17, at three.

The sales were to people. That had been the second night. Names, landings, prices. *Widow Kell, second landing, her husband, 4. Old Corran, for his boys, 2 btl., 7.* And the trade: *To J. Pike, 2nd landing, case of 20, at 2* — Pike, who had written his own labels in a big slow print and sold one of them to Vane. Hundreds of entries. Sable had read every one of them, and had been glad, for the first time since she stole it, that she was reading a daybook and not the Ledger, because the Ledger would have wanted them all and she would have had to say no.

And then on the third night, last night, sitting up at the counter beside five blue bottles while the boy Tobiah breathed in the next room and a candle burned down, she had found the other column.

Once a month, on the first Nullday, at the foot of the page, in a different ink: *To the House. Tuning stock. 40 btl.* And a price. Six sovereigns the bottle. Better than he got from widows.

Forty bottles a month. For six years.

She had sat with that for a while. Then she had turned to the back of the daybook, where a careful man keeps his carrier's receipts, and found them, a thin sheaf of them, each one stamped at the top in red wax with a mark she had last seen on the lintel of a door she had walked through every morning for nineteen years.

A key, crossed with a quill, inside a ring.

*Office of the Catalogue. Hall of Carriage. The Organum.*

---

Here is what Sable knew about the Hall of Carriage, which she did not say aloud in the chandler's back room, or anywhere, to anyone.

It is a long grey building on the east side of the Organum's Registry Square, three floors and a basement. It does not appear on public plans of the city. It is where the Catalogue sends what it takes. The cores from Whisper-Grove went there before they went to the Legions. The pressings from the harvest-houses go there to be sorted and graded and assigned. And on the third floor, at the end of a corridor with a carpet on it, in a room Sable had been inside exactly once, there is a cabinet of very small cores in velvet trays that are not assigned to anything at all, because they belong to the Office's own staff, and are kept against the day the staff might need reminding of their duty.

She had been told, the one time she was in that room, that hers was in the third tray.

She had two things left of herself. Her mother's hands in her hair. The smell of the Veil's scriptorium on a winter morning. She had given up the third three weeks ago in the harvest-house at the Malting to put a woman's name back in a woman, and the woman had screamed until nightfall, and it had gone so completely that Sable could no longer tell what it had been. She supposed, from what was left, that it must have been her own name — the true one, the one under *Sable* — because the other two were still there and the name was not. She did not regret it, quite. But the Office took a copy of every scribe's name on the day the scribe was sworn, as surety, and the copy of hers was on the third floor of the Hall of Carriage in the third tray, and the trench off Cape Dissonance had been sending that building forty bottles a month through a counting-house at the top of the Salt Stair.

The Catalogue did not buy things it did not need. Sable had designed the harvest schedule. She knew that better than anyone alive.

So at eleven in the morning on Nullday, with the weather piling up black over the Cay and the boats gone and the whole harbour holding its breath, she put the daybook under her coat beside the Ledger, and went to the door.

Mags Tennant was sitting on the step outside it with her strapped arm across her knees, watching the Yard.

Mags looked up at her. Mags looked at the coat, and at the Salt Stair going up the cliff behind the Yard in its six wet flights, and back at Sable's face.

*Where?* she signed.

Sable had learned the hand-speech on the *Kestrel* in three weeks, badly, the way she had learned everything: by writing it down. She signed back, slowly, a word at a time.

*Top. The House. Books.*

Mags looked at her for a long moment. Then she signed, *Her?* and made a shape with two fingers that Sable had not learned yet but understood anyway: two short strokes, crossed.

*I know,* signed Sable.

*Alone?*

Sable considered that. It was a reasonable question. She had stood in the trees outside a harvest-house twice and gone home both times. She had been told three weeks ago, by a woman with a slate, that recording is not the same as witnessing and witnessing is not the same as doing anything, and she had written that down too.

*Yes,* she signed. *Alone.*

Mags looked at her for another moment, and then shrugged one shoulder — the good one — and went back to watching the Yard. Sable would learn later that she had followed her up as far as the fourth landing, and sat on the step there, by the broken rail, for two hours, with a belaying pin across her knees.

---

Entry. The Salt Stair. Ascending.

Eight dealers in vox-bottles had kept shop on the Salt Stair in the spring, and seven of them were dead. Sable knew where each of them had been and how each of them had died, because Oona Salt's people had been keeping a list since the second one and Oona had let her read it, and because she had climbed this staircase once already, on the fourth, and had catalogued it, as she catalogued everything, without meaning to.

She climbed it again now and counted them off.

*First landing. Abel Strake. Rope and tar, and bottles under the counter. Found in his back room on the second of Leafturn. Two cuts, crossed, across the belly. No blood on the floor. No sound heard by his wife, who was in the room above.*

*Second landing.* The shop with the big slow print. *Jonah Pike. The one who sold the Captain his. Found at his counter on the ninth of Leafturn with his chalk still in his hand. Two cuts. No blood. His dog, which was at his feet, did not bark.*

She passed the broken rail on the fourth flight. Someone had lashed a spar across the gap where the stanchions had been, and hung a lantern from it, and under the lantern someone else had laid a single sprig of something green on the step, and Sable stopped for a moment and looked at the sprig, and at the drop beyond the spar, a hundred and twenty feet to the Lantern Yard roofs, and did not write anything. Then she went on.

*Fourth. Fifth.* Two more dealers, two more crosses. Whoever was coming up this staircase was not choosing by anything except the stock on the shelves.

*Sixth landing.* The two who had run on the fourth, a yard apart, tidy. And the last dealer's shop, with its shutters open and its lamp out now, and its shelves bare, and its owner not found at all. The Harbour had searched the Stair for him from top to bottom. He had not come down it.

The sixth landing was the top of the Salt Stair. Beyond it there was only the cliff-top, a yard of wet flags with the wind going over it, and across the yard stood the counting-house.

---

It was the oldest building in Port Cantabile: a square stone house with a slate roof and iron shutters, built by the Synod's tax-farmers a hundred years before the Fracture to count what the harbour owed, and given to the Church in the spring, when the Flambeaux came west. Over its door there was a sun-disc of hammered brass, and on the disc the Church's flame. Below the flame somebody had chalked, quite small, two short crossed strokes.

The door stood open.

Sable stood at the head of the stair in the wind and looked at it for some time. Then she went in, because she had told Mags Tennant *alone* in the hand-speech, and she had not yet worked out how to un-say a thing in it.

The first thing she noticed was the quiet.

Not the quiet of an empty house. She knew that one: she had lived for three years in a city of Hollows, where every house was empty in that way. This was different. The wind had been loud on the stair. The weather was coming over the Cay with a roar in it like surf on a reef. She stepped over the threshold into the counting-house of the Flambeaux of Port Cantabile and the wind *stopped*, as if she had shut a door behind her, which she had not, and her own boots on the flagstones made no sound at all.

Not none. Less. A little. As if the house had been padded with felt.

It was not a quiet. She knew what a quiet did; she had read the Harbour's report on the *Bitter Nancy*. A quiet took all the sound and then it took the warm. This was only the *edge* of something — the place where a quiet's border would have been — a thinning, as if sound in this house had been told to keep its voice down and had obeyed.

Sable walked into it with her hands clasped behind her back, like a woman in a museum, and began, very carefully, to make entries.

---

*Entry. The counting-room. Ground floor, east. A long table, ledgers, a strongbox, the box open and the coin in it untouched.*

*Brother-Novice, Flambeau Order, name unknown. Perhaps twenty. Seated at the table's head with a pen in his hand. Two cuts, crossed, across the belly, through the cassock and the shirt and the skin beneath. Shallow: half an inch. The edges of the cuts are white, and dry, and there is frost on them.*

She stopped there and looked at that for some time, because it was the thing the Harbour's report had not said and could not have said, because the Harbour's people had found their dead in the open air hours later. Here the house was warm, there was a fire still smouldering in the grate, and on the lips of a half-inch cut across a dead boy's stomach there was a fine white rime of frost like the frost on a window-pane, and it was not melting.

The cut had not bled because there had been nothing to bleed into. Something had opened that boy for the length of a hand and a half, and where it had passed, for an instant, there had been *nothing* — not air, not cold, not darkness: an absence, a hole in the world the shape of a blade — and the blood had gone into it, as water goes into a hole in a hull. All of it. He was grey. Grey as ash, as slate, as a thing in a crypt. His face was very calm and his mouth was open, and he had not made a sound.

Sable did not record what her hands were doing while she looked at him. They were shaking. She clasped them tighter behind her back.

*Brother of the Order, name unknown, forty to fifty. On the floor by the window. Two cuts, crossed. Frost. His censer is in his hand and it is lit. The coals are still red. He had time to light it. He did not have time to swing it.*

*Brother of the Order, name unknown. In the passage. Two cuts. Frost. He was running toward the door.*

*Brother of the Order, name unknown. On the stair to the upper floor. Two cuts.*

*The Prior.*

The Prior of the Flambeaux of Port Cantabile was in his study on the first floor, in a high-backed chair at a writing-desk, facing the window. He had been writing a letter. It lay on the desk under his right hand, finished and sanded and not yet folded, and Sable read it without touching it, by bending over his shoulder, the way she had read the labels on the bottles.

*To the Reverend Inquisitor Ignatius Crane, at the Chapter-house of Sol-Luminary, with all haste. The ship* Drift-Kestrel*, formerly OK-114, lies at Port Cantabile on the Harbour's bench. Aboard her, by the report of our informants in the Lantern Yard, a Veil scribe, an automaton of the Catalogue pattern, a stolen press of the Office, a Cantor of the Reach, and a man with an arm of glass the colour of the Dawning Spire. I await your instruction and—*

There was no more. There was a pen lying across the bottom of the page, and a single drop of ink where it had fallen from his fingers, and the Prior sitting in his chair with his chin on his chest and two cuts across his belly crossed in the middle, and frost.

Sable stood and looked at the letter for a long time.

It had not been sent. The Prior had not finished it. Whoever had come into this room had come in while he was writing *and*, and had let him get no further, and had then — this was the part that made her stand there — left it on the desk, finished and sanded, for anybody to fold and seal and send east by the next Church packet out of Port Cantabile.

Sable Veil-sworn, last of her order, keeper of seventy-four thousand names, had spent nineteen years writing things down so that other people could do something about them.

She picked the letter up off the desk. She folded it in four. She held it in the flame of the Prior's own lamp until it caught, and turned it in her fingers while it burned, and dropped the last corner into the cold grate, and watched it go black.

It was the first document she had ever destroyed in her life.

She did not make an entry for it.

---

She found the boy in the kitchen.

He was under the table, in the corner, behind a sack of meal. He was perhaps eleven. He wore the grey smock of a Church foundling and he had his knees drawn up and his arms round them and his face pressed into his arms, and he did not look up when she came in, and he did not make a sound.

He had not been cut.

Sable knelt down on the kitchen flags, which hurt her knees, and stayed there, at the edge of the table, not reaching under it. She did not know how to talk to children. She had catalogued four thousand of them at Whisper-Grove. She knew their ages and their voices and their last words, and she had never once in nineteen years had to say anything to one.

"My name is Sable," she said. "I am an archivist. I write things down." She thought about it. "That is not a very useful thing to be, at present. I am sorry."

The boy did not look up.

"I will not touch you," said Sable. "I am going to sit here. When you want to come out, you may. If you do not want to, you may stay there, and I will sit here anyway, because I have nowhere particular to be." She considered. "That is a lie. I have somewhere to be. But it can wait."

She sat there for some time. The quiet thinned and thickened around her like a breath. Outside the iron shutters, very faintly, she could hear the weather arriving over the harbour.

At last the boy said, into his knees, so softly that in the felted air of that house she almost did not hear it:

"She came in the window."

"Yes," said Sable.

"Nobody heard. Brother Cassian was singing the Office and she came in the window and he stopped singing and nobody heard him stop."

"Yes."

"She looked at me." He lifted his face then. It was a very ordinary face, freckled, white with old crying. "She looked right at me. Her eyes were purple. Like the sky goes. And she put her finger on her mouth, like that, and then she went upstairs." He swallowed. "Why didn't she do me?"

Sable looked at him for a while.

"I do not know," she said. "But I would like to ask her. Will you wait here for me? There is a woman on the fourth landing with one good arm and a stick, who does not hear anything and is not afraid of anything. If I am not back by the time the rain starts, go down and find her. Her name is Mags. She will not be able to hear you, so take her hand. She will understand that."

---

The records room was at the top of the house, under the slates, at the end of a short passage. Its door was open, and there was a lamp lit inside, and somebody was reading.

Sable stood in the doorway.

The room was a cataloguer's room. She would have known it blind: shelves floor to ceiling, ledgers in calf with the years stamped on their spines, a sloped clerk's desk under the dormer, a smell of dust and ink and lamp-oil. At the desk, on the clerk's high stool, in a long dark sea-coat with the collar turned up, sat a woman with a ledger open in front of her, turning its pages with one finger.

On the floor at her feet, gagged with his own neckcloth and tied at wrist and ankle with tarred marline in a seaman's knots, sat a fat middle-aged man in a good waistcoat, weeping silently.

The woman did not look up.

"You're the scribe," she said.

Her voice was low and dry and very quiet. It did not carry; it did not need to. In that felted room it arrived in Sable's ear as though it had been spoken from a hand's breadth away, and Sable understood, with the part of her that made entries, that this was why: the quiet of the house was coming from *her*. It went where she went. A little of the trench had come up with her, and she wore it the way other people wear a coat, and it took the edge off the world wherever she sat down.

"Yes," said Sable.

"Oona Salt said there was a Veil scribe on his ship. In grey, with a book." The woman turned a page. "She said you'd signed the catalogue at Whisper-Grove. All seventy-four thousand of them."

"Seventy-four thousand, six hundred and eleven," said Sable. "Yes."

The woman looked up then.

Sable had been told about the eyes, by the boy, and by Oona Salt's list, and by the rumour that had followed Vane up and down the Rust Coast for six months, and none of it had been adequate. They were not purple. They were the colour the sky goes on the far side of evening, in the last minute before full dark, when the blue has gone out of it and there is only depth: violet shot through with very small, very cold points of light, as if the woman were looking out through two holes into a night sky somewhere a long way below the sea.

She was perhaps thirty. Pale, sea-weathered, with dark hair cropped short and a long thin scar running up from the corner of her mouth into her hairline. Her hands on the ledger were ordinary hands. Then she moved one, to turn a page, and Sable saw that the nails were not nails but something darker and longer, curved and fine and faintly translucent, like the claws of a cat carved out of smoked glass.

There were two hooks at her belt, in a crossed sheath. Sickle-daggers. The blades were not metal. They were not anything. Looking at them was like looking at the place on a wall where a picture used to hang: the eye slid off, and found the outline of something that was not there, and could not settle on what it was.

"Vesper," said Sable.

"Archivist," said Vesper. She looked down at the fat man at her feet. "This is Ormond Dace. He kept the shop on the sixth landing, and before that he kept a boat, and he took that boat out to the lip off Cape Dissonance once a month for six years with a crate of empty bottles and a stopwatch." She put her foot, lightly, on Ormond Dace's knee, and he stopped weeping and went entirely rigid. "He bottled the Coast. And then he sold it back to the Coast at four sovereigns, and he sold the rest of it to this house at six."

"Forty a month," said Sable. "To the Hall of Carriage."

Vesper's eyes came up again. Something in them changed — not much. A point of light shifting in a dark sky.

"You've read it too."

"His daybook." Sable took it out of her coat and held it up. "I took it from his shop on the fourth. Under the Twelfth it is Harbour property and I have committed a hanging offence." She paused. "I have a great deal of experience of reading things I should not have read and doing nothing about them. I am trying to give that up."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment.

Then she laughed. It was very low and very short and almost no sound at all, a breath through the nose, and it changed her whole face for a second, and Sable, who catalogued faces for a living, saw in that second exactly the woman who had been first mate of the *Wave-Cutter* at twenty-five and had laughed like that at Vane's jokes on deck in a gale for six years.

Then it was gone.

---

"Do you know what's in the bottles, archivist?"

"Voices," said Sable. "Some of them real."

"Some of them real." Vesper turned the ledger round on the desk so that Sable could see it — the counting-house's own receiving-book, in the Prior's hand: dates, cases, numbers, a carrier's mark. "Shall I tell you which?"

Sable said nothing. Vesper did not seem to need her to.

"You go down into the trench," she said, "and you don't die. That's the first thing nobody up here understands. The Hush doesn't kill what it takes. It *keeps* it. It holds you, in the dark, in the cold, and it digests you, very slowly, the way the sea digests a wreck. A little at a time. For years. You stay awake for all of it." She said it flatly, as a pilot gives a sounding. "And while it's digesting you, some of you leaks. Up. You think about your mother, or your ship, or you hum a song to keep yourself from screaming, and a little of that goes up through the dark toward the light, the way a bubble goes up — and at the lip of the trench, if a man holds a bottle upside-down over the water with the stopper out, some of it goes into the bottle."

Sable looked at the ledger. *Forty. Forty. Thirty-eight.*

"So the real ones," she said, very carefully, "are not the dead."

"The real ones are the people still down there," said Vesper. "Being eaten. That's who Ormond here has been selling to widows at four sovereigns a go for six years. Not their husbands' ghosts. Their husbands. Still awake." She took her foot off the fat man's knee. "And the ones that aren't real — sometimes that's a mimic that's learned a name off the water, and sometimes it's nothing, and sometimes it's what the *Nancy* got. A little bit of the Hush that came up the neck instead of a man. Ormond never knew which he was selling. He never opened them. He just held them over the side and counted to a hundred."

Ormond Dace made a sound behind his neckcloth. Vesper did not look at him.

"And the Catalogue buys forty a month," said Sable, "because—"

"You tell me, archivist. You designed their schedule."

Sable stood in the doorway of the records room with her hands clasped behind her back and made herself think it through, the way she had made herself think through every efficiency she had ever built into the harvest at Whisper-Grove: coldly, step by step, as a problem.

The Catalogue unnamed people. It drew a person's name and memory and voice out of them and pressed it into glass, using the Memory Anchor. It needed the Anchor to do it. It needed the person present. It needed a harvest-house and a press and staff and a schedule, and every one of those cost money and was hard to hide.

And here, at the lip of a trench off a forgotten cape, was a place where the Hush was doing it for nothing. Slowly. To people already gone. And what leaked up was voice, and memory, and name — already loosened, already half-drawn, already coming apart from the person it belonged to.

Pre-unnamed. That would be the term. She could see it on a requisition in her own hand.

"Tuning stock," said Sable aloud. "For the cores. A pressed name will not set in glass on its own. It needs something to set *around*. A seed. We used—" She stopped. "The Office used the Hollows' own last words, at first. They were not enough. There were never enough." She looked at the ledger. "This is enough. Forty a month, for six years. Every one a living person in the dark, singing to keep from screaming."

"There," said Vesper. "Now you know what I know." She closed the ledger. "And now you know why I'm going up this coast one door at a time."

---

Something moved under Vesper's coat.

Sable saw it: a small shifting, low on the left side, at the hip, as if something the size of a cat had turned over in its sleep. Vesper put her hand on it without looking, the way you put a hand on a dog's head, and the movement stopped.

And Sable felt a thing she would afterward have the greatest difficulty recording.

She felt *better*.

Not a great deal. A little. It came over her the way warmth comes into cold hands, from the fingertips in: a loosening. She had been carrying, since the Malting, a particular kind of ache that she had not let herself catalogue — the place where the third thing used to be, the thing she had given to put a woman's name back in a woman, a hole in the shape of something she could no longer remember the shape of — and it had been with her every waking minute for three weeks, like a missing tooth under the tongue.

It eased.

She stood in the doorway and felt it go dim, the way a lamp dims, and something under Vesper's coat made a small sound. A purr. Very low, and very warm, and coming, she was quite sure, from a throat that had never belonged to anything that lived in the Octave.

"Don't," said Vesper, sharply.

Sable did not know what she had been about to do. She had made some movement. Toward it.

"Don't thank it," said Vesper. "It isn't being kind to you. It's having its breakfast." She put her hand flat over the shape under her coat and pressed, gently, and the purring stopped, and the ache came back into Sable all at once, entire, as if a door had opened in winter. "It eats what hurts. It likes it best of anything. And it's a child of the thing at the bottom of that trench, so you'll understand I don't let it eat as much as it wants."

"What is it?"

"Mine," said Vesper.

---

The second blow arrived.

They heard it on the slates above them — even through the felted quiet that came off Vesper like cold off ice: a hiss and then a roar, the weather that had been piling up over the Cay since Memoryday arriving all at once over Port Cantabile.

Vesper got down off the clerk's stool. She was taller than Sable had thought. She stood for a moment looking down at Ormond Dace on the floor, and Ormond Dace looked up at her with his eyes running, and Sable understood, in the instant before it happened, that she was about to see the two cuts made.

"He bottled Merret Salt," said Sable.

Vesper's hand stopped on its way to the hooks.

"In '14. Off the Chime, not the trench. It is in his daybook on the seventh page — *M. Salt, pilot, lost off Wendmere, 1 btl., held for H.Q., not to be sold under 40.* Harbour-Queen." Sable held the daybook out, open. "He was keeping Oona Salt's own brother back, to sell to her at forty sovereigns when he judged she would pay it. It went out with the Cape stock to be kept cool, and the *Bitter Nancy* found it there. She has not opened it. She has asked the Cantor to open it for her before the *Kestrel* leaves this harbour." She swallowed. "If he is to be killed, he will be killed. I cannot prevent it and I have watched a great deal worse. But there is a woman at the foot of this stair who has a better claim to him than you do, and under the Articles he is Harbour property, and I have already committed one hanging offence today on the Harbour's account."

Vesper looked at her for a long time with her night-sky eyes. Then she looked down at the daybook in Sable's hand, and the seventh page, and the line.

"Article the Twelfth," she said.

"Yes."

"You recited it to eight men on the fourth flight with your hands behind your back. Oona told me. She said you sounded like a woman reading a will." Vesper reached down, took Ormond Dace by the collar of his good waistcoat, and hauled him to his feet with one hand as if he weighed nothing at all. "Here. Harbour property. Under the Twelfth." She put the end of the marline that bound his wrists into Sable's hand. "Mind him on the stair. He's frightened of heights. He told me so last night, at length."

Sable took the line. It was the first time anybody had ever handed her a prisoner. She did not know what to do with it, so she held it, the way she held a pen.

"And the boy in the kitchen?" she said.

Vesper paused at the door.

"He's eleven," she said. "He's a foundling. The Church found him under a bell, I expect, same as the Coast found me." She did not turn round. "Take him to Hesper. Hesper's good with the ones who don't talk."

---

She stopped at the head of the stair. The rain was coming down so hard that the Salt Stair below her was a waterfall, every step running white, the gutter down the middle roaring.

"Your captain," said Vesper, without turning. "Tell him the Mole-head. Tonight. The dead hour."

"He will ask why."

"He won't. He's been waiting five years for somebody to ask him to the Mole-head at the dead hour." The violet eyes came round, briefly, over the turned-up collar. "Tell him to bring both pistols. *Last Rites* and *Second Opinion*. He'll pretend he's forgotten which is which. He hasn't."

Sable nodded.

"And archivist." Vesper paused. "Keep the glass one away from it. The one with the arm. Don't let him come."

"Why?"

"Because I was down there five years, and I know what the bores sound like from the outside," said Vesper. "Every one of them on this continent is a pipe with something listening at the far end. And that arm is one too. It's very small. But it's the same note." She looked down the roaring stair toward the harbour, where, through the rain, the patched envelope of the *Kestrel* swung on its tower in the wind. "There's an old man at the other end of it, archivist. I could hear him breathing from the sixth landing. And what I've got to say to Vane, I'd rather he didn't hear."

Then she went down the Salt Stair into the rain, quite fast, and Sable, standing at the top with a fat man on a string and a dead Prior's ash in a grate behind her, noticed that although the stair was a river and the wind was a roar and the whole cliff was booming with the storm, she could not hear Vesper's boots on it at all.

## CHAPTER SEVEN
### The Mole-Head

> *Article the First. No captain shall leave a hand in the water while the ship can bear the weight.*
>
> *Amended, in the hand of the Harbour-Queen, the eleventh of Frostmonth, 414: — or until she cannot.*
>
> — Articles of the Free Captains of Port Cantabile

*The Long Mole. Nullday the eighth of Ashfall, the dead hour.*

---

He loaded them at the chandler's counter at two in the morning, by one candle, with the storm going over the roof like cavalry.

*Last Rites* first. She was the long one, the Fortis-made one with the brass furniture and the barrel engraved with a bell, which he had won off a Null-Watch quartermaster at cards in his first year as a captain and which had never once, in all the years since, missed anything he had pointed her at. He drew her charge and looked at it and put it back. Powder. Wadding. A pellet of Aether-Glass from the tin in his coat, a sovereign's worth of Spire-blood the size of a pea, which would go through a hound like a hot wire through wax and through a woman like any other ball. Then a patch, and the rammer, and the pan primed and the frizzen shut.

*Second Opinion* second. Shorter, uglier, a Cantabile coach-pistol with the barrel cut down and a butt-cap of plain iron, which had been his father's and before that God knew whose. Lead in that one. Always lead. A man wanted one of each, he had told Mags once, because when you met a thing in the dark you could not always tell which sort of thing it was, and you did not want to waste the good stuff on a man or insult a monster with a ball.

His left hand would not close round either of them.

The tarred line had gone into the palm to the meat on Memoryday when the *Patience* went over and he had sat there holding it, and Bettany had cleaned it and stitched it in two places and bound it in linen and told him that if he so much as made a fist with it before Hearthday she would take it off at the wrist herself. He looked at the hand now in the candlelight. It was a lump of grey linen with fingers coming out of it, two of them swollen and dark. He tried, once, to close it on *Second Opinion*'s butt, and the room went white at the edges.

So he tore a strip off the bottom of his shirt and bound the pistol into the hand. Round the butt, round the wrist, twice, a half-hitch, and his teeth on the end to pull it tight. It would fire. He could hold it level and he could pull the trigger with what was left of his first finger, and he would not be able to drop it if he tried.

That would have to do.

"You'll want the axe," said Kaelen, from the doorway.

Vane did not look up. "I'll want a great many things."

"She's got two hooks and claws. You've got one hand and two shots." Kaelen came in. He had his coat on, and his collar open — he did not bother with the collar any more, since the Chime; there was no point, the glass came up out of his shirt to within an inch and a half of his throat and everybody on the Coast had seen it — and he stood across the counter from Vane in the candlelight with that new cold thread of green running through the cyan at his collarbone. "When the shots are gone you'll have nothing."

"When the shots are gone," said Vane, "I'll think of something. I always do. It's never any good, but I think of it."

He slung the boarding axe through the loop at the back of his belt anyway. Then he looked up.

"You're not coming, Engineer."

"I know." Kaelen's face did not change. "The scribe told me what she said. About the arm." A pause. "She's right. He's at the other end of it. He's heard every word I've said since the Foundry."

"Then don't say any."

"I wasn't going to." Kaelen looked down at the counter, at the four blue bottles still in their row where nobody had had the heart to move them, and the gap at the end where Vane had taken his back that afternoon. "I've stopped saying most things. You'll have noticed."

Vane had noticed. Everybody had noticed. He did not say so.

"Vane," said Kaelen. "On Memoryday. The line."

"Don't."

"You had the knife on it."

"I said *don't*, Engineer." He stood up from the counter, too fast, and the candle jumped. "I had the knife on it and I didn't use it, and you came up, and that's the whole of it, and if you thank me I'll put *Second Opinion* through your good foot. I've only got one shot in her and I'll waste it on you. I swear to God."

Kaelen looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded, once, and stood aside from the door.

"Get aboard," said Vane, going past him. "And stay aboard. She said keep you away. She didn't say anything about sixty feet up."

Lyra was on the stair to the loft with the slate in her lap. She had been there, Vane suspected, the whole time. She did not get up. She held the slate up to him as he went past, and it had two words on it, written large, and had evidently been written some time ago, because the chalk was smudged where her thumb had rested on it while she waited.

*COME BACK.*

"Cantor," said Vane, "I've never once in my life done anything else."

---

The Long Mole of Port Cantabile was four hundred and twelve yards of dressed stone, twenty feet wide, running out from the foot of the Lantern Yard into the sea like a pointing finger. On the left as you walked out, the harbour: a sheer drop of twelve feet at that tide to black water slopping against the stones, with iron ladder-rungs let into the face every forty yards. On the right, the sea, behind a parapet wall of granite blocks the height of a man's chest. Bollards every twenty paces down the middle, iron, mushroom-topped, waist-high. And at the end, the Mole-head: a round stone platform thirty yards across, with the mast-tower standing up out of the middle of it, and on top of the mast-tower, sixty feet up, moored by her nose and swinging in the wind like a hanged thing, the *Drift-Kestrel*.

The Mole-bell hung from an iron bracket on the side of the tower at head height. They rang it once for the dead, when they put them in the water.

The storm was coming from the south-west, off the open sea, straight across the Mole from the right. Every seventh wave or so it broke against the parapet and came over the top in a single solid sheet of white that crossed the whole twenty feet of the Mole at knee height and poured off the harbour side in a waterfall. Between the big ones, spray came over in bursts like flung gravel. The stones were black and running. The bollards stood up out of the wash like the heads of drowned men.

He walked out along it in the dark at the dead hour with his hat jammed down and his collar up and a pistol tied to his ruined hand.

He did not look at the sea. He looked at the bollards. He counted them. That was what he did; that was the only thing he had ever been good at. Bollard. Twenty paces. Bollard. A sheet of white coming over the parapet on his right and he stood braced and let it go over his boots to the shins and pour off the edge to his left, and counted it too, seven waves, seven, the seventh one, and walked on.

Mags was behind him. He knew that without turning. Forty yards back, keeping to the parapet, where a wave would hit her first and she would see it coming. She had a belaying pin. She could not hear the storm and she could not hear him and she would not hear anything that was said at the Mole-head. That was why he had not told her to stay. She would follow him whatever he said, and of all the people in Port Cantabile she was the only one who could watch what happened without hearing a word of it, and he found that he wanted somebody to watch.

Fourteen bollards. Fifteen.

At the sixteenth, the storm went quiet.

---

Not the wind. The wind still pushed at him from the right, wet and hard, and the spray still came over the parapet in its handfuls of gravel. But the *sound* of it — the roar and boom of the sea on the granite, the scream in the mast-tower's stays, the *Kestrel*'s envelope drumming sixty feet above — went down, all of it, all at once, as if somebody had laid a hand over his ears. He could still hear it. It was a long way away. It was on the other side of a wall.

He could hear his own breath. He could hear the water running off his hat-brim.

And he could hear the Mole-bell, at the base of the tower, thirty yards ahead of him, swinging very slightly on its bracket in the wind, and not ringing at all.

She was sitting on the bollard at the foot of the mast-tower, under the bell, with her back to the stone and her boots crossed at the ankle and her coat-collar up, and the lantern on the tower above threw her shadow out across the wet stone toward him, long and thin.

She looked at him come up.

He stopped six paces off. He did not know why six. It seemed a reasonable number. In the quiet around her he could hear the rain on the shoulders of her coat.

"You've got old," said Vesper.

"I've *got* old? Have you seen a glass lately?" His voice came out of him too loud, the way it always did when he was frightened, and the quiet took it and folded it up small. "What happened to your face?"

"The trench happened to my face."

"Well, it suits you. Always said you needed a bit of character. You had a face like a church candle."

"You never said that."

"I thought it very loudly."

She looked up, once, past him, at the *Kestrel* swinging at the top of the tower in the storm, and back.

"He's aboard," said Vane. "Sixty feet. Out of earshot."

"Nothing's out of earshot of that," said Vesper. "But it'll do."

She did not smile. She looked at him with the night-sky eyes in the lantern-light, and he looked back, and found that he had absolutely nothing else to say, not one joke, not one line; that he had spent five years composing what he would say to her if he ever saw her, in the wheelhouse at night, drunk and sober, kind and cruel, a thousand versions of it, and that every one of them had gone out of his head like water out of a split cask.

He said, "I had ten heartbeats."

"I know."

"I counted them. I *counted*. I had the axe on the hawser and I looked at you through the spray and I counted to ten, and then—"

"I know, Vane," she said. "I was there."

---

She got up off the bollard.

She did it without hurry, the way she had once got up off a hatch-cover to take a watch, and walked toward him across the Mole-head through the rain, and he heard none of it. That was the thing. He could hear the rain on her coat and the water running off his own hat, and he could not hear her boots on the stone. Not because they were soft. Because they were *not there*. She walked across the space between them in a moving bubble of nothing, and the stone under her feet forgot to make a sound.

She stopped an arm's length from him. He did not step back. It took everything he had.

"You made the right call, Captain," said Vesper.

The quiet held it. It went into his ears from a hand's breadth away, soft and dry and completely clear.

"The winch was going," she said. "Another three seconds and the hawser would have taken the *Cutter* down by the stern, and all forty-eight of them with her. I saw it from the water. I watched the winch-pawls go. I counted them, the same as you." Her face did not move. "You cut it and forty-eight boys lived. Teo and Benny and both the Harland brothers and that boy from the Cay with the squint who couldn't tie a bowline. I counted them too, from down there, while I was going. Forty-eight. It was the right call. You'd make it again. So would I."

He stood on the Mole-head in the rain.

"No," he said.

"Yes."

"*No.* Don't you — don't you *dare* stand there and—" It came up out of him from somewhere so far down that he did not recognise the voice it came in. "Tell me I'm a coward. Tell me you hate me. Five years, Vesper. Five years in the *dark*, and you come up out of it and walk up this whole coast cutting throats, and you get to the top and you look at me and you say — you say—"

"It was the right call."

"*Tell me you hate me!*"

The quiet swallowed it. A man screaming on the end of a mole in a storm, and it came out of him into the air and went three feet and died, and nobody in Port Cantabile heard it but her.

"No," said Vesper. "I held my breath for five years waiting for you to come back for me, Vane. And somewhere about the third year I worked out you'd been right not to. And that was the worst thing that happened to me down there." She looked at him. "So no. I won't give you that. You don't get to be hated. You get to have been right."

He drew *Last Rites* and shot her.

---

He did not decide to. He would say that afterward, to Mags, the one time he ever said anything about it, and Mags would look at him and sign, very slowly, *liar*, and he would not argue.

*Last Rites* came up out of his belt in his right hand the way she always had, the long brass-furnished barrel finding the line between his eye and the left side of Vesper's chest without any help from him at all, and he pulled through, and felt the shot.

Felt it. Did not hear it. The pan flashed and the pistol kicked his wrist back and a yard-long tongue of flame went out across the Mole-head and lit the rain gold, and there was no sound at all. A sovereign's worth of glass shot went out of the barrel at a distance of four feet.

And went through the space where she had been.

She had not been there for half a second. He had not seen her move, and he had not *heard* her move, and that, he understood in the half-second after, as the flame died and the dark came back, was going to be the whole of it. He had fought for twenty years by ear. A man's boots on a deck told you where his weight was. A blade being drawn told you which hand. The scrape, the breath, the grunt — every fight he had ever lived through, he had lived through by listening, the same way he had sailed through fog by the sound of surf.

Inside her quiet, there was nothing to listen to.

She was on his left. His bad side. He knew it because the lantern-light changed on the wet stone, a shadow sliding where no shadow had been, and he turned toward it with *Second Opinion* tied into his ruined hand and the hand screaming, and something passed across the front of his coat from right to left, very fast, with no sound.

Cold.

He looked down. His coat hung open. The lapel was gone, both lapels, the whole front of the good blue wool had been cut through in a single long line from his right hip to his left shoulder and lay open like a book, and the lining was cut, and his shirt under it, and along the line, on the cloth, there was frost.

He had not felt the blade touch him. There had not been a blade. There had been, for an instant, a line of *nothing* across the front of him, the shape of a hook, and everything it had passed through had simply been — removed.

"That's your coat," said Vesper, somewhere on his left. "The next one's you."

---

He started to count.

It was all he had. Not by ear. By light. The tower-lantern above them threw its light down across the Mole-head in a long yellow fan toward the Mole, and everything that moved in that fan threw a shadow, and in a quiet a shadow was the only thing that still told the truth. He counted her shadow. He counted the waves. Seven. He counted the bollards behind him, the distance to the parapet on his right, the distance to the harbour edge on his left: eleven feet. Twelve-foot drop. Black water.

He did not look at the black water.

Her shadow moved on the stones. He turned with it. She came in low from his left and he put *Second Opinion* — the ugly lead one, the father's one, tied to his wrecked hand — between them like a club, and the hook took the barrel. He felt it take it. The weapon went light in his hand, and he looked down and the last four inches of the barrel were gone, sliced off clean as a carrot, with frost on the cut face of the steel.

Then the claws.

She raked him with her left hand across the face, from the corner of his mouth up across his cheekbone to his hairline, the same line as the scar on her own face, as if she had drawn it on him from memory. It was the first thing she had done that he felt as pain — real pain, hot, ordinary, blood — and he knew it was meant to be. He went back a step with his face opened and blood running into his collar and the rain washing it down his neck, and he laughed.

The laugh. The loud one. The one the whole Rust Coast knew, the one that came up out of him in every gale and every boarding action and every fight he had ever been certain he was about to lose. It came out of him on the Mole-head with his face cut to the bone, and the quiet took it and swallowed it whole, and he heard it die three feet from his mouth like a candle in a jar.

He had never heard himself laugh in a quiet before. He found that it was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to him.

"Still doing that," said Vesper.

"It's a gift."

"It's a *tell*." She was on his right now. The shadow had moved; he had not seen it go. "It always was. You laugh when you're about to do something stupid."

He did something stupid.

---

The seventh wave was coming. He had been counting it. He could not hear it — inside her quiet the sea was a dumbshow, a great white wall heaving up silently on the other side of the parapet — but he could *see* it, the black water rearing against the lantern-light, and he knew where it would land: across the Mole-head, right to left, knee-high, straight over the place where she was standing, toward the harbour edge.

He went *into* it.

He threw himself forward and right at her, head down, straight at the parapet and the wave coming over it, and as he went he swung his ruined left hand behind him with what was left of *Second Opinion* tied into it, and pointed the stub of the barrel back down at the stone behind his own heels, and pulled the trigger.

The Drift.

It was the oldest trick on the Rust Coast and the most stupid, and every corsair in the Archipelago knew it and almost none of them would use it, because the Tempest Archipelago lived on Velocity, and Velocity was bleeding, and here a thrown stone did not always stop where it should and a gun's kick did not always stay in the gun. Fire a pistol on the deck of a ship in the Drift and the recoil could throw you three yards. Fire it behind you and it could throw you forward faster than any man could run. *Tailwind*, the old men called it. Half the men who tried it broke their arms. The other half broke their necks.

*Second Opinion* went off behind him without a sound, and the kick of it came up through his ruined hand and his wrist and his elbow and shoulder and took him off his feet and threw him across the Mole-head at Vesper like a ball out of a gun, and the wave came over the parapet in the same instant and hit them both.

They went down together in a ton of white water.

---

What happened then, he would never be able to tell in order. Mags saw most of it from forty yards back, in the lantern-light, and told it to him a week later in the hand-speech in the *Kestrel*'s hold, slowly, a piece at a time, like a woman reading him his own will; and she told it to him without any sound, which was the only way it could have been told.

He hit Vesper at the waist with his shoulder and the wave took them both across the Mole-head toward the harbour edge, rolling, tangled, and he felt one of her hooks go across the back of his thigh, cold, and felt the other go across his ribs on the left side under the arm — not deep, she was still not cutting deep, she was still choosing — and felt the frost of it, and felt nothing else, because there was nothing to feel; the cut was simply there. He got his right fist into her face. He felt her nose go under his knuckles. She did not make a sound. She broke two of the fingers of his left hand — the bad hand, the tied hand, the stitched hand — by taking hold of the stub of *Second Opinion* and turning it against the binding until something gave, and the white came up at the edges of everything and he heard himself scream, inside his own head, because outside it there was nowhere for the scream to go.

They stopped against a bollard, half in the wash. He was on top.

He had the boarding axe. He did not remember drawing it. He had it in his right hand, the haft across her throat and his weight on it, the way he had held a hundred men down on a hundred decks, and she lay under him in the running water with her cropped hair floating and her nose broken and her violet eyes open, and she did not fight him.

She did not fight him at all.

She lay there with the axe-haft across her throat and her hands open on the stone on either side of her head, the hooks lying loose in her fingers, and looked up at him through the rain.

"Well, go on," said Vesper.

He did not move.

"You had ten heartbeats last time." Her voice was very quiet, under the haft, and perfectly clear. "Count them. You're good at it."

He knelt on the end of the Long Mole in the rain with his face opened and his ribs opened and his hand broken and his whole weight on an axe-haft across the throat of the only person he had ever loved, and he counted.

One.

Two.

Three.

At four he took the axe off her throat and threw it, as hard as he could, out over the harbour edge, and watched it turn end over end in the lantern-light and go into the black water without a splash he could hear.

---

She lay under him for a moment longer. Then she got up.

He let her. He sat back on his heels in the wash and let her get up, and she stood, with water running off her coat and her nose bleeding — it did bleed, he saw, a little, red, ordinary; whatever she was now, the trench had not taken all of her — and looked down at him for a long time.

Then she walked to the harbour edge.

He did not understand what she was doing until she got there. She walked to the edge of the Mole-head where the stone stopped and the twelve-foot drop began, with the black water heaving and slopping against the face below, and she turned round so that her back was to it, and looked at him.

And stepped backward off it.

The way Pell had stepped off the *Patience*. The way you step off a quay into a boat that you know is there.

---

He did not count.

That was all. That was the whole of it, when Mags told it to him, a week later, in the hold, in the hand-speech, slowly. *You didn't count,* she signed. *I was watching your mouth. You always count. Your lips move. You didn't.*

He was up and moving before she had gone below the edge. He did not take off his coat. He did not take off his boots. He did not look for a ladder, or a line, or a lifebuoy on the bracket by the bell, though there was one there and he had walked past it. He did not think about the harbour, or the black water, or the five years he had spent not looking at the sea, or the hawser, or the axe, or the ten heartbeats.

He went off the end of the Long Mole after her into the harbour of Port Cantabile at the dead hour of the night in a storm, with a broken hand, without a sound.

---

The water was so cold it stopped his heart.

He felt it stop. He felt it clench in his chest like a fist and hold, and the black closed over his head, and the salt went up his nose and into his opened face like fire, and he went down, and kept going down, because his coat was full of water and his boots were full of water and there was a pistol tied to his hand, and his heart was not beating, and he could not find up.

The Drift had him. He felt it. The harbour water moving under the Mole in long slow surges that did not stop where water should stop, that kept on going, pulling, a hand on his coat drawing him down and out, away from the stone, toward the harbour mouth and the open sea and the trench beyond it, forty miles south, where the Hush lay in the dark and held things.

He thought: *this is it. This is what it was like.*

He thought: *good.*

And then something took hold of the back of his collar, and pulled.

---

She held him up.

He came out of the water into the air with his heart starting again in great sick lurching beats and his mouth full of the harbour, and she had him. She had one arm across his chest from behind and her other hand on an iron ladder-rung in the face of the Mole, and she was holding his head out of the water against her shoulder the way you hold a drowning man, with the sea surging up and down the stone around them, lifting them both and dropping them, and the storm roaring overhead on the other side of her quiet, very far away.

He coughed up half the harbour. She held him.

He tried to get hold of the rung and his left hand would not close on anything, it was a ruin of broken fingers and wet linen with a cut-off pistol still tied into it, and he could not get his right arm round, and his boots were dragging him down, and he understood, with what was left of his head, that he could not get himself out of this water and that she could, easily, and that she was not going to let go.

And she was humming.

Very low. Right by his ear. Under the quiet, under the storm, so soft that he would not have heard it at all if her mouth had not been an inch from the side of his head.

He knew it. He knew it in the first three notes. Every man who had ever sailed on the *Wave-Cutter* knew it. It was the shanty they sang rounding Cape Dissonance outward bound, the slow one, the capstan one, the one with no proper words that anybody could agree on, that you sang to keep time when the cable was heavy and the wind was wrong and you did not know if you would make the turn. She had sung it at the capstan every time they rounded the Cape. She had had a voice like a bell.

She did not have a voice like a bell now. She had a low dry broken hum, cracked and flat, a voice that had been five years in a place without air.

"That's what I did," said Vesper, into his ear, between one line and the next. "Down there. Five years. Every day. I hummed that, so I wouldn't scream."

The water lifted them and dropped them.

"I held on," she said. "Like this. To nothing. In the dark. And I hummed it so I'd know I was still me." Her arm was hard across his chest. "And I thought: he'll come back. He'll come back with a line. He counted, so he'll come back."

He made a sound. It was not a word.

"You didn't count, just now," she said.

"No."

"Not even one."

"No."

He felt her breathe out, against the side of his head. It was the first breath he had felt from her all night that was not perfectly controlled.

"Well," said Vesper. "That's one."

---

Mags got them out. Mags, who had come the forty yards down the Mole at a dead run in the wash and seen the empty Mole-head and the black water and the two heads in it at the foot of the ladder, and had not been able to shout for anyone, and had not tried. She had taken the lifebuoy off its bracket by the bell, and its line, and gone down the ladder to the bottom rung one-handed with the line in her teeth, and got the buoy over Vane's head, and climbed back up and made the line fast round the nearest bollard with a round turn and two half-hitches, and then hauled. One arm. Like the Aegis, without stopping.

Vesper came up the ladder last, on her own, without any help, and sat down on the Mole-head stones with her back against the base of the mast-tower under the silent bell, beside him.

He lay on his back in the wash and looked up at the *Kestrel* swinging sixty feet above in the storm, and breathed.

He hurt. He hurt more than he could remember hurting in his life. His face was open from mouth to hairline and his ribs were open on the left and the back of his thigh was open, and every one of those cuts was cold — not bleeding, or barely, the lips of them white with frost — and his left hand was a bag of broken sticks that the sea had got into, and his chest felt as if someone had stood on it. He was shaking so hard that his heels drummed on the stone.

And under all of it, worse than all of it, there was the other thing. The old thing. The ten heartbeats. It was still there. It had not gone anywhere. He had gone off the end of the Mole without counting and she had held him up in the water humming the capstan shanty, and it was still there, under his breastbone, exactly where it had been for five years, and he understood that it was always going to be.

Something moved under Vesper's coat.

---

He saw it come out. Mags saw it too; she was standing over them both with the wet line still in her fist, and she saw it, and he watched her face, and her face did a thing he had not seen it do since she was nineteen years old and came aboard the *Wave-Cutter* with a bag and a slate.

It came out from under the hem of Vesper's sea-coat on the left side, low down, where it had been sitting all night against her hip. It was about the size of a cat. It was not like a cat in any other way.

It was pale. The colour of the inside of a shell, of a pearl, of a drowned man's hand: a soft, pearly, faintly translucent white, with a sheen on it like oil on water. It had no legs that he could see. It moved the way the quiet in the *Bitter Nancy* had moved in Oona's telling — not crawling, *leaning* — a soft folded shape like a tongue or a slug or a closed flower, with a fringe along its underside that rippled. It had no eyes. It had no face. In the middle of it, where on anything else there would have been a belly, it was faintly, very faintly, lit from inside, and the light was the light in her eyes: a deep violet with small cold points in it, as if somewhere inside this little soft white thing there was a sky at night, a very long way down.

It was warm. He could feel it from two feet away, a bath-water warmth coming off it into the cold rain.

It leaned across the wet stone toward him, slowly, and stopped by his left hand.

"That's Mote," said Vesper. She had not moved. "Don't be frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"You're lying on your back in a puddle shaking like a wet dog."

"That's the *cold*—"

Mote touched his hand.

---

It went into him the way warmth goes into frozen fingers. From the hand in. Up the wrist, up the arm, across the shoulder, into the cut on his face and the cut along his ribs and the cut on his thigh — a soft, spreading, terrible *ease*, as though someone had poured warm oil into every wound he had at once. The cuts stopped hurting. The broken fingers stopped hurting. The cold in his chest where his heart had stopped in the harbour loosened and went, and he lay on the stones of the Mole-head and felt, for the first time since the trench opened under the *Cutter* five years ago, nothing at all.

And then he felt it go further.

He felt it go in, under the breastbone. Past the cuts and the bruises and the broken hand. Down. Looking. Feeling about in him, very gently, like a hand in a dark cupboard, for the thing that hurt worst.

It found the ten heartbeats.

He felt it find them. He felt it take hold — softly, so softly — of the thing he had carried for five years, the axe and the hawser and the spray and her face in the spray and the counting, one, two, three, four, all the way to ten, and the axe coming down — and he felt it begin, very tenderly, to *eat*.

And it was going. He could feel it going. Getting lighter. Getting smaller. The way a fever goes. The way a bell goes on ringing after it is struck, fainter and fainter, until you are not sure you can hear it any more.

"No," said Vane.

He did not know he was going to say it. It came out of him like the laugh had, from somewhere a long way down.

"No. *No.* Don't — leave it. Leave it, that's *mine*—"

He got his right hand onto the soft pale back of the thing and pushed, weakly, and it did not move; it was eating; it purred, low, under his hand, like a contented cat; and he lay there on the stone and felt the worst thing in his life being taken out of him a little at a time, gently, like a splinter, and found that he could not bear it. That he would rather have it. That it was the only thing he had left of her, of the *Cutter*, of the day the sea opened — and that if it went, he would not know who he was.

"*Please,*" said Vane. "It's mine."

"Mote," said Vesper. "Leave him that one."

It stopped.

It stopped at once, the way a good dog stops. He felt it let go. He felt the ten heartbeats come back into him all at once, entire, exactly the weight they had always been, exactly the shape, the axe and the spray and her face, and it was the worst thing he had ever felt and he lay on the stones and wept with relief.

Mote leaned back across the stone to Vesper's hip and folded itself against her and went still. It purred for a little while. It had, he thought, through the tears, a slightly disappointed air.

Vesper looked at him.

"I know," she said. "I don't let it have mine either."

---

They sat there for some time with the storm going over, on the other side of the quiet. Mags sat down on the bollard with the line across her knees and watched them both and did not sign anything.

After a while, Vane got his right hand inside what was left of his coat, to the inside pocket, over the heart. He was not sure it would still be there. It was. It had come through the harbour and the cold and the claws and the hooks, and it lay in his palm, blue glass, palm-high, the wax at the neck unbroken, the paper label soaked and spoiled and still, just, legible in the big slow print of a man called Jonah Pike on the second landing.

**VESPER. FIRST MATE,** ***WAVE-CUTTER****. LOST OFF CAPE DISSONANCE, 414.*

He held it out to her.

She looked at it for a long time. She did not take it.

"Did you ever open it?"

"No."

"Good," said Vesper. "That one's real."

He looked at the bottle in his hand.

"Dace was out at the lip in the spring of '17," she said. "Pike sold it you, but Dace caught it. I know, because I heard his boat. I was humming." She looked out at the harbour, at the black water where she had held him. "Three years in. Every day. It's a long song if you sing all of it, and I sang all of it, and some of it went up. Somebody was bound to catch a bit." She looked back at the bottle. "That's me, in there. Three years down. Humming a capstan shanty in the dark so I wouldn't scream. You paid four sovereigns for it."

He did not say anything. He could not.

"Keep it," said Vesper. "Don't open it. It's not for listening to." She pushed it back toward him, very gently, with one finger, the claw not quite touching his skin. "It's the only one of those five years you've got. I'd rather you had it than the Hall of Carriage."

He put it back in his coat, over the heart.

---

Mote began to hum.

It had been still against her hip, folded up, warm, and it began, very softly, to make a sound — not a purr this time. A note. A single, sustained, perfectly pure tone, high and clear and very sweet, coming out of the soft pale shape of it the way a note comes out of a struck glass, and holding, and holding, and holding.

Vesper looked down at it quickly.

"Mote. *No*—"

But the note went on. And in the quiet that sat over the Mole-head — in that felted, folded, swallowing quiet, where a man's laugh had died three feet from his mouth and a pistol had fired with no sound — the note did not die. It went out. It went out through the quiet as if the quiet were not there, and up, and Vane heard it go up the mast-tower, sixty feet, to the *Kestrel* swinging at the top in the storm.

And the *Kestrel* answered.

Not the ship. Something in the ship. A faint, high, clear tone, glass-thin, coming down out of the dark at the top of the tower, the same note exactly, the same pitch, five hundred and twenty-eight, answering it. Holding it. Ringing along.

He looked up. They all looked up.

There was a man at the *Kestrel*'s rail, sixty feet above them, in the light of the tower-lantern. He was standing quite still with his left arm out from his side, the bare glass of it lit cyan from inside, and the arm was ringing — he could see it ringing, the glass shivering with light — in perfect unison with the little pale thing at Vesper's hip, and the man's face, even from sixty feet, even in that light, was the colour of the stone.

Vane had told him not to come. He had not come. He had stayed aboard, as asked, sixty feet up, out of earshot.

The note had come to him.

"Vesper," said Kaelen Vance, from the rail.

His voice came down the tower thin and small through the storm, and the quiet let it through, because the quiet was letting everything through now; the quiet was full of that one pure note and had no room for anything else.

"Vesper. Where did it learn that?"

Vesper did not answer for a moment. She had her hand flat on Mote's back and was pressing, and the note was not stopping, and her face in the lantern-light was the face of a woman doing a sum she did not like.

"It didn't learn it," she said at last. She said it up the tower, to him. She said it, Vane saw, knowing exactly who else was listening, at the other end of that arm, five hundred miles away. "It was *born* on it. Seven years ago, at the bottom of the trench, in the dark — two years before I got there. It told me. It tells me things, the way it tells you it's warm. Something fell down into that dark out of the sky in the middle of a morning, still singing. A woman's voice. One note. Falling all the way from the Dawning Spire to the bottom of the world, and still holding it when it got there." Her hand pressed on Mote's back. "That was the first thing this ever heard, Engineer. That's its first word."

Kaelen did not move at the rail.

The note went on — pure, high, sweet, five hundred and twenty-eight — the note of the three hundredth sunrise, of forty copper-wired throats on a riser at the Siphon Foundry, of the morning the Light let go. The note a young engineer had calibrated a bore to, with a method that still bore his name.

Vane lay on the stones of the Mole-head in the rain and listened to it, and did not know what it was, and watched Kaelen's face, and knew.

Then Mote stopped. As suddenly as it had begun. It folded itself small against Vesper's hip and went still and dark, and the quiet came back down over the Mole-head like a lid, and the storm went far away again, and the glass at the *Kestrel*'s rail went on ringing alone for a long moment after, fainter and fainter, until it too was gone.

Nobody said anything.

Five hundred miles east, in a white room under the Organum, in a chair that faced no window, an old man who had been listening to that arm for seven years lifted his head.

He had heard that note before. He had written it down, once, in a small hand, on the cover-sheet of a document marked for the Arbiterium's eyes only, eight months before the Dawning Spire fell.

He had never expected to hear it again from the bottom of the sea.

---

*End of Part One.*
