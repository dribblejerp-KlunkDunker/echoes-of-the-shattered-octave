## CHAPTER ONE
### Salvage

> *Article the Ninth. Any vessel making the harbour under no hand shall be boarded by the Harbour's people and no other, and what is found aboard her belongs to the Harbour. Article the Tenth. If what is found aboard her is still hungry, Article the Ninth is void and the Harbour disclaims all interest.*
>
> — Articles of the Free Captains of Port Cantabile

*Port Cantabile, the Tempest Archipelago. The fourth of Ashfall, 419 SR. Dawn.*

---

The tide came in singing a flat E, and Oona Salt was on the Long Mole at first light to hear what it had brought her.

She did that every morning and had for nineteen years. Port Cantabile was built on a reef of singing coral — great pale fans of the stuff under forty feet of green water, which hummed when the swell crossed it, so that the whole harbour had a note the way other towns have a smell. In a good year that note was a clean low A and you could set a fiddle by it. Since the Fracture it wandered. This morning it was flat, and sour, and under it, out past the Cay, something was ringing that should not have been ringing at all.

"That's the Chime," said Teague, at her shoulder.

"I know what it is."

"Three weeks it's been quiet. Now it's—"

"Teague," said Oona Salt. "I have ears, a harbour, and a headache, and I can only put down one of them."

She was fifty-six, built like a capstan, in a coat that had been an admiral's before she took it off an admiral. She was the Harbour-Queen of Port Cantabile, which is not a title anybody gives you and not one anybody can take back, and she had got it by being the only person on the Rust Coast who paid what she promised on the day she promised it, which on this coast is very nearly sorcery.

Out past the harbour mouth, the thing that was coming in had no one at the wheel.

---

She knew the boat before Teague got the glass up. Everyone on the Mole did. A sixty-foot coaster with a patched gaff and a green stripe along her rail: the *Bitter Nancy*, Mellis Crane's boat, out three weeks on a salvage run to the lip of the trench off Cape Dissonance, and overdue by eleven days.

She came in beautifully. That was the first wrong thing. A boat with nobody steering does not come in beautifully; she yaws, she luffs, she goes broadside and hangs. The *Bitter Nancy* came through the harbour mouth dead straight on the tide, and rounded the Mole-head with eighteen inches to spare, and lost way exactly where a good skipper would have lost it, and lay down against the stone like a dog settling.

The Mole had gone quiet. Forty people — dockhands, net-women, two of Hesper's girls on their way home — all of them watching, nobody moving.

"Right," said Oona.

"Harbour-Queen—"

"Article the Ninth. Teague, gaffs and lanterns and the long hooks, and nobody steps aboard until I do." She was already shrugging out of the admiral's coat, because you do not board in good wool. "And send up the Mole for Hesper's cousin, the deaf one. Mabb! Where's Mabb?"

"Here, missus."

"Bring the box."

The box was pine, two foot by one, and it lived in the Harbour office under Oona's desk with a lock on it she carried the key to. In it were nineteen pellets of Aether-Glass shot, bought at a sovereign a pellet from a woman in the Lantern Yard, and a short-barrelled coach gun to put them in.

They had used it four times in nineteen years.

---

The deck of the *Bitter Nancy* was clean.

That was the second wrong thing, and it stopped Oona at the rail with one boot over. Three weeks at sea, a salvage run to the trench lip, and the deck was swept and the ropes were coiled down and the gear was stowed: hooks on the rack, buckets nested, the boom crutched and lashed. She had been going aboard other people's boats for forty years and she had never once seen a working deck that tidy with the crew aboard.

"Mellis!" she called. "Mellis Crane! It's Salt!"

The harbour note hummed under everything.

No answer.

She went down the deck with the coach gun broken over her forearm and Teague behind her with a boat-hook and Mabb behind him with the lantern, and at the main hatch she stopped and put her hand flat on the deck-boards and held it there for a count of three, the way her mother had taught her.

Nothing. No engine; the *Nancy* had no engine. No voices. No rats. No water slapping in the bilge.

Nothing at all.

"Teague," she said. "Listen to me and don't argue. Go back up the Mole and get everybody off it. Everybody — the net-women, the girls, the boy with the barrow. Fifty yards and then another fifty."

"Oona—"

"There's a *quiet* aboard her," said Oona Salt.

---

Here is what the Rust Coast knows about quiets, which is more than the Synod knows and less than it needs.

They came up out of the trenches in the Screaming Year, the same year the hounds came down out of the hills. Everybody inland worries about the Cacophony: the hounds, the mimics, the things that eat voices and wear them. The Coast has those too. The Coast also has the other kind, and the other kind is worse, and the reason nobody inland writes about them is that the people who meet them do not write anything afterward.

A hound hunts sound. A quiet hunts the opposite.

Where a quiet is, sound stops working. Not muffled — *stopped*. The air inside its reach forgets how to carry, and you stand in a bubble maybe thirty feet across in which your boots make no noise on the boards, and the bell on the Mole-head does not reach you, and your own heart, which you have heard in your ears your whole life without noticing, is gone.

And you can scream — people do, every time, it is the first thing anybody does — and your throat will tear and your face will go purple, and no one twenty feet away will hear a thing.

They do not eat you quickly. That is the part the Coast does not say out loud. They take the warm first, and the warm takes a while.

Glass shot does not frighten them. Everybody tries it. Aether-Glass is Spire-blood, and the Cacophony are torn-off pieces of Spire-song and hate it the way a dog hates a whip — but a quiet is not a piece of anything. It came from outside the Octave, where there was never any song to tear, and a pellet of glass to a quiet is a pebble.

What works is iron and cold water and about four people who do not panic.

---

She counted thirty-one aboard when she started down the ladder, which was the last number she heard for a while.

Four steps down, the world went off.

It is not like going deaf; Oona had asked Hesper's deaf cousin about that once, and Mags Tennant's answer — which she had got third-hand and remembered for nine years — was *you think it's a room with the door shut; it isn't; it's a room that was never built.* Deafness is a quiet room. This was a room with the air taken out of it. She felt the ladder under her boots and heard nothing. She felt her own breath go out over her teeth and heard nothing. Her ears pushed outward for something to hold and found a kind of hiss that was not a sound but her own mind inventing one, the way an eye invents colour in the dark.

She got to the bottom and turned, and Mabb came down after her with the lantern and his mouth already open, and she grabbed his jaw and shut it with her hand and shook her head.

The hold of the *Bitter Nancy* was full of her crew.

They were alive. All eleven of them. They were sitting on the deck in a loose ring with their backs against the hull and the cargo and each other, the way men sit at the end of a watch, and their eyes were open, and they were looking at the thing in the middle of them.

They had been like that for some time. Oona could see that from the state of them: unshaven, hollow, one with a broken hand that had set wrong, all of them sitting. Mellis Crane was nearest the ladder with her boots off and her feet going black.

The thing in the middle was sitting too.

It is hard to say what a quiet looks like, because the eye does with them what the ear does: it invents. Oona's eye gave her a shape about the size of a seated man, low and wide and wet, grey-blue, folded over itself like a tongue, with a sort of fringe along its underside that moved. It had no face that she could find. At its centre, where a belly would be, it was faintly, horribly warm — you could feel that from six feet, a bath-water warmth coming off it — and the warmth was Mellis Crane's and ten other people's and had been going in for eleven days.

In front of it, tipped on their sides in a row, were eleven blue glass bottles with their wax seals broken.

*Vox-bottles*, Oona thought, with a clarity that surprised her. *They did find something out there. They found a case of bottles on the trench lip and they brought it home and somebody opened one to see whose it was.*

And a quiet came up the opened bottle the way a wasp comes out of a jar.

---

She got Mellis Crane by the collar and dragged her backward eight feet and the woman did not resist and did not help.

Teague came down the ladder with the boat-hook. Mabb came with the lantern and a gaff. Two of the Mole crew came after them — Colm, who was nineteen, and a woman called Pell whose last name Oona would be asked for in an hour and would not be able to give.

And then it was four and a half minutes of silent work in a ship's hold, which Oona Salt would be telling badly, in pieces, for the rest of her life.

You cannot shout *left*. You cannot say *behind you*, or *mine*, or *drop*, or *for God's sake get out of the way*. Everything that forty years of harbour work had built into her — every order, every warning, the entire vocabulary of her trade — was gone, and what was left was hands and eyes and the width of a hold.

She pointed. Colm did not see her point.

The quiet took him across the thighs. It did not lunge; it *leaned*, and its fringe went over his boots and up, and he went down with his mouth wide and his throat working and absolutely nothing coming out of it, and Oona felt the deck shake under her when his shoulders hit and heard none of that either, and the worst thing in the whole of it — the thing she would wake up with — was that Pell, eight feet away with a gaff in both hands, *did not know he had fallen*, because a man going down in a hold is a noise, and there were no noises.

Oona got the coach gun up and put both barrels into the mass of it from four feet.

Nineteen sovereigns of Aether-Glass went into a thing out of the Rift and did precisely what the old men on the Mole said it would do, which was nothing whatsoever. The shot went in and was gone. The body closed over the hole like water over a dropped stone.

*Iron*, she thought, *and cold water, and four people who don't panic*, and found that she had two.

So she used the boat.

She took Teague by the arm and put the point of the boat-hook in his hands against the seacock housing under the sole-boards, and he looked at her as though she were mad, and then he understood, because Teague had been on the water since he was six.

He broke out the seacock with the butt of the hook.

The sea came into the hold of the *Bitter Nancy* in a column as thick as a man's leg, and it was the January water of the Rust Coast at six in the morning, which is cold enough to stop a heart, and none of them could hear a drop of it.

The quiet came off Colm.

They got it the rest of the way with the gaffs: Oona at its head and Mabb at its flank and Pell — who had now understood, and whose face Oona did not look at — driving a long iron hook through the fringe and pinning it to the boards while the cold came up over all their boots. It had no scream to make. It was already drowning the room in silence; it could not take the water's heat the way it had taken a man's, because there was too much and it was moving, and it *shrank*. That was the whole of the horror. It did not die. It got smaller, and colder, and finally slack, and when the water was at her knees Oona Salt put her boot on it and pushed what was left out through the broken seacock into the harbour, and the hold made a sound.

She heard it. The water roaring, and her own breath, and Mabb sobbing, and above them on deck somebody's voice shouting her name over and over.

It came back all at once, and it was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

---

They lost Colm Teague — Teague's nephew, which she had known and had still sent him down the ladder, and would be carrying up the Mole in front of his uncle for the rest of her life.

They lost two of the *Bitter Nancy*'s crew in the first hour on the stones, and a third that night. Mellis Crane lived and never went aboard anything again. Of the rest, four would be all right in a season and three would not: the quiet had been in that hold eleven days and there were things in a person that go cold and do not come back warm.

And at eight in the morning Oona Salt sat on a bollard on the Long Mole with wet legs and an empty coach gun across her knees, and looked at eleven blue glass bottles laid out on a tarpaulin in a row, and the one at the end with its wax broken and its paper label still legible.

The label said, in a Cantabile scribe's careful hand:

**MERRET SALT. HARBOUR PILOT. LOST OFF THE CHIME, 414.**

Her brother.

Somebody on the trench lip had been bottling the Coast's dead for five years and selling them in the Lantern Yard at four sovereigns apiece, and the *Bitter Nancy* had gone out to find the stock, and they had.

"Who sold these," said Oona.

Nobody answered, because everybody knew, and because the dealer on the Salt Stair who sold vox-bottles had been doing it with the Harbour's licence and the Harbour's protection and three percent of the Harbour's cut, for five years, at the Harbour-Queen's own say-so.

Oona Salt sat on the Mole for a long while with her brother's last words in a bottle on a tarpaulin in front of her, and did not open it.

---

The lookout came down from the Mole-head at nine.

"Ship, missus. North-east, low, coming in slow — brass-ribbed, patched envelope, four tanks and one of them dark." He was out of breath. "She's flying no colours and her name's painted over twice, and Ferris says the old mark under it is OK-114."

Oona did not move for a moment.

Then she got up off the bollard, and picked up the admiral's coat, and put it on, and buttoned it to the throat, and looked out north-east at the little limping shape coming down the sky over the singing water with a hold full of something she already knew the shape of, because she had taken money for telling a woman with violet eyes exactly where to find it.

"Well," said Oona Salt. "He's brought it to my harbour."

## CHAPTER TWO
### Four Hundred and Eighty

> *A debt on the Rust Coast is not a number. It is a rope with two ends, and the question is only ever which end you are holding when the tide turns.*
>
> — Rust Coast saying

*Port Cantabile. The fourth of Ashfall, mid-morning.*

---

It had taken them eleven days to come four hundred miles, which is a thing you can do in nine hours in a healthy ship.

They had done it in hops: forty miles and down, patch, pump, forty miles and down. Pennick had turned the number-three tank into a religion — a clip, a gasket, two hours of coaxing, a prayer, forty more miles — and twice they had come down in country where Vane had been genuinely unsure whether the people who came out of the trees would bring water or knives. Once it had been both.

So the *Drift-Kestrel* came over the singing coral of Port Cantabile at nine in the morning on the fourth of Ashfall, nose-high, crabbing, with her envelope patched in eleven new places and twenty-one Oakhaven farmers at the rail staring at the sea for the first time in their lives.

"It's *moving*," said somebody, behind Vane. "It doesn't stop. Does it ever stop?"

"No," said Vane.

He did not look at it himself. He had the wheel and a mooring to make and a harbour full of men who would know the ship, and he kept his eyes on the Long Mole and the mast-tower at the end of it, and did not look at the water, and had not looked at the water since the Flume crossing, and intended to go on not looking at it for as long as he could manage.

Down on the Mole there was a crowd, and a tarpaulin, and a body under a sail.

*Somebody's had a morning*, he thought, and brought her round into the wind, and did not know how right he was.

---

"Captain Vane," said Oona Salt. "You look like forty miles of bad road."

"Harbour-Queen."

"Four hundred and eighty sovereigns."

"Four hundred and twenty," said Vane. "I paid you sixty in the spring."

"Four hundred and eighty with the spring's interest, which you did not read, because you never read anything." She looked past him at the ship on the tower. "You're down a tank."

"I'm down a tank."

"And up a great deal of weight." Her eyes came back to him. "You've twenty-odd farmhands on that deck, a press on your foredeck under a tarp which I can see the shape of from here and which is Office property, a Veil scribe in grey who hasn't blinked since she came down the ladder, a thing nine foot tall that has not moved in ten minutes, and a hold full of what you've been running up and down this coast for two years."

"Oona—"

"I'm not asking, Vane." She said it almost kindly. "I'm telling you what I can see from forty feet, so that you'll understand what everybody else on this Mole can see too, and what they'll be saying in the Lantern Yard by noon, and what they'll be saying in the Church's counting-house at the top of this town by this evening, because there's a Flambeau chapter up there now and they pay for talk. You've brought a cargo into my harbour that is worth eleven thousand sovereigns to the Catalogue, forty thousand to the Consortium, and the rope to anybody caught holding it."

Vane put his hands in his pockets.

"How much to put her on the drift-bench," he said, "and give me a whole number-three tank, and let me out of your harbour in four days?"

"The cargo."

"No."

"The ship, then. Sign the *Kestrel* over, take your farmhands off, and I'll find you a berth on something going east."

"No."

"Then you've nothing I want," said Oona Salt, "and a tank is a hundred and ten sovereigns of drift-glass on a dead market, Captain, and the yard is Harbour property, and I am a woman with a dead boy on this Mole this morning and no patience at all."

He had known all of it was coming. He had worked through the whole conversation three times in eleven days, at the wheel, at night, over the Verdant Pilgrimage, and he had arrived at the same place every time, which is the place you arrive at when you have nothing to trade: you have to give them a thing they want more than money.

"There's a war coming," said Vane.

"There's always—"

"Not like this one." He took it out of his coat: a sheet of paper, folded in four, in a young engineer's careful hand. A fair copy of a fair copy. "That's a transcript of an Arbiterium cover-sheet dated eight months before the Dawning Spire broke, and it says the Rite of Pure Unison was never about fuel. It says an Anchor will give up its law if you convince it that somebody's taken over the singing, and that Sol-Luminary was the trial, and that the method transfers to the other seven." He held it out. "There's a courier running east with the order for the full attempt in his hand. When that gets read out in front of the Consortium's seven chairs and the High Patriarch, the Synod comes apart, and every coast on this continent is on fire by midwinter."

Oona Salt did not take the paper.

"And why," she said, "would the Harbour-Queen of Port Cantabile want to know that four days before the Church does?"

"Because you've got eleven weeks to decide which end of the rope you're holding," said Vane, "and I'm giving them to you for a lift-tank."

---

She gave him a job instead, which he had expected, because nobody on this coast has ever once simply accepted a deal.

"The Chime," she said.

"No."

"Captain—"

"*No.* Harbour-Queen, I'll do anything else you've got. I'll carry contraband to Driftwood Cay. I'll fly the Null Sentry line for you with the Legion shooting at me—"

"There's a drift-tank on the Chime," said Oona.

Vane stopped.

"The *Seraph's Gale*," she said. "Synod packet-boat, forty-one, went into the reef in the gale of '16 with four tanks in her belly and nobody's been able to get at them, because the Sunken Carillon is a flooded bell-tower with a siren in it and the last two crews that went never came back up. I have been looking at those tanks for three years." She let it sit. "Bring me two. Keep one. Debt cleared, bench free, and I'll have you off my coast in four days with a full set."

"You said the last two crews didn't come up."

"They didn't have a Cantor," said Oona Salt, "or a glass-armed man, or whatever that is standing on your foredeck." She looked up at the *Kestrel*. "Three days. The tide's wrong before Dawnday and after Memoryday there's weather."

Vane rubbed his face with both hands.

"One more thing," he said. "And I want it straight, because I'll find out anyway and then we'll have an unpleasantness."

"The woman," said Oona.

"The woman."

"She came in off the water in the evening eleven weeks ago. She paid in old coin and she asked which captain cut his tow over the trench off the Cape, and I told her, because that is what I do." She said it without a shred of apology, which he had also expected. "And since then there have been seven dead in this town that I know of, and all seven of them are men who dealt in bottles, and all seven of them went with two cuts that crossed, here—" she drew it on herself, a shallow X across the belly — "and not one of them made a sound doing it. I have a dealer on the Salt Stair who has not opened his shutters in nine days. He is the last one." A pause. "She is not here for you, Captain. You flatter yourself. She is going up that staircase one door at a time."

"And when she gets to the top of it?"

"Then I expect she'll have time for old friends," said Oona Salt.

---

He went up the Mole with his hands shaking and put them in his pockets so the crew would not see, which did not work, because Mags Tennant missed nothing and Mags Tennant was waiting at the tower with her arm still strapped.

*Well?* she signed.

*Three days. We dive the Chime for tanks. Then she clears the debt.*

Mags looked at him for a moment.

*The Chime ate the Hollis brothers.*

*I know.*

*And Dunnet's crew.*

*I know, Mags.*

She let that stand just long enough to be a comment, which was her entire conversational style, and then signed: *The farmers?*

That was the thing he had been carrying up the Mole underneath the rest of it, and he let himself look at it properly for the first time.

Twenty-one people from Oakhaven: nine children, Nell Carder, Hetty Bram, old Bettany, a Tanner cousin, Lissa Merrow with no hands, and the rest. They had been on his deck for twenty days. They had watched a town go under a wood and a man grow into the foredeck, and two of the children had stopped talking entirely, and there was nowhere in the Reach to put any of them back.

"Port Cantabile's no place for them," he said aloud, and signed it after.

*No,* Mags agreed.

*Driftwood Cay. Hesper's people take in wreck-orphans; they've been doing it since the Screaming Year.* He watched her read it. *Four days. Oona gives us the tank, we put the farmers on the Cay with money, and then we take four hundred and thirty-two people home.*

Mags signed: *And the Cantor?*

"The Cantor," said Vane, "will tell me what the Cantor wants, at volume, at a time of her own choosing."

---

Kaelen came down the ladder at noon with his coat on in warm weather and his collar up, which told anybody who had been watching him for twenty days exactly where the tideline had got to.

He stood on the Long Mole in the sun and looked at the harbour: the fans of coral green under the water, the carillon rigging on the moored ships — Cantabile boats carry a bar of little bronze tubes at the shroud-head, which the wind plays, which is beautiful and which inland people find horrifying — and the Salt Stair going up the cliff in six flights with the Lantern Yard halfway and the Church's counting-house at the top.

"Engineer," said Sable, beside him. "Don't say anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to say that it is a fine harbour." She had the Ledger at her elbow and her eyes on the water. "Say nothing for a moment and listen instead."

So he listened, and heard what she had heard.

Out past the Cay, at the edge of what the ear could hold, something was ringing. Not the harbour's note; the harbour's note was a flat E in the coral under everything. This was higher and slower and came in groups of three, and between the groups there were long gaps in which the silence had a shape to it, like a held breath.

His arm had been quietly ringing along with it since he came down the ladder.

"The Sunken Carillon," said Sable. "Dungeon Three on the Office's old hazard schedule, which lists it as *closed, siren-held, do not insure*. The bells are the original harbour peal of a town that went under in the Screaming Year. They are forty feet down and they still ring, and nobody can say what rings them." She tilted her head. "And it is Velocity country. Six hundred and thirty-nine. Your arm is Dawning glass and it should not care."

Kaelen looked down at his left hand.

"It cares," he said.

---

Lyra came off the ship last, with Bettany's hand under her elbow, and stood on the Long Mole and looked at the sea for a long time without saying anything, because she had never seen it, and because of what was lying under a sail twelve feet away.

She went over to it.

Nobody stopped her. The Mole crew had got the body up and nobody had yet decided where it was going, and a Harbour-Queen with a morning like hers had six other things on her hands, and the upshot was a nineteen-year-old on a tarpaulin in the sun with his uncle sitting on a bollard beside him not doing anything at all.

Lyra Ashgrove knelt down on the stone.

"He's not to be sung over," said Teague, without looking up. "He went in a quiet. They say the sound doesn't take, after. They say it's wasted on them."

"Who says?"

"Everybody."

"Then everybody is guessing," said Lyra, in a voice like a torn sheet, "because nobody down there has ever come back to tell us, and in my town we sing the ones we're not sure about twice."

And she sang the first part of the office on the Long Mole at Port Cantabile in front of ninety strangers, badly, in a voice six weeks ruined, in a harbour where no one had sung out of doors in four years.

It cost her. Bettany got her under the arms at the end of the second part and sat her down on the stone with her head between her knees, and there was blood at the corner of her mouth again, and Kaelen came across the Mole very fast and then stopped dead six feet away because she put her hand up without lifting her head.

It also did something nobody expected, which Vane understood about four seconds before anyone else and which made him want to sit down.

Every rigging-chime in the harbour answered her.

Not loudly. A ripple — the little bronze tubes at forty shroud-heads picking up the note and giving it back, boat by boat, out across the water, so that for perhaps six seconds the whole of Port Cantabile rang quietly in the key of a dead boy's funeral.

Ninety people on the Long Mole went absolutely silent.

Then Teague, who had not moved in an hour, put his face in his hands.

And on the second-from-top flight of the Salt Stair, far up the cliff, in a shuttered room above a shop that had not opened in nine days, something that had been waiting very patiently for a week heard a Cantor's voice come up off the harbour — and stopped waiting.

## CHAPTER THREE
### The Salt Stair

> *Article the Twelfth. Nothing is to be lifted off a vessel lying on the Harbour's bench. The Harbour does not forbid theft; the Harbour forbids theft from the Harbour's own pocket, which is a different crime and carries the drop.*
>
> — Articles of the Free Captains of Port Cantabile

*Port Cantabile. The fourth of Ashfall, afternoon.*

---

The Salt Stair went up the cliff in six flights, and every flight was forty-one steps, and every step was wet.

Kaelen counted them on the way up because counting was a thing to do with the part of his head that had been telling him, since the Mole, that his arm had begun to ring along with something out past the Cay and had not stopped.

Four feet wide. Cut into the rock, with a gutter down the middle that ran all day with the overflow of the cistern at the top, so that the stone was green and slick and the whole thing smelled of salt and soap. On the left, the cliff. On the right, a rail of iron stanchions and two strung cables, and beyond that a drop that started at thirty feet on the first flight and finished at a hundred and ninety at the sixth.

At the head of each flight, a landing: a flat place the size of a small room with a lantern on a hook, a washing line, and usually a shop cut back into the rock. Rope and tar on the first. A bottle-dealer on the second, shuttered nine days. On the third, a chandler with a diving-helm in the window that had been new in his grandfather's time.

They were going to the chandler, which is a very boring reason to be on a staircase.

"Three suits," Vane had said. "And weight-belts, and forty fathom of air-pipe, and a bellows crew, and don't let him tell you the brass is from Fortis because it never is."

Kaelen went up with Mags, because Mags could carry more one-armed than most men could with two; with Tobiah Marsh, because Tobiah had asked and Kaelen had not been able to think of a reason to say no; with Sable, who wanted the shuttered shop on the second landing; and with Coll Madder, who came because somebody had to carry rope and because he had been on a ship for twenty days with a wife and a newborn and had asked, very politely, whether he might walk somewhere.

Coll Madder was thirty-four. He had a daughter nineteen days old who had been hummed into the world through a clay jar.

That is the whole of what matters about him and Kaelen would have it in his head for years.

---

They were on the fourth flight when the lantern on the landing above them went out.

Kaelen stopped walking.

It had not guttered; there was no wind in a cliff-cut stair, which is one of the two reasons people live on one. It had gone out the way a lamp goes out when a hand comes over the top of it.

"Down," he said.

Nobody moved, because *down* on a staircase means nothing, and because they were all looking up at the dark landing, which is what you do.

So the first thing came down on them while every face was tilted.

It was a crate of ballast-stone, about a hundredweight, and it came off the landing edge twelve feet above them and it had been aimed at the middle of the line, which was Mags. She did not see it and she did not hear it. Kaelen hit her in the shoulder with his hip and took them both into the cliff-wall, and the crate went past his back close enough to take the skin off two knuckles and burst on the step behind, and the stone of it went down the stair in a clattering rush, and then there were men.

Six from below, coming up fast.

Two from the landing above, coming down.

---

Kaelen's first thought, which arrived with the clarity of a dropped tool, was: *four feet wide*.

He could not swing his arm.

It had taken him nineteen days and four fights to learn what Oakhaven had cost him in ribs: the glass is nine inches longer than a man's arm ought to be and weighs most of a hundredweight at the end of the reach, and in a street or a vault it is the single most dangerous thing in the room, and in a corridor it is a wall you happen to be carrying. There was the cliff on his left and a cable rail on his right and three feet of green wet stone between, and the swing he had put through a Legionnaire's bell and a Steam Golem's knee would, here, hit rock.

So he did not swing it.

He turned side-on, left shoulder downhill, and made the arm a door.

The first man up the stair came into it at a run with a fish-knife low, and Kaelen caught the blade and the wrist and the whole man on the flat of the glass and let him break himself on it, and the sound — glass taking a running weight, a deep clean *bock* like a struck post — went up the stairwell and bounced.

The second one did the sensible thing and went under.

He was not a soldier. None of them were. They were dock-men and bottom-crew, five of them with knives and gaffs and one with a boarding axe, and they fought like men who have won fights by weight and surprise in places where nobody has anywhere to go. This one went down on his hands and came in low under the glass to take Kaelen's legs, which would have worked on a drier step and nearly worked on this one; Kaelen's boot went out from under him in the gutter, and he came down on his bad ribs on the stone edge and felt something in his chest go from mended to not.

Mags Tennant killed that man with a belaying pin.

She had come off the wall while Kaelen was going down, and she had one arm and no voice and a nine-inch hardwood pin she had carried up the stair in her belt, and she put it into the back of his skull at the joint with her whole bodyweight behind it, and then she was past him and down two steps, filling the stair, because she had worked out what Kaelen had worked out: *four feet wide is a door, and a door only has to be held by one person at a time.*

---

Above them, Sable said, "Article the Twelfth."

She said it in the flat clear voice of a woman reading a will, up the stairwell, into the dark landing where two more were coming down.

"Nothing is to be lifted off a vessel lying on the Harbour's bench. You all signed the Articles; every man on this coast signs them at fourteen. That ship is on the Harbour-Queen's bench as of this morning and her cargo is Harbour business, and what you are doing is not theft, it is theft *from the Harbour*, and it carries the drop."

One of them hesitated.

Kaelen felt it happen above him — a man's weight changing on a step — and understood in that half-second two things about the archivist that he had not previously bothered to put together: that she could not fight at all, and that she had just done the only useful thing available to her, which was to make one man in eight think about a rope.

Then the other one shoved past the hesitator and came down three steps at a time with a gaff.

Tobiah Marsh got in front of her.

He had no business doing it. He was twenty-four and an engineer and the whole of his combat experience was standing on a rail calling pitch numbers while his hero sabotaged a bore, and he got in front of a Veil scribe with his arms out because she was behind him and small and there was nobody else, and the gaff went into him under the ribs on the right side and came out again.

He sat down on the stair very suddenly, with an expression of enormous surprise.

---

Here is what Kaelen Vance did in the next eleven seconds, which he would be able to recount afterward in exact order, because that is the curse of a mind built for failure analysis.

He got up off the ribs.

He did not go up toward Tobiah, because there were four men below him and Mags was holding them alone with one arm at the narrow of the flight and had already been cut twice across the forearm.

He put his right hand on the iron stanchion beside him — the third from the bottom of that flight, the one with the rust-bloom at the base where forty years of cistern overflow had run down the inside of the post — and he pulled.

Every structure fails where the load finds it. The Salt Stair's rail was a hundred and nine years old and its stanchions were set in lead pockets in the rock, and the lead on the gutter side had been wet for a century, and he had been reading the whole flight on the way up without meaning to, because he always did, because it was the only thing his head had ever been any good at.

The stanchion came out of the rock.

He went backward with it into the cliff-face and brought eleven feet of cable rail down off three posts, and the two men coming up on the outside of the stair — the axe and the gaff — went out into the air with it.

They did not scream all the way down. The Coast would talk about that: there was a hundred and sixty feet under that flight and they went quiet after about forty, which is what men do when they stop believing it.

Four left. Two below, two above.

---

The man with the boarding axe had been the one on the landing, and he came down the last flight at Kaelen with the axe up, which in a four-foot stair is an error.

Kaelen let him raise it.

He stepped *in* — one pace, inside the arc, which is the one place an axe cannot reach and the last place anybody wants to be — and put his glass hand flat on the man's chest and shoved him into the cliff wall, and the man's head hit the rock and his axe hit the rock a half-beat later, and then Kaelen hit him with the glass at a range of eight inches, twice, and that was that.

The sound of the second one was not good. He would hear it in the spring.

Behind him, Mags put her pin through a man's knee, took his gaff off him as he went down, and used it as a bar across the stair, and the last man below her stopped coming.

Above, the one who had hesitated over Article the Twelfth turned and ran up the stair into the dark, and his mate went after him.

It had taken about forty seconds.

---

Tobiah Marsh was sitting against the cliff wall with both hands over the hole in his side and blood coming between all eight fingers, saying, "I'm all right. I'm all right. It's not — it's the side, it's just the side, it's not the—"

"Hands off. Let me see it. *Marsh.* Hands off."

It was the side, and it was not just the side; the gaff had gone in under the floating rib and come out dirty, and a gaff is a hook made for fish and the exit had taken a piece with it. Kaelen packed it with Sable's scarf and Mags's shirt and got the boy's belt round it, and Tobiah shook so hard his heels drummed on the stone.

"Coll," said Kaelen, without looking up. "Get down the Mole and bring the midwife — the old woman, Bettany — and tell her gaff wound, right side, under the rib. Coll."

Nobody answered.

---

He was on the flight below, at the turn, where the rail was gone.

He had taken the first man up the stair, who had gone into the glass at a run and come off it dazed and come up again with his knife, because dock-men on this coast have been in a hundred fights that never got anybody killed and do not stop the way soldiers stop. Coll Madder had put the coil of rope down to help. That was the whole of it. He had been standing at the back with forty fathom of air-pipe and a coil of three-strand over his shoulder, and a man had come at the Cantor's people with a knife, and Coll Madder from the Hollow had set the rope down on the step and taken hold of him.

They had gone over at the turn, where the stanchions were out.

Kaelen got down the flight in four strides. There was nothing at the bottom but the Lantern Yard roofs, a hundred and twenty feet down, and two shapes on them, and people already running.

He stood there with his hand on the last stanchion that was still in the rock.

He had pulled the rail out eleven seconds before. He had done the arithmetic on a hundred and nine years of wet lead with a man with an axe coming at him and he had got it right, and the rail had come out exactly where he meant it to come out, and he had not once — not in any part of the eleven seconds — thought about who was standing on the open side of the flight behind him.

"Engineer," said Sable, from above.

"I know."

"The boy is going to bleed to death on this staircase if we stand here."

"*I know.*"

---

They carried Tobiah Marsh down four flights of the Salt Stair in a cargo net, and Bettany met them at the second landing, and the boy lived.

That was not obvious for three days. Bettany worked on him on a trestle in the chandler's back room with Lyra holding a lamp and two of Hesper's girls holding Tobiah, and at the end of it she came out into the Yard and sat down on a step and said, "If it doesn't go bad, he'll be on his feet in a month and useless for two. It'll go bad or it won't. That's all there is to say and I'd thank you not to ask me again before Driftday."

Coll Madder was carried up the Mole at four in the afternoon by six men of the harbour crew, with Vane walking in front of them, because Vane had insisted on that with a particular expression that stopped the argument.

Ruth Madder came down the ladder off the *Kestrel* with nineteen days of baby in her arms.

Lyra went to her. She had nothing in her throat and she had nothing in her hands and she had been singing somebody else's dead boy that morning on this same stretch of stone, and she went to her anyway, and Ruth Madder hit her once, hard, across the face, and then took hold of her and did not let go for a long time, and both of those things were fair.

---

Afterward, in the last of the light, Oona Salt's people went up the Salt Stair to collect what was left on it, because that is Harbour business and because the Articles are specific about bodies.

They found four on the stair and two in the Lantern Yard.

They found the two who had run — the one who had hesitated at Article the Twelfth and his mate — on the sixth landing, at the very top, outside the shuttered shop of the last bottle-dealer in Port Cantabile.

Neither of them had got his knife out.

They were lying a yard apart, tidily, on their backs, and each of them had two cuts across the belly that crossed in the middle: a long shallow X, opened with something that had gone through leather and shirt and skin in a single draw without snagging on any of them. There was very little blood. There was, the Harbour's man noted, no blood at all on the stones around them, which is not possible, and which he put in his report anyway.

Nobody on the sixth landing had heard a thing. Three families live on the sixth landing. One of them had been eating supper eleven feet from the door.

And the shutters of the bottle-dealer's shop, which had been closed for nine days, were standing open onto an empty room with a lamp still burning in it, and the stock-shelves behind the counter were bare, and on the counter, in a row, somebody had set out four blue glass bottles with their wax seals unbroken and their labels turned to face the door, the way you set out a thing you want found.
