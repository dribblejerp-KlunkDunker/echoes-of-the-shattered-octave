## CHAPTER SEVENTEEN
### The Cloister of the Lost Choir

> *Requisition 44: small clear voices, forty (40). Source: charitable choir, Cloister road, Whisper-Grove. Note: one roof, one hour, one wagon. Efficient.*
>
> — Office of the Catalogue, requisition file, Ashfall 416

*The Cloister road. Nullday, first light.*

---

The Cloister of the Lost Choir had been called something else before 416. Nobody could remember what.

That was the joke Sable made about it, walking up the road in the fog with her arm strapped across her chest, and it was not a joke, and nobody laughed. The building was low and long and built of good grey stone, with a slate roof and a bell-cote with no bell in it, and silver willows growing so close on the north side that their branches had come in through two windows and made themselves at home.

"Two hundred yards past the orchard wall is the Malting," Sable said. "That is the harvest-house. Eleven staff, two presses, a queue-yard, and a sky-dock they do not use because the Office's freight comes by road. We will see the chimneys from the choir-room windows. I stood in that room twice and looked at those chimneys and came home."

"Then we go in through the choir-room," said Vane.

"Yes."

"Is there anything in the choir-room?"

"There was an Aegis pattern on the requisition," said Sable. "Office property is guarded until the Office withdraws it. The Office has not withdrawn it."

Mags, who had been reading her lips by lantern-light and had missed nothing, made one short gesture: *how big.*

"Nine feet," said Sable.

---

Lyra went in first, because she was the only one of them who had a reason to want to.

The door had been left open. Three winters had come in through it: leaf-mould in the porch, a drift of it against the inner door, and on the flagstones the pale corkscrews of seedlings that had come up and died for want of light.

Inside, the choir-room went back forty feet in the grey dawn.

It was a good room. That was the first thing she thought, and she thought it in the language of her trade: it was a *good room*, high and narrow with a barrel ceiling of plastered lath, the sort of room that gives a young voice back to itself just slightly improved, so that a child who is not sure she can sing stands in it and hears herself and decides that she can.

Down the left-hand wall, at child height, a row of iron pegs.

There were cloaks on them. Eleven cloaks, small ones, wool, mostly brown, one of them red and much better made than the others. Three years of damp had taken them; they hung in their own shapes, furred green along the shoulders, and under each peg was a pair of boots or clogs, set neatly, toes to the wall.

Lyra stood in front of the red cloak for a while.

At the far end of the room were the risers: three tiers of oak, waxed by generations of small hands, with the middle tier worn into shallow dips where children had stood in the same eleven places for a century. Chalk crosses on the boards. And behind them, on the wall, the hymn board — a slate frame with the number chalked up for the last practice anyone had held in this room.

**HYMN 9.**

And on the floor between the risers and the door, a shoe. One. Small.

Kaelen came in behind her and stopped.

She heard him not say anything, and was grateful, and then she heard a second thing, underneath, and turned round with her hand up.

*Listen.*

Very faint, very slow, from the dark at the back of the room beyond the risers: a sound like a cupboard full of plates settling.

Then the chime.

---

It came out of the alcove behind the risers and it did not hurry, and it was nine feet if it was an inch.

Bronze and iron, green-black with three years of damp, built like a man in the way a bell is built like a mouth — a barrel chest, two long arms, legs of piston and strut that unfolded, that *unfolded*, so that the thing stood up out of its alcove in three separate motions and had to duck its head under the barrel ceiling. There was no face. There was a grille where a face would be, and behind the grille, dim, a single point of lilac light.

Across its chest-plate, stencilled in the Office's block capitals and kept clean of verdigris by something, three years after the last child had left this room:

**DEFEND THE SINGERS**

"Out," said Vane. "Out, out, back up, *out*—"

It hit Mags first because Mags was nearest the risers.

It did not swing at her. It *stepped*, and a piston in its shoulder let go with a crack, and the arm came across in a flat arc at the speed of a falling gate, and it took her off her feet and into the wall under the pegs twelve feet away with a sound Lyra felt in her own ribs. Plaster came down. One of the cloaks fell off its peg onto the floor.

Mags got up.

That was the moment Lyra understood how the next four minutes were going to go, because Mags got up with one arm hanging and blood coming out of her hairline and her mouth shut, and the Aegis had already turned its head an inch to track her, and the point of lilac light behind the grille had not changed at all.

Kaelen went at it with the glass.

He got one clean strike in — the arm came round and up and the fist went into the thing's flank with a noise like a cathedral door slamming, and Lyra saw the bronze *dent*, and saw Kaelen's whole body stop dead against three hundredweight of standing iron that did not move an inch.

Then it hit him.

Not with the arm. It brought its chest round and *opened* — a row of louvres in the barrel of its torso lifting like the ribs of a fan — and out of the chest came a chord.

Lyra had spent her life inside sound and she had never heard anything like it. Nine tuned bars and three bells, struck at once, at close range, in a stone room built to give voices back: a great bronze shout of a chord with a bite to it, and it went through the choir-room and off the barrel ceiling and came down on all of them at once.

Her knees went. The lantern went out of her hand. She was on the flags with her palms over her ears and her mouth open and the after-ring rolling around inside her skull, and she could not get her eyes to hold still. Ten feet away, Vane was down on one knee, retching. Sable had gone over sideways into the risers with her bad arm under her and was not moving.

Kaelen was on the floor screaming.

His arm had done what it did at the plaza and worse: the glass had caught the chord and held it, and was ringing with it, and could not stop, and the whole limb had gone rigid from fingertip to collarbone with the sound still pouring through it, and he was on his back on the flagstones of a children's choir-room with his own arm ringing him apart.

The Aegis took a step toward the nearest singer.

That was the part Lyra could not make sense of afterward, lying awake: it did not go for Kaelen, who had struck it. It went for the *risers* — for Sable, who had fallen into them — because the risers were where the singers stood, and the directive on its chest was four words long and had no clause in it for who was standing there.

And Mags Tennant, who had heard none of it, walked through the middle of the chord.

---

Deafness is not silence. Mags had told Vane that once, in hand-cant, at some length, and he had repeated it to Lyra on the deck two nights before without knowing why he was telling her. *You think it's a room with the door shut. It isn't. It's a room that was never built.*

So the chord that put four people on the floor of the Cloister of the Lost Choir did nothing at all to the bosun of the *Drift-Kestrel*, except move the air against her face.

She crossed twenty feet of flagstone with one arm swinging dead, got both hands into the collar of Kaelen's coat, and dragged him backward out of the thing's path in three long heaves, and then put herself between the Aegis and the risers with her boots planted and her one good fist up, which was, Lyra thought later, the single most ridiculous and magnificent thing she had ever seen a human being do.

The Aegis stopped.

It looked at her. There is no other word; the grille came round and the lilac point behind it steadied on her face, and for a moment the whole machine was absolutely still, and something inside its chest went *tick*, three times, unevenly, like a clock being wound by somebody nervous.

Then it lifted the arm.

---

Lyra was on her feet without knowing she had got up.

She could not shout. She could not sing. She had six days of nothing in her throat and a hole under her jaw and Bettany's *not for weeks*, and there was no version of the next four seconds in which a Cantor's voice was of the slightest use to anybody.

So she did the only thing she had left, which was the thing she had done in a water-meadow at dusk with a whistle and a seven-year-old, and the thing she had done in a lit plaza with a tuning key and two fingers in her mouth.

She pointed at the hymn board.

Sable, on her back in the risers, with her eyes streaming from the chord, turned her head and looked at where the Cantor was pointing.

**HYMN 9.**

And Lyra saw her understand — saw the whole thing land on the woman in about a second and a half, because Sable was the fastest reader of a situation Lyra had ever met and that was exactly her curse — and saw her face go the colour of the plaster.

"No," said Sable.

Lyra pointed again, hard.

"I can't. I have never sung in my *life*. I don't—" She was scrambling backward up the risers on one elbow as nine feet of iron came at her. "I stood in the *doorway*. I stood in the doorway with the intake sheets and they were singing it while we counted them, do you understand, they sang it the *whole way through the first eleven names*—"

Lyra got up onto the bottom riser in front of her, and turned, and put her back to the machine, and lifted both hands in front of the archivist's face in the oldest gesture of her trade — palms up, thumbs together, the way a Cantor asks a frightened child in a sickroom for a note.

*Give it to me.*

And Sable, entry-writer of the Office of the Catalogue, who had signed seventy-four thousand six hundred and eleven people into a book and had not sung since she was a girl, opened her mouth and sang Hymn 9 of the Cloister of the Lost Choir.

It was terrible. It came out flat and cracked and far too low, in the wrong key, in a voice that had been reading aloud and nothing else for three years:

*"Hold us in the little light — hold us in the little light until the morning comes—"*

The Aegis Sentinel stopped with its arm at the top of its swing.

---

It stood like that for the length of two more lines, while Sable sang the hymn to the floor of the room she had counted children in.

Then the louvres in its chest closed, one rib at a time.

And it knelt.

Nine feet of Office ironwork went down on one knee-strut on the flagstones with a crash that brought more plaster off the ceiling, and put its head down below the level of the risers, which is where a head goes when you are nine feet tall and you are looking up at somebody standing on the bottom tier.

And then, out of the shut chest, very quietly, it chimed.

Three bells and two bars. Not the tune Sable was singing.

The line under it.

Lyra's hands went up over her mouth. She knew what she was hearing the way you know your own name: it was the second line of a three-part hymn, the middle voice, the alto — the part you give to the children who are steady and can hold a note against a tune that is trying to pull them off it. It was in time. It was in tune. It was being played by a machine that had knelt down to do it, in a room where forty-one children had sung that exact part three years ago while a clerk in the doorway wrote their names in a book.

Sable stopped singing.

The Aegis went on for one more bar, alone, and then it stopped too, and waited — chest shut, head down, the lilac light steady — the way a child waits, on a riser, for the next line.

---

Afterward:

Mags: collarbone broken, three ribs, sixteen stitches in the scalp, and would not sit down until Vane physically sat on her.

Kaelen: the arm came back over the course of an hour, the way a leg comes back from being slept on, and rang a tone and a half flat for the rest of the day. When he could stand, he went out to the porch and was sick in the leaf-mould, which he seemed to find more humiliating than the screaming, and then came back in and measured the tideline with a piece of string, because he had left the tailor's tape in a hut in Oakhaven, and did not tell anybody the number.

Vane: bruised, deaf in one ear till noon, and unable to look at the row of pegs.

Sable: she got down off the risers, and stood in front of the kneeling Aegis for a long moment, and then she did the thing that Lyra would think about for months.

She stepped up and put her hand on the grille in its chest — the small grille low down under the stencilled words, where the core sits — and tilted her head to read the serial stamped in the bronze beside it in the Office's blocky numerals.

Lyra was four feet away. She saw the number too.

**WG-41,005.**

She did not know what it meant. She knew what it did to Sable, which was that every bit of colour went out of the woman's face in the space of one breath, and her hand came off the bronze as though it had burned her, and she took one step back and then another.

"Sable?"

She did not answer. She looked at the kneeling machine for perhaps five seconds.

Then she said, in a perfectly level voice, "We should move. The Malting has a shift-bell at the seventh hour and we want the yard crossing before it," and turned and walked out of the choir-room into the fog.

The Aegis Sentinel got up and followed them.

Nobody invited it. Nobody could have stopped it. It came out through the door it had been left to guard for three years, ducking its head, nine feet of Office property walking out of Office property, and fell in behind Lyra Ashgrove at a distance of about eight paces and stayed there, and when she stopped, it stopped.

She looked back at it once from the orchard wall. It had the red cloak from the floor of the choir-room hanging over the fingers of its left hand, picked up in passing, carried the way you carry something you mean to give back.
