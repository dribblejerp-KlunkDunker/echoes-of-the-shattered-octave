## CHAPTER FOUR
### The Boar & Dulcimer

> *VOX. Genuine Last Words, Sealed at the Deathbed.*
> *Do Not Unstop Within a Mile of Open Country.*
> *No Refunds.*
>
> — Label on a vox-bottle, Port Cantabile

*Oakhaven. Hearthday.*

---

There were twelve crates in the hold of the *Drift-Kestrel*, and all twelve of them talked in their sleep.

Captain Vane had carried worse. He told himself so at regular intervals, generally with a drink in his hand, and it had the great virtue of being true. He had carried powder for rebels and saints' bones for the Church, and forty barrels of something from the Blighted Caldera that ate through its own staves in a week and had to be poured into fresh barrels every three days by men in gloves. He had once carried a live Terrasque calf for a collector in Fortis, and it had eaten Pennick's predecessor and most of the starboard rail. In twenty-five years of carrying things for money, he had carried almost everything.

He had never carried anything that talked in its sleep.

"Morning," he said to the hold, from the top of the ladder.

The hold, being full of stencilled crates — OFFICE OF THE CATALOGUE • RESONANT ENGRAMS • HANDLE IN SILENCE — did not say good morning back. It said what it always said: a low overlapping murmur, like a crowded room heard through a floor. A man counting. A woman giving directions to somewhere. Somebody laughing at a joke that had been told a long time ago. It never stopped, it never grew louder, and it never quite resolved into anything, and if you stood at the top of the ladder long enough, you started leaning down toward it to make out the words.

Vane had stopped standing at the top of the ladder long enough.

He shut the hatch and went up on deck, where it was merely cold.

---

The *Drift-Kestrel* rode at the Consortium's mooring mast a hundred feet above the Oakhaven quarry, turning slowly on her line in a wind that smelled of wet stone and cut timber and, faintly, of the green-gold glass the quarry had been bleeding for two years and more. She was sixty feet of brass rib and oak plank under a grey sailcloth envelope that had been patched so often it looked like a quilt, and four lift-tanks slung beneath her keel glowed the faint cobalt of drift-glass. Drift-glass wanted, above all things, to *move*; the whole art of skyship engineering lay in persuading it that the direction it most wanted to move in was up. When the persuasion was going well, the tanks hummed a low, contented chord. At present they were humming a chord with a sour note in it, which meant Pennick had been up all night with his tuning-hammer again and would be impossible until noon.

She was not a beautiful ship. She was not, strictly speaking, entirely his ship. But she was the only thing Vane owned that did not float on water, and he loved her with the uncomplicated devotion of a man who has decided not to think about how she was paid for.

Mags was at the rail, splicing line.

Margarethe Tennant was six feet and some inches of Rust Coast bosun, with forearms like hawsers and a face like a closed door, and she had been deaf since the day she was born. She read lips in three languages and spoke Rust Coast hand-cant faster than Vane could follow when she was angry, which was often. When the tonal trenches had started opening off the Coast, and the first vox-bottles had turned up in the markets at Cantabile, and it had become generally understood that the dark was now full of things that could call to you in your mother's voice, the Free Captains had begun paying double for deaf bosuns. Vane had hired Mags before all that. He liked to think it showed foresight. It had in fact shown that she was the only bosun on the Driftwood Cay docks who had not already heard about him.

*Ship's sick*, Mags signed, without looking up from the splice. *Tanks.*

*Pennick?*

*Asleep on the tanks. Tuning-hammer in his fist. Like a baby with a rattle.*

*Let him sleep.* Vane leaned on the rail beside her and looked down into the quarry. *Paymaster?*

Mags spat over the side, which was answer enough.

Two days. Two days they had ridden at this mast with twelve crates of the Catalogue's property in the hold, waiting for a Consortium paymaster to sign a Consortium chit so that a Consortium garrison could take delivery of its replacement cores. The paymaster would not sign until the Factor approved the invoice, and the Factor was — the paymaster had said it with a perfectly straight face — *occupied*. So the crates stayed aboard. So the crates kept talking. So Captain Vane had spent the last two evenings at the only public house in Oakhaven, which had the singular distinction of being the one place in the Reach quieter than his own hold.

*Going to the tavern*, he signed.

Mags looked at him. She had a way of looking at him that he had not been looked at since he was a boy, by his grandmother, when he came home with his knuckles skinned.

*It's afternoon*, she signed.

*It's a very quiet tavern.*

*You'll come back drunk.*

*I'll come back*, Vane signed, *which is the main thing*, and swung himself onto the mast-ladder before she could answer.

---

He did not think about the rumor on the way down.

He was good at not thinking about it. He had had since spring to practice. A rumor in the waterfront taverns of Driftwood Cay: a woman, pale as a drowned thing, with eyes the color of the sky after sunset, going from tavern to tavern along the Rust Coast and asking, very politely, whether anyone knew where a certain captain was berthed these days. The captain who used to have the *Wave-Cutter*. The one who cut his tow over the trench off Cape Dissonance, five years back.

Vane had signed on for the Catalogue's northern runs the next morning. The northern runs went inland. Nobody had ever been drowned inland.

In his coat, against his ribs, a small stoppered bottle knocked with every rung of the ladder.

He did not think about that either.

---

The Boar & Dulcimer stood on the corner of the square across from the Greenhall, and it was, Vane had decided on his first night in Oakhaven, the saddest public house on the continent of Aethelgard. He had drunk in the taverns of Redoubt Zero, where the dead don't rot.

It was a good room in its bones. Low black beams, a deep hearth, settles worn smooth by generations of Reach backsides. Over the hearth, on two iron hooks, hung the dulcimer that gave the house its name: a fine old thing of polished cherrywood, with its strings cut. All of them, snipped short at the pegs, so that the bright ends curled like a dead man's hair.

The tankards were leather. The tables were covered in felt. Hush-lamps whistled from every beam. And by the door, on a black-painted board, in the landlady's square white capitals, were the rules of the house:

NO SINGING.
NO TOASTS.
NO DICE.
NO WEEPING ABOVE A HAND.

He had read that board every night for two nights, and every night it had got him in exactly the same place, somewhere under the breastbone, and he had had to go and sit down.

The landlady was Hetty Bram, who was nearly as big as Mags and considerably more frightening, and who communicated almost entirely with her eyebrows. She raised one at Vane as he came in, and he raised one back, and she drew him a leather mug of the dark Reach ale without being asked and put it on the bar and took his coin, and that was the whole of their conversation, as it had been the whole of their conversation for two nights. He was beginning to think it was the most honest relationship he had ever had.

He took the corner settle. It was late afternoon, and the room was a third full: carters and quarrymen mostly, a few old men by the hearth, a table of women from the dye-works. They drank the way people drank in Oakhaven, which was the way people prayed elsewhere: quietly, steadily, heads close together. The conversation was one low steady murmur, a hand's width of voice across the whole room.

It was, Vane thought, exactly like his hold. Except that everyone in here still had a body.

He drank to that. Silently. No toasts.

---

He was halfway down his second mug when the door opened, and the whole room turned its head without turning its head, the way a flock turns.

A man and a woman.

He knew the man before the door was shut. He had heard about him from the quarry-hands at the mast — *the glass man, the Governor's boy, the one the hounds won't touch* — and the moment the man stepped into the lamplight Vane could hear him too: a thin, high, sweet ringing, faint through the felt of his left sleeve, like a finger going round the rim of a wine glass in another room. Tall. Thirties. Charcoal under his nails and in the creases of his knuckles, and a face that had been handsome before it was made to do something it did not want to do for a very long time.

The woman, he didn't know. But the room did. Three people touched her sleeve as she crossed it — lightly, quickly, the way sailors touch a figurehead for luck — and murmured the same word: *Cantor*. She was small and dark and plainly dressed, with a rag wrapped round her right hand, and she crossed the room the way certain captains cross a deck. She didn't look like she thought she owned it. She looked like she thought she was responsible for it, which was worse, and much rarer.

They came straight to his corner.

"You're the *Kestrel*," said the glass man.

"I'm her captain," Vane said. "She's a ship. People do get that confused. She's much prettier." He pushed a stool out with his boot. "Sit. You're making the room nervous. Well — more nervous."

The man sat. The Cantor did not. She stood at his shoulder with her wrapped hand resting on the back of his stool and looked at Vane with a steady, measuring, not unkind look, as if she were counting his ribs.

"Kaelen Vance," said the glass man.

"I'd heard. Vane." He didn't offer a hand, and the glass man didn't seem to expect one. "And the lady?"

"Lyra Ashgrove." A whisper — torn and hoarse, barely a whisper at all. She touched her throat, and he understood. A Cantor with her voice gone. Like a gunner with wet powder.

"I need passage," said Kaelen Vance. "South. Three berths."

"South's a big word."

"Out of the Reach. Greenroot, then the Salt-Chime Route to the coast—"

"Not the coast."

It came out faster than he'd meant it to. The glass man looked at him. Vane smiled, broad and easy, the smile that had got him out of more harbor-masters' offices than he could count.

"The *Kestrel*'s a sky-girl," he said. "Salt gets in her tanks and makes her moody. Anywhere south of the Reach and north of the water, I'll take you. For a price."

"Name it."

Vane named it. He named, in fact, about three times what it was worth, on the principle that a man who asked a smuggler for passage in a public house in broad daylight was a man with nowhere else to go.

Kaelen Vance reached into his good coat and set a purse on the felt between them and loosened its string.

Consortium scrip. Green and black, stamped with the drill-and-wheel. A good deal of it.

Vane looked at the scrip. Then at the glass man. Then at the Cantor. Then, because he could not help it, he laughed — silently, into his mug, shoulders shaking.

"Consortium scrip," he said, when he could. "You're paying the Consortium's courier with the Consortium's money to carry something out from under the Consortium's nose." He wiped his eyes. "I don't know what it is yet, and I love it already."

"It's not a something," said Kaelen.

"It never is." Vane looked up at the Cantor. "It's you, isn't it?"

"I'm not going," Lyra Ashgrove whispered.

"She's going," said Kaelen.

Vane looked from one to the other. They were not looking at each other. They were so carefully not looking at each other that the air between them practically hummed. He had seen it before, in old married couples and in mutineers: two people who had been not-saying the same thing to each other for about twenty years and had got very, very good at it.

"Marvelous," he said. "I'll want that in writing. From both of you."

---

"How many can you carry?" the Cantor whispered.

"Three. With my cargo aboard."

"Put the cargo off."

"Can't. It isn't mine."

"What is it?"

"Glass," said Vane.

Kaelen Vance was looking at him. It was not a hostile look. It was the look of one man reading another man's manifest.

"Engrams," the glass man said quietly.

The word sat on the felt between them, next to the purse.

"Glass," Vane said again, pleasantly, and held his eye.

After a moment the glass man nodded, very slightly, the way you nod to another man in a place neither of you should be. He said nothing more about it. Vane found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he was grateful, and then found that he disliked being grateful, and drank.

The Cantor had missed none of it. He could see her not missing it.

"We survived the Fracture," she whispered — to Kaelen, not to him, as if it were an old argument between them. "We'll survive this."

"Survived?" Vane set his mug down. "Gods above, Cantor, don't romanticize it. Surviving just means you were lucky enough to be standing five paces to the left when the sky dropped its guts." He tipped his chair back against the wall. "Last year the tide came into Cantabile harbor singing a high C-flat, and my frigate — a good ship, the best ship I ever had, laid up at her moorings minding her own business — dissolved into blue jelly under the harbor-master's nose. Hull, masts, figurehead. There was a man asleep on her deck. We never found him." He shrugged. "You can't sail on water that forgets its own surface tension. So now I fly. And I carry glass for whoever pays. And if you three-chord saints ever do hammer those Spire-pins back into the bedrock, honest enterprise might resume, and I'll go back to smuggling brandy like an honest thief."

"When?" said Kaelen Vance.

"When do I leave?" Vane considered it. "When the Consortium pays me for the cargo, or when the Consortium tries to kill me. Whichever comes first. Could be tomorrow. Could be a week."

"Eight days. Hold the berths eight days."

"Eight days is a long time to hold a berth."

The glass man pushed the purse an inch further across the felt.

"Eight days," Vane agreed, and put his hand on it.

---

He didn't notice the man at the hearth until the man stood up.

Afterward he would think he should have. He'd been in enough taverns. He knew the look of a man who has been drinking with purpose since noon — the particular stillness of him, the way the landlady watered his mug without being asked and kept one eye on him while she did it. Vane had seen it and filed it — *trouble, hearth, not mine* — and gone back to his berths and his scrip.

A big man. Thirties. A carter's jerkin and hands scarred by rope. Red-eyed, and perfectly steady on his feet, which was the worst kind.

He stood up from the settle by the hearth, and the whole room stopped.

It was astonishing how completely it stopped. Vane had seen a crew go still on deck when a waterspout came up off the bow, and it was nothing to this. Every head in the Boar & Dulcimer came up and every mouth closed and every hand went flat on the felt, and in the silence the hush-lamps whistled from the beams like a field of frightened birds.

The Cantor's hand had closed hard on the back of Kaelen Vance's stool.

"Davin," she whispered. "Oh, no. Davin, no."

The big man lifted his chin and opened his mouth and sang.

*Oh, the last sheaf's in and the barley's high—*

Full voice. Not the Narrow. Not a hand's width. A big rough carter's baritone, cracked and wet with drink and grief, filling the low room from the flags to the black beams and going up the chimney and out through the shutters into the square, where it rang off the front of the Greenhall and came back.

*—and the swallows are low in the harvest sky—*

Hetty Bram was out from behind the bar. She went at him like a ship coming about, both big hands up, and he put out an arm without looking and pushed her away, and she stumbled into a table, and a leather tankard went over, and nobody moved to right it.

"She sang it," the big man said, between lines. He was weeping openly now. Above a hand. Well above. "Every harvest home. Every year of my life. Fifty years. And they've put the mark on her door, and they're going to burn her in her chair, and nobody's going to sing it for her. Nobody's allowed. So I'm going to sing it." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "She should hear it once more. She should hear it from somewhere."

And he went on.

*—so fill up your cup and fill up your can, for the reaper's the last and the best of man—*

Vane found that he was counting.

He didn't mean to. It was simply what he did when something was coming and he couldn't stop it. *One.* The Cantor was moving, slipping round the tables toward the hearth, her ruined voice making no sound at all. *Two.* Kaelen Vance was on his feet. *Three.* The old men at the hearth had their hands over their ears, as if that would do anything. *Four.* Someone should hit him. Someone should put a fist into the side of that big grieving head and lay him out on the flags and put a hand over his mouth, and everyone in the room knew it, and nobody moved, the way nobody moves when there's a snake on the floor.

*Five.* The Cantor reached him. She laid her wrapped hand flat on his chest, over his heart, and looked up into his face, and he looked down at her and kept singing, and the tears ran into his beard.

*Six.*

Outside, in the square, the iron-oaks began to ring.

It came from everywhere at once: every oak in Oakhaven together, a long rising shimmer of bronze, as though something enormous had drawn a hand across the whole town. The hush-lamps fluttered in their beams. Then the ringing stopped, all at once.

And in the silence after it, every bird in the square stopped too.

*Seven.*

Vane had his hands inside his coat.

*Eight.*

Faintly, from outside — from the square, from the lanes, from the Hedge, from several directions at once — someone began to sing *The Last Sheaf* back.

In Davin Hale's voice. His exact voice, cracked and wet and grieving. Only wrong. A half-beat behind. A quarter-tone flat. As if someone who had never heard a song before had listened to this one very carefully, just once, and was trying to sing along.

*Nine*, thought Vane, and knew exactly which number came next, and had known since the big man stood up, and laughed.

---

They came in through everything.

Not the door. The door was barred and felted and packed round with wool, and they didn't bother with it. They came in through the gaps — under the sill, down the chimney, through the chinks in the shutters — the way heat comes up off a summer road: a shimmer first, a wrongness in the air, a patch of room that looked as if it had been drawn by someone who did not quite understand rooms. Then the shimmer thickened and had a shape, and the shape was a dog. More or less. A long low hound-shape made of air that jittered like a bad reflection. It had no eyes. It had mouths instead, along the length of its back like the gill-slits of a shark, a row of small wet mouths that opened and closed. Out of every mouth came a different sound.

A baby laughing. A cartwheel creaking. A church bell, bright and far off, a bell nobody in the Reach had rung in three years. A man screaming *run*.

And from the mouth nearest its head, a quarter-tone flat: *the last sheaf's in and the barley's high*.

Five of them. Six.

Vane had his pistols out.

They were a matched pair of Rust Coast work, long-barrelled and silver-chased, and he had named them *Last Rites* and *Second Opinion* in a waterfront bar a very long time ago, for reasons that had seemed extremely funny at the time. They were loaded, as they were always loaded now, with glass shot: little pellets of Aether-Glass cast by a woman in Cantabile who charged a sovereign a pellet and was worth every copper. The Cacophony did not like glass. Nobody knew why. Some said it was the ring; some said glass was the Spires' blood and the Cacophony were the Spires' enemies. Vane had never much cared why, so long as it worked.

He was laughing. He couldn't stop.

He knew exactly how he looked. He had seen himself do it in a mirror once, in a brothel in Port Cantabile during the Screaming Year, when the first hounds came in off the harbor and took the girl in the next room, and he had laughed so hard he couldn't breathe. It wasn't bravery. It wasn't madness either, not quite. It was just what his body did when it was more frightened than it could hold. The laughter came up out of him like water through a split hull, and there was nothing to do but keep bailing.

*Last Rites* went off like the end of the world.

In that hushed room the shot was a physical blow. Vane felt it in his teeth and saw the old men at the hearth fold over with their hands on their ears. The glass shot took the nearest hound through its shimmering flank, and the hound came apart like a dropped tray of glasses played backward, a spray of bright falling notes and a shriek that was a dozen stolen voices shrieking at once. The mouths along its back went on singing for a moment after the rest of it was gone.

Every other hound in the room turned toward the gunshot.

And toward the laughter.

Which was — Vane understood, with a clarity that was almost peaceful — the loudest remaining sound in the Boar & Dulcimer.

Davin Hale was still singing. The hounds had reached him first. They were around him now like dogs around a fallen stag, and he stood in the middle of them with the Cantor's hand still flat on his chest, and he was singing. But the song was changing. It was rising in pitch, and thinning, and breaking into grains, the way a voice breaks up at the far end of a speaking-tube. And Davin Hale was changing with it. The edges of him were going soft. Grainy. Like a charcoal drawing someone was smudging with a thumb. Vane could see the hearth through his shoulder.

The Cantor had not let go.

Kaelen Vance reached her in two strides and took her round the waist with his right arm and pulled her bodily away, and she fought him — silently, furiously — and the hound at Davin's left side turned its eyeless head toward the movement.

The glass man tore the felt sleeve off his left arm with his teeth.

The sound of the glass filled the room like a struck bell. It was so pure and so loud and so high that Vane's laughter caught in his throat. Every hound in the tavern flinched. Every one of them — it was like watching a pack of real dogs cringe from a raised whip — flattened itself and shrank from that clean terrible ringing, and the mouths along their backs clamped shut all together.

Kaelen Vance put Lyra Ashgrove behind him and walked into them.

He did not fight like a soldier. He fought like a man breaking up a wall. His glass fist went into the nearest hound's flank and through it, and where it went through, the hound's jittering substance fell apart into a spray of dying sound — a laugh, a creak, a scrap of hymn — and the glass arm drank it. Vane saw it drink. The ringing of the arm dipped for an instant, as if swallowing, and then came back stronger. Fuller. As if the thing had fed. The glass man hit another one, and another. His face was perfectly blank. He looked like a man chopping wood.

*Second Opinion* went off. Another hound came apart in a shower of glass-bright notes.

Vane realized he was still laughing, and that two of the hounds were coming for him across the felt tables, low and fast. And behind the bar, where the Cantor had got to — somehow, in the middle of all this, she had got the dye-works women and two small children behind the bar — there was a knot of people making no sound at all. The Cantor was kneeling in the middle of them with two children's faces pressed into her chest and her wrapped hand held gently, gently, over a third child's mouth, and her eyes were closed.

She couldn't sing. She was doing something else. She was being the quietest thing in the room.

And he, Vane, was the loudest.

He could have stopped. He thought about it afterward, a great deal. It would have been easy. He had emptied both pistols and there was no time to reload. He could have gone quiet and still, and let the two hounds cast about for the next loudest thing, which would have been — he glanced — a child behind the bar, who was beginning to whimper.

He threw back his head and laughed.

Loud. Deliberately. The biggest, ugliest, most carrying laugh he had, a quarterdeck laugh, a laugh for rounding Cape Dissonance in a full gale. It hurt his chest. It filled the room. Both hounds turned from the bar and came for him, and he laughed right into their mouths as they came, and he was very frightened, and none of it was funny at all.

The first one reached his boots. He felt it the way you feel the moment before you fall asleep: a lurch, a loosening, the sound of his own laughter going grainy in his ears. Then a pale blue fist came down through the middle of it, and it burst into bright falling noise, and the second one broke and fled up the chimney, singing *The Last Sheaf* in Davin Hale's voice all the way up and out over the square and away across the rooftops toward the Hedge.

Then there was nothing in the Boar & Dulcimer but people breathing.

---

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Davin Hale's boots stood on the flags by the hearth, side by side, still laced. Nothing else. Not his jerkin. Not his hat. The boots, and a leather mug on its side, and a patch of hearthstone where the ash had gone dead and flat, and would not, Vane knew from long experience, make a sound when you stepped on it for a day or two.

Someone was weeping. Above a hand. Nobody stopped them.

Kaelen Vance stood in the middle of the room with his left arm hanging bare at his side, blazing and ringing, and the ringing was different now. Deeper. It had a fullness in it that it had not had when he came in. He was looking down at the arm as though it belonged to someone else, someone he did not like very much.

Vane's hands were shaking so badly he could not get his powder-horn open. He put the pistols down on the felt and sat on them.

The Legionnaires came eventually: four of them in a row, bell-helms lifted, clanking softly across the square. They were too late, as Legionnaires always were, and a Synod clerk followed them with a ledger, looked at the boots, and wrote for some time. Vane read it upside down as the man passed: *Hale, D. Carter. Dissonant: self-inflicted. No further action.*

Then Hetty Bram, who had not said a word all night, went to the black board by the door. She took a rag and wiped the rules off it, slowly, line by line, until the board was clean. Then she picked up the chalk.

NO SINGING.
NO TOASTS.
NO DICE.
NO WEEPING ABOVE A HAND.

Her hand did not shake at all.

---

The Cantor came and sat down across from him at last, the glass man beside her. She looked at him for a long time with that counting look.

"You drew them off the children," she whispered.

"I laugh when I'm frightened, Cantor. It's a medical condition. I've had it looked at."

"You did it on purpose. After the first one."

He picked up his mug, found it empty, and set it down again.

"Eight days," he said to the glass man. "Three berths. And you come aboard for the loading. Your — that." He nodded at the arm. "They didn't like that. I'd sleep better with it on deck. Call it ballast."

"I'm not coming," said Kaelen Vance.

Vane looked at him. At the blank face, and the arm, and the way he stood between the Cantor and the rest of the world without seeming to know he was doing it.

"No," he said. "You wouldn't be."

He knew the look. He had seen it once in a shaving-mirror, a long time ago, on a morning off the Rust Coast before a tow went wrong.

---

It was full dark when he climbed back up the mast-ladder. Mags met him at the top with a lantern and read the whole evening off his face in one look, and signed a single word — *singing?* — and he signed *yes*, and she asked nothing else. Pennick was still asleep on the lift-tanks with the tuning-hammer in his fist. The tanks had stopped humming sour. Somewhere in the night the boy had found the note.

Vane went down into the hold.

He always did, at night. He didn't know why. He took the lantern and climbed down the ladder and stood among the twelve crates in the smell of pine and straw and cold glass, and he listened, the way a man might go and stand at a ship's rail on the anniversary of something.

The murmur rose around him. The man counting. The woman giving directions. The laughter. Tonight he leaned in, the way he'd sworn he wouldn't, and put his ear to the rough pine of the nearest crate.

A child. A little girl, by the sound of her. Very close.

*—it's your turn, it's your turn, you have to count, you can't peek—*

And then, in a different voice, a smaller one, carefully:

*—one. Two. Three. Four—*

He took his ear away from the crate before it got to ten.

He stood in the hold for a while with the lantern. Then he sat down on the bottom rung of the ladder, and reached into his coat, and took out the bottle.

It was a small thing of blue glass, sealed with red wax and a paper label in a Cantabile scribe's hand. A dealer on the Salt Stair had sold it to him in the spring, the week before the rumor started, for an absurd amount of money. *Genuine last words*, the dealer had said. *Recovered from the trench off Cape Dissonance. First mate of the* Wave-Cutter*, Captain. I thought you'd want it.* The label said, in small careful letters: VESPER.

It was a fake. It was obviously a fake. There were no last words in the trench; nobody had gone down with a bottle; the dealer had seen a drunk captain with money and made up a name. Vane knew that. He had known it when he paid.

He had never unstopped it. If it was a fake, he would lose the one thing it had actually sold him, which was not a voice. And if it wasn't—

He turned it over in his hands, in the lantern-light, among the murmuring crates.

"Forty-eight," he told the dark, the way he did most nights.

The dark, as usual, had nothing to say to that. But all around him, in the straw and the pine and the cold glass, the crates went on talking quietly among themselves, the way they always did, as if he were not there at all.
