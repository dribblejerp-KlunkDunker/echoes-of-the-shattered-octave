## CHAPTER FIVE
### The Quarry

> *An engram is a harmonic template and possesses no identity. Speech emitted by a damaged engram is to be disregarded and is not to be recorded.*
>
> — Catalogue Circular 12, *On the Maintenance of Carillon Engrams*

*The Oakhaven quarry. Hearthday, the middle watch.*

---

The quarry gate opened for Consortium glass the same way the Hedge gate did.

Kaelen held the disc up to the grille, and the clerk in the gatehouse looked at it, and at him, and for a long moment at the felt sleeve, and pulled the lever. Two Legionnaires stood on either side of the gate with their bells tilted to the sky, and he did not so much walk between them as through the murmur they made, like walking through a room where two people are talking in their sleep.

He had come straight from the Boar & Dulcimer. There was still a smear of something on the felt of his sleeve that he did not look at. The glass beneath it was ringing deeper than it had in years: a full round note with a new warmth at the bottom of it, like a bell recast heavier, and when he flexed the fingers he could feel the fullness in them, like the ache of a limb after a meal. He had not measured the tideline. He would measure it at dawn. He already knew.

The Oakhaven quarry had been a hole in the ground for two hundred years before the Consortium came: a stepped bowl of grey stone a quarter-mile across, from which the Reach had cut its millstones and its chapel steps and the lintels of half the houses in Oakhaven. Kaelen had swum in its flooded bottom as a boy, in the summers, with Lyra, and the water had been so cold it made your teeth sing.

The water was gone. In its place, at the bottom of the bowl, stood the headframe.

It was smaller than the Siphon Foundry. That was the first thing he noticed, and it did not help. It was iron, and it was ribbed, and it was lit from below — a green-gold light coming up out of the shaft the way light comes up out of a well that someone has dropped a lamp into — and around it, on the stepped terraces of the old quarry, stood everything he remembered. The cable-drums. The tanker bays. The glass-channels cut into the stone. The gantries, and the long low sheds of the depots, and a censer-platform, empty, draped in white. They were expecting a Church officiant. Above it all, black against the stars, rose the mooring mast, and at the mast rode the little smuggler's ship with no colors, a single lantern burning low in her hold.

He did not stop to look at it. He walked down the terraces at an engineer's pace, looking at the things an engineer would look at, while the arm under the felt listened to the shaft and to the thing breathing at the bottom of it.

---

"Engineer Vance?"

The young man came up the last flight of steps from the headframe at a run and stopped two steps below him, and stood there with his mouth open as if Kaelen were something out of a book. Twenty-four, perhaps. Consortium drab, with a junior's single green bar on the collar. A face full of freckles and sleeplessness, and ink on his thumb.

"Tobiah Marsh, sir. Junior engineer, night shift. The Factor said you might come, any hour, she said — I didn't think—" He stopped. He swallowed. Then, visibly unable to help himself, he dug into the satchel at his hip and brought out a small battered book with a broken spine and held it out in both hands. "I'm sorry. I know it's — I studied it. At the Conservatory. We all did. It's why I—" He went red to the ears. "Would you sign it, sir?"

*On the Calibration of Resonant Bores*, said the spine, in gilt worn nearly away. *K. Vance.*

Kaelen looked at it for a long moment.

He took it in his right hand. The pages were soft with thumbing. Whole paragraphs had been underlined in two colors of ink, and the margins were full of a small eager hand: *yes!!* — *but see ch. 4* — *hold beneath the failure-pitch — ALWAYS BENEATH*. On the flyleaf the boy had written his own name, and beneath it the year: *409 SR*. The year the book was printed. He would have been fourteen.

Kaelen took the pencil the boy held out and signed his name under the boy's. His hand was quite steady. It was always steady. That was what he had instead.

"Thank you, sir," said Tobiah Marsh. "Thank you. You don't know—" He put the book away as though it were made of glass. "Will you want to go down? To the face? The Factor said you'd want the readings."

"I want the readings," said Kaelen.

---

The night-shift office was a plank shed against the foot of the headframe, full of the tick of instruments and the smell of lamp oil and wet slate. Tobiah laid the readings out on the drawing-table with the eagerness of a boy showing his father his schoolwork. Pitch against depth. Torque against time. The resonance envelope of the bore-head, plotted every quarter-hour for a month in the same small careful hand, and running through all of it like a crack through a plate, the slippage.

"It's worst near the face," Tobiah said. "The Sequence assumes a static failure-pitch, sir — you know that better than anyone — but the Root's isn't static. It shifts. Like it's—" He hesitated. "It'll sound stupid."

"Say it."

"Like it's moving in its sleep." He did not look up from the charts. "The last time a head shattered, I was at the rail. The pitch went up a whole tone in about four heartbeats, like when you touch someone who's sleeping and they flinch. And then the head went. And the crew on the platform—" He stopped. "Marten, and Old Coyle, and the Tanner twins. I'd been drinking with the Tanner boys the night before."

Kaelen said nothing. There was nothing to say that the boy would believe.

"The Organum's revisions are brilliant, though," Tobiah said, rallying. "Revision Seven especially. The unison hold will pin the Root's pitch exactly. It won't be able to shift. I've never seen anything like it." He brightened. "And the Factor runs the tightest works in the Reach. She walks the Legion every morning before the day shift, with her lenses, and listens to every one of them. They keep everything, you know. Everything they've seen and heard. She can hear it all back like reading a ledger. Nobody gets away with anything on her terraces." He said it with real pride. "Oh — and the kit came up yesterday. For Stage Nine. Would you like to see it?"

"Yes," said Kaelen.

It lay in a locked cabinet at the back of the shed, in a case of black leather lined with white velvet, with the Church's gold sunburst tooled on the lid and, beneath it, very small, the Arbiterium's scales. Tobiah unlocked it with a key on a chain around his neck, lifted the lid, and turned the case to the lamp.

A tuning collar. Brass, finely made, a ring the width of a woman's throat, with four small tuning pegs at the quarters and two eyelets at the front. A coil of copper wire, very fine, wound on an ivory spool. A long silver needle, curved like a new moon, lying in its own fitted groove in the velvet.

Kaelen did not touch it. He did not need to. He had seen forty.

"My mother used to take me to hear the Sky Choir," Tobiah said softly, looking at the collar. "When I was small. Before the Fracture. We'd stand on the Pilgrims' Stair at dawn, and you could hear them all the way down the mountain. I thought it was the most beautiful thing in the world." He touched the edge of the case with one ink-stained finger. "They say it's a great honor, for the Cantor. To be chosen for a Rite. Do you think she knows yet, sir?"

"She knows," said Kaelen.

---

The cage went down on its cable in the dark — a hundred feet, two hundred — the stone walls of the shaft sliding past close enough to touch, lit by the single lamp Tobiah held and by the green glow coming up from below. At three hundred feet the stone gave way to clay, packed grey and sweating. At four hundred, the clay gave way to something else.

Root.

He had seen it on the drawing, a twisting mass in green ink, and the drawing had not prepared him. The shaft opened out below the clay into a chamber the size of the Chapel of the Pure Note, and the chamber had not been dug. It had grown. Its walls were root — a single root, or the flank of one, so vast that its curve was the curve of the chamber, rising out of the dark below and away into the dark above. Grey-brown, ridged like bark, but warm, and wet, and veined: every ridge threaded with glowing lines of green-gold glass, like the veins on the back of a hand held up to a lamp. The veins pulsed. Slowly. In, and out.

The air was thick with the smell of sap, green and sweet and overwhelming, and of hot iron, and it was full of the grinding.

The Carrow Bore hung in the middle of it on its chains. Its head was buried in the Root to the collar, turning, turning, and where it went in, the Root bled. Green-gold glass welled out of the wound in a slow thick stream and ran down into the channels cut in the platform floor, and along the channels to the pumps.

There were men at the pumps. A dozen of them, in grey smocks, working the long iron handles in pairs, up and down, with a rhythm so perfect it looked like clockwork. None of them looked up when the cage arrived. None of them looked at anything.

"Catalogue labor," said Tobiah, following his eyes. His voice had gone a little flat. "They don't need rest. They don't need — much. The Factor says it's a kindness, giving them work. Otherwise they only wander."

Hollows. Kaelen had heard the word. He had never seen them. The man at the nearest pump had a wedding ring on his hand and a scar through one eyebrow and a face as smooth and empty as a pond with nothing in it, and he was working the handle up and down, and his lips were moving. Kaelen could not hear what he was saying. He did not try.

---

He stepped out of the cage onto the platform, and the glass heard the Root.

It came up through his boots and into the arm like cold water climbing a wick. Under the grinding of the bore, under the creak of the pumps, under everything: the breathing. It was not faint anymore. It filled the chamber. In, slow as a tide coming in over flat sand. Out. And inside the breathing — he had not been able to hear this from the clearing, and he had told himself there was nothing to hear — there was something else.

Humming.

Very low, very slow, and very tired. Not a song. The shape of a song, worn down to its bones by a very long time. Like someone humming in their sleep. Like his mother had hummed the winter he was twelve, in the last week of the fever, lying in the big bed in the Governor's house with her eyes closed, humming the same five notes over and over because she could no longer remember the rest.

He had put his hand on the Root before he knew he meant to.

His left hand. The glass.

The humming stopped.

Nothing else in the chamber changed. The bore went on grinding, and the pumps went on pumping, and the glass went on bleeding into the channels. But the humming stopped, the way his mother's had stopped when he came into the room and she opened her eyes — and in the silence where it had been, he felt something turn.

Not toward the bore. Toward him.

He had felt it once before. He had felt it in the Siphon Foundry at the foot of the Dawning Spire, with a tuning fork in his fist and forty girls singing, when a chord ten thousand years wide drew in, and in, and in, until it was the size of a question.

He took his hand off the Root.

"Sir?" Tobiah was staring at the gauge on the platform rail. The needle was swinging, slowly, back down toward its line. "Sir, it just jumped. A whole tone. Just now. Did you see?"

"Slippage," said Kaelen.

His voice was perfectly level. It always was.

---

He made himself do what he had come for.

He walked the platform with Tobiah and let the boy talk, and asked the questions an engineer would ask, and looked where an engineer would look, and the whole time he was reading the bore the way he had once read a hundred bores, listening for the note at which it wished to fail. It did not take long. He had built its grandfather. The Carrow Bore's harmonic lock was housed in a drum of brass and glass on the bore's collar, where the head met the shaft, and in the drum's flank, sealed by a plate and eight bolts, was the phase port: the place where the Sequence's timing entered the machine, and the place where, if you knew how — if you had written the book — it could be made to fail.

A reverse-phase coil, seated in that port at the wrong moment, would throw the lock. The head would seize in the Root. The Sequence would die.

"How many coils have you got?" he asked, looking at the drum. "Reverse-phase. For the slippage."

"Two left, sir. They burn out fast down here. They're in Depot Three, by the tailings." Tobiah hesitated. "Do you think we'll need them, for the casing?"

"I think," said Kaelen, "you'll need everything you have."

---

He left the boy at the headframe with the readings and a promise to come back, and walked up the terraces in the dark toward the tailings.

Depot Three was the last shed on the lowest terrace, a long low building of tarred planks with its back to the slag-slope, where the waste stone of two centuries tumbled away to a pit of grey rubble and old green water. There was no one at the door. The Consortium did not waste guards on spare parts. It had not wasted a lock, either. He went in.

The coils lay at the back on a rack, in straw: two squat drums of copper and glass, each the size of a hatbox, stencilled RPC-II, their glass windows dark. He knelt and lifted one out of the straw with his right hand to feel its weight. Heavy. But a man could carry it. It could be carried in a toolbag. It could be carried down a shaft in a cage, among an engineer's instruments, by an engineer the Consortium had begged to come and supervise.

Behind him, the door creaked.

He did not turn at once. He set the coil back into its straw, carefully, and stood, and then he turned.

The Legionnaire stood in the doorway with the night behind it. Its bell was tilted downward now, toward him, and the grille at its mouth was worked in a pattern of interlaced reeds.

He knew that grille. He had walked across Oakhaven beside it three days ago.

*—the blue jug, Mam. Not the white—*

It had followed him down from the headframe. He had not heard it come. He had been listening to something else.

It did not move. It did not need to. In the morning, before the day shift, the Factor would walk her line of Legionnaires with her green lenses and listen to each one, and this one would tell her that the Engineer had gone alone to Depot Three in the middle watch, and knelt, and lifted a reverse-phase coil out of its straw, and weighed it in his hand.

"I'm sorry," Kaelen said.

He said it to the grille. To whoever was behind the grille. It did not seem to hear. It raised its hands — long pewter gauntlets, slowly, with that small sound of oiled joints — and came toward him to take him by the arms.

He took it by the bell instead.

He laid his glass hand flat against the side of the bronze, the way you lay a hand on a struck bell to stop it ringing, and felt the bell try to sound — felt the whole of its thin, fine bronze gather itself for the one enormous note that would have woken every soul in the quarry — and felt the glass drink it. The note went into his hand and up his arm and was gone, swallowed, and afterward the arm rang low and full, like a mouth closing on something.

The bell made no sound at all.

Then he drew back his fist and put it through the grille.

The bronze reeds broke. His knuckles went through into the dark inside the bell and struck something smooth and round and cold, the size of an apple, and it cracked beneath them like ice under a boot.

And the voice came out.

Not murmuring now. Clear. Close. A young woman's voice, very frightened, right there inside the bronze, as if she had been shut in a cupboard in the dark and someone had just opened the door.

"—*Mam?* Mam, where — I can't find it, I can't find the blue jug, I've been looking and looking — where *is* everyone—" A breath that was not a breath. "I'm Tamsin. I'm Tamsin. I'm *Tamsin Reed*. Tell her. Tell Mam I'm Tamsin Reed, I'm—"

And stopped.

The Legionnaire folded at the knees and went down onto the plank floor, slowly, the way a tent comes down when the pole is pulled, and lay there. Inside the broken bell, something small and cold and cracked rolled once against the bronze and was still.

He stood over it for a long time.

Then he reached into the bell with his right hand and found the core in the dark and took it out: a sphere of pale lilac glass the size of an apple, cracked clean through, with a milky fog inside it that was already going thin. It was cold. It was very light. He put it into the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart, and buttoned the pocket.

Then he dragged the Legionnaire out of the back door of Depot Three by its pewter feet, forty yards along the lowest terrace in the dark, to the head of the slag-slope, and set his boot against its breastplate, and pushed. It went over the edge and tumbled end over end down two hundred feet of grey waste stone, bell and gauntlets and pewter plate, and came to rest at the bottom in the old green water of the pit, and sank.

It made no sound. The glass had drunk that too.

---

He walked home through the Hedge gate in the grey before dawn. Old Harrow let him through without a word and did not look at his sleeve, where the felt was torn across the knuckles.

The hound's bed was five paces from his door.

He stood and looked at it in the growing light: a dead dry oval in the wet grass, pressed flat, the size of something long and low. Five paces. He could have stepped from his threshold into it in five strides. Beyond it the clearing lay grey and still, and the stumps at the forest's edge stood in the mist like mourners, and nothing moved.

He went inside and sat on the edge of the cot and took the tailor's tape from the shelf and measured.

Three fingers, from the point of the shoulder along the collarbone.

He wrote it on the wall beneath the others.

Then he took the cracked core out of his coat and held it in his right hand and looked at it. The fog inside was almost gone. When he held it to his ear there was nothing — not a murmur, not a word. Whatever had been in there had said the last thing it had, and it had said it to him.

*Speech emitted by a damaged engram is to be disregarded and is not to be recorded.*

He had read that circular once, years ago, at a desk in the Organum, and turned the page.

"Tamsin Reed," he said aloud, to the hut. So that he would not forget it. He had got into the habit, living alone, of saying things out loud. Nobody heard. That was the point. "Tamsin Reed. I'm sorry."

He set the core on the shelf beside the tape.

And from outside — from the clearing, from the grey mist among the stumps, from somewhere much nearer than the stumps — a voice said, quietly, perfectly:

"*Tamsin Reed.*"

His voice.

His exact voice. The Reach in the vowels, the rasp at the bottom of it, the particular way his own tongue came down on the *R*. Not an echo. It came a full breath after he had finished speaking, the way an answer comes. Then, softer, almost tenderly, as if trying the shape of the words:

"*I'm sorry.*"

Kaelen sat very still on the edge of the cot, with his glass arm ringing low and full in the dim room, and did not move, and did not breathe.

For seven years he had said things out loud in this hut because nobody heard.

He sat in the grey light with the broken glass of Tamsin Reed on the shelf in front of him and understood that, for at least some of those years, somebody had.
