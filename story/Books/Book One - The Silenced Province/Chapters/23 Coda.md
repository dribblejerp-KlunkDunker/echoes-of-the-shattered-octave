## CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE
### Coda

> *For a death with no body, sing the full office anyway. The song is not for the dead. It is for the hole the dead left, and a hole will take a song the way dry ground takes rain.*
>
> — T. Ashgrove, Cantor's notebook, 404 SR

*The clearing by the Mausoleum of Chords. Nullday night.*

---

He watched her come across the clearing with the papers in her hand and he did not get up.

"You've read them," said Kaelen.

She sat down on the moss in front of him — not beside him; in front, where he would have to look at her — and set the slate on her knees, and wrote, and turned it round.

*TELL ME.*

"Lyra—"

*TELL ME. OUT LOUD. ALL OF IT.*

So he did.

It took the better part of an hour, and she never once wrote anything else, and he was grateful for that in a way he could not have explained, because the silence meant he had to keep going.

He told her about the Conservatory, and the rented room over the chandler's, and a young man who had been the cleverest person in the Organum for about four years and had known it. He told her what the Vance Sequence was, and why it was beautiful, and that it was beautiful. He told her about the Foundry: the risers, the censer-chain, the thirty-nine. The girl on the top tier, and her collar, and the ninth morning. He told her that he had gone back up the gantry.

He told her about line ninety-four.

He told her about the question — about the chord drawing in and in until it was the size of a hand on his shoulder, and what it had meant, and the three seconds he had spent being sure, and what he had shouted, and who had answered him.

And then, because she sat there with her ruined throat and her father's papers in her lap and did not look away, he told her the rest of it, which nobody alive had heard.

That he had read the grey folder in the archive at the Malting.

That it was dated eight months before the Fracture. That the object of Stage Nine had never been extraction; that the whole Rite had been built to put a question to a god in a form it could not mistake; that the Arbiterium had expected the Light to let go, and had regarded that as the proof of a method; and that there was a line at the bottom of the third sheet, in a small beautiful hand, saying that Engineer V. was not to be informed, because his ear was more accurate when his conscience was not engaged.

He took the four sheets out of his shirt and put them on the moss between them.

"I was used," said Kaelen. "That's what those say. I've had them since yesterday morning and I haven't shown them to anybody, and I want you to understand why, because it isn't modesty." He looked at his own hands. "When I read them, the first thing I felt was relief. It came up in me like water. *It wasn't me. It was never me. A better man in my place would have done exactly the same.*" He swallowed. "And Coralie is still dead. Fifteen years old, second riser, left shoulder, and she used to make jokes under her hem with her hands because the wire wouldn't let her talk. And she gets nothing out of my relief. Nobody gets anything out of it except me. So I decided I wasn't entitled to it, and I put it inside my shirt, and I was going to burn it."

Lyra picked up the four sheets.

She read them by the shuttered lamp, slowly, the way she read everything. When she got to the line about his conscience, her hand closed on the edge of the paper hard enough to crease it.

Then she put them down.

And she wrote, and her hand was shaking so badly that the letters came out like a child's:

*THERE IS SOMETHING ELSE. SAY IT.*

Kaelen looked at her.

"In the archive," he said, "something spoke to me out of the glass."

---

He told her about Vaelen, too. All of it: two pieces of one Spire are one thing in two places; seven years of listening; a voice that had heard every word Kaelen Vance had said aloud in an empty room since the year the sky went grey.

"He's heard me talk to the hut," he said. "Every night. The second winter, when I — he's heard the whole of it. And he never spoke, because he said it would have been an intrusion." He laughed, once, with no humour in it at all. "That's the man. That's exactly the man. He will unmake the world and he will be *polite* about it."

*WHAT DOES HE WANT?*

"He wants me to help him let them stop." Kaelen looked up at the willow-tops. "And here is the thing I have to say to you, Lyra, because if I don't say it tonight I never will. I stood in that archive with the Anchor of Memory underneath me trying to get to the end of a list it can't finish, and a man was telling me that eight of them have been in pain since before there were people, and that the term of the thing ran out in the year the Light broke, and that nobody came.

"And I didn't have an argument."

She went very still.

"I don't now," he said. "I've been looking for one for two days. The Root under Oakhaven is still singing with a hole in it, right now, badly, and it will go on singing badly for as long as it can hold out, because that's what it agreed to do ten thousand years ago when somebody promised it that we'd learn to take over." He made himself say it. "I think they are owed. I think he's right about what they're owed, and I think he is entirely wrong about how it ends, and the distance between those two things is the only ground I have to stand on, and it is not very wide. So when you ask me what I am — I am a man who built the drill, and the only person on this continent who understands what the man at the other end of it wants, and some nights that feels less like opposition than like being the same thing twice."

The lamp hissed.

"That's everything," said Kaelen Vance. "That's the whole account. There isn't any more."

---

Lyra Ashgrove opened her mouth to reach for the chalk.

And her voice came back.

It came back the way a stream comes back after a dry month: not with the first push, and then suddenly, wrongly, in the wrong place. What came out of her was a cracked, ruined, barely-pitched thing, two tones lower than it had been a fortnight ago and furred like a split reed, and it was the first sound she had made in six days.

"I breathed on your arm," she said.

Kaelen's head came up.

"In my kitchen. On Rootday." Every word cost her something visible. "I had nothing left — I'd spent it all on a boy's hands — and I put my mouth on the glass and breathed the Root note into it because it was the only thing I had, and you sat in my father's house and let me, and you had *this* in your head the whole time."

"Yes."

"I sang in a procession with a wire in my throat for you."

"Yes."

"I was going to go down that shaft and hold a bore's pitch and I didn't know *what I was holding it for*—"

"You did," he said. "I told you what Stage Nine was. I told you everything I knew about the Rite. What I didn't tell you was what I was."

"That's the same thing!" It tore; she went on through the tearing. "That *is* the same thing, Kaelen. I chose you. I chose you on Rootday at my own table and I chose you in the Hollow and I chose you on the line over Oakhaven, and every single time I was choosing a man who was missing a piece, and you knew which piece, and you let me do it." She was crying now, which she had not done in front of anyone since her father, and she did not stop talking. "I would have forgiven you. Do you understand that? On Rootday I would have forgiven you in about four minutes, because I'm a Cantor and that is what we are *for*. I can't now. Not because of the Spire. Because you let me pick you up without telling me how heavy you were."

Kaelen sat with his back against the tree.

"I know," he said.

"Don't you *dare* be calm about it—"

"I'm not calm. I've got no idea what my face is doing." He said it quite steadily. "You're right. All of it's right. I'm not going to argue a word of it, and I'm not going to apologise either, because an apology is a thing you give somebody so they'll stop, and I don't want you to stop. You're the only person who's said any of this to me in seven years and most of what I know about what I am I learned in the last four minutes."

Lyra put her head down on her knees.

They stayed like that for a long time, two feet apart, in a clearing in the Spectral Woods with an eleven-hundred-year-old Veil-house rotting in the trees behind them.

"I hate that it told me first," she said at last, into her knees. "That's the part I can't — it *should have been you*, and instead it was a thing in a fog wearing your voice, and it was kind about it. It was kind, Kaelen. It let me sit down."

She lifted her head.

"And I'm going to kill it tonight," she said.

---

Here is what the Wearer was, and what it was not.

It was not clever. It had no plan beyond its appetite, and its appetite had one shape: a voice, taken whole, and the sounds around that voice, taken with it. It could not read. It could not guess. Everything it knew, it knew because somebody had said it out loud where it could hear.

But it learned, and it kept what it learned forever, and it had been learning Lyra Ashgrove for two years.

It knew her note. It knew her torn voice and her whole one. It knew the falling fifth, and Pim's whistle, and eleven hymns, and the particular hitch her breath made when she was about to cry. It had followed her note four miles out of a drowning town, and then nine more to a drowned city, and then nine again through the deep woods to a clearing by the Mausoleum of Chords, because once it has your note it can find you anywhere, and because she had never once, in forty-one nights, told it to stop coming.

It knew everything about her except one thing, which is the thing nobody can learn by listening: what she would do.

---

She set it up like a procedure, because that was how you did a thing you could not afford to feel.

"It won't come where people are," she told them, in the bow's shadow, in that ruined voice, with Vane and Sable and Kaelen and Tobiah around her and Mags watching her mouth. "It never has. In two years it has never crossed the Hedge, or come through the culvert, or set foot in the Hollow, and at the Cloister it stood in the fog and would not come into the room. It wants me *alone*. That's the whole of its nature. So I am going to be alone, out there, at the edge of the trees, and all of you are going to be back here where it can see that you are."

"And when it comes?" said Vane.

"It won't come straight away. It'll circle, and it'll use voices, and it will do everything it can to make me walk further out." She looked at Kaelen. "You get one strike. You get it when it's inside the ring of lamps and not before, because if it sees you it will simply go away and come back in a week when I'm asleep."

"Lyra," said Sable, "may I say the obvious thing."

"No."

"You are the bait, with no voice to speak of, at night, in a wood, against the thing that has killed four people we know of—"

"I'm not bait," said Lyra. "Bait doesn't sing."

---

She walked out past the last lamp at the second hour and stood in the moss between two silver willows with her hands at her sides.

And she sang her father's funeral.

She had never sung it. That was the thing she had carried for two years, under everything, under the Hedge and the stone and the forty-one nights: Tobin Ashgrove had gone into the dark on a spring morning in the Pellows' yard and there had been nothing left to bury but a lyre, and a Cantor does not sing the office over a stile. She had sung four hundred people down in her life. Every one of them had got the full office — the six movements, the naming, the long low close that goes under the floor — and the only one she had never given it to was her own.

It was the oldest thing she knew. It went back past the Synod and past the Reckoning and probably past the Compact itself, and it was in five parts and one coda, and it was not a beautiful song. It was a working song, the way a plough is a working tool. You sang it to make the hole the right shape.

She sang it in a voice like a torn sheet, in the dark, badly, at full volume.

*Tobin Ashgrove of the orchard rows. Son of Mara. Cantor of Oakhaven.*

The wood went quiet.

*He sang the births. He sang the burials. He hummed off-key on the fourth bar and he never once learned better.*

She could hear her own voice going out between the trunks and not coming back, the way sound goes in deep woods, and she kept singing, and her throat opened something it should not have opened and she tasted iron and kept going.

*He went out into the yard so the hounds would follow him and not the house. He was fifty-six years old. He was afraid of ladders and nothing else.*

And somewhere out in the dark, perfectly in tune, her father's voice began to sing the second part.

---

It knew the office. Of course it knew the office; it had eaten a Cantor.

It came in on the harmony line the way Tobin Ashgrove had come in on it four hundred times at four hundred gravesides, a fifth below, warm and hoarse and a little late on the entries the way he always was, and for eight bars Lyra Ashgrove and the thing that had eaten her father sang his funeral together in the dark.

That was the worst eight bars of her life, and she would have them for the rest of it, and some part of her — the part she would not forgive, ever, not until she was very old — was glad of them.

Then she saw it.

It came between the trunks at thirty feet: her father's height, her father's stoop, the long-backed forward lean of forty years over reed-rows, with the willow-dark showing straight through it and its edges crawling like heat off a road. It had no face. It had the place where a face was not, rippling, and the voice came out of that.

*"Lark,"* it said, between the phrases. *"Oh, lark. You never sang it for me."*

"I know," said Lyra, and kept singing.

*"I waited."*

Third part. The naming. She lifted her chin and sang her father's name into the dark four times, which is what the naming is, and the thing came closer with every one, because it could not help it, because that was what it was: a mouth that goes toward a voice.

Twenty feet. Fifteen.

*"Come out of the lamps, lark,"* it said, in her own whole voice, gently. *"I can't come in there. Come out to me and I'll sing the rest with you. You can't do the close on your own, you know you can't, it's a two-voice part—"*

It was true. That was the obscenity of it. The close of the office is two voices, and she had not had a second voice since the Pellow farm, and the thing in the trees knew it because it had been the second voice.

Ten feet.

"I know it's a two-voice part," said Lyra Ashgrove.

And she sang the coda alone.

---

It came apart four feet in front of her.

Kaelen came out of the dark behind it at a dead run with his left arm bare and blazing and his ribs grinding and he hit it with everything he had left, and nine feet of bronze came out of the dark one second behind him and hit it from the other side, because DEFEND THE SINGERS is four words long and has no clause in it for what the thing in front of you is wearing.

The Wearer burst.

It did not die like the hounds in the tavern, in a spray of bright falling notes. It had eaten a Cantor and four others and two years of a woman's nights, and when the glass went through it, all of that came out at once, in the space of about three seconds, in a wall of sound that knocked Tobiah Marsh off his feet at forty yards.

Davin Hale, singing the fourth line of the harvest home.

Old Tam, going up for the high note he never once reached.

Ada Pellow, saying *oh, it's easy, come on, it's easy from this side* in the voice of a woman who has just discovered otherwise.

A boy whistling a falling fifth with too much air in it.

Lyra's own voice, whole and warm and twenty-nine years old, saying *I've shown him how to hold his hands out stiff, Da, so they won't touch anything.*

And under all of it, rising up through it as the rest came apart, her father.

Not the voice it had worn. The one underneath. Tobin Ashgrove at the end, in a farmyard in the spring, singing the calling-song at the top of his lungs and walking away from a house with a sick child in it, and the song getting ragged as the thing came, and then not ragged — *louder* — because he had worked out, in the last four seconds of his life, that the louder he was the further away they would be from the door.

He was still singing when it took him.

That is what came out of the Wearer in the clearing by the Mausoleum of Chords, and Lyra Ashgrove stood four feet away with her hands at her sides and let every bit of it go through her, and did not cover her ears.

The last of it was one word, in her father's voice, from about the height of her shoulder, very close, almost surprised:

*"Lark—"*

And then the clearing was quiet, and there was nothing in the moss in front of her at all.

---

She sat down.

Not gracefully; her legs stopped and she went down on them in the wet moss. Kaelen got to her and did not touch her and knelt a yard away with his arm ringing low and full — it had fed; she heard it feed; it was the fullest she had ever heard it — and said her name twice.

"That's it," said Lyra.

"Lyra—"

"That's it. That's the end of him." She was looking at the empty place in the moss. "That was the last of his voice in the world, and it was in *that*, and I've just put it out." Her face did something she had no control over. "I've been going out to the Hedge for forty-one nights to listen to a monster because it was the only place my father still existed, and I knew exactly what it was the whole time, and tonight I killed it on purpose, and now there's nowhere."

Kaelen said nothing at all, which was, for once, precisely right.

"Say something," she said.

"No."

"*Kaelen.*"

"You don't want something said," he said. "You want somebody to sit here. I can do that one."

So he sat there, a yard away in the wet moss, with a nine-foot automaton standing over the pair of them holding a child's red cloak, and the lamps burning down, and the ship creaking in the dark, and four hundred and thirty-two people murmuring in her hold.

And at some point before dawn Lyra Ashgrove put her hand out sideways, without looking, and found his good one, and did not let go of it, and did not forgive him either.

Both of those things were true at the same time, and would be for a long while yet.
