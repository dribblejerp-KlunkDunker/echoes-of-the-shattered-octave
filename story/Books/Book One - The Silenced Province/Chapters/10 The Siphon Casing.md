## CHAPTER TEN
### The Siphon Casing

> *STAGE 9 — UNISON HOLD. On failure of the hold, for any cause, the Sequence is not to be halted. Slippage is to be absorbed by the source.*
>
> — Vance Sequence, Rev. 7, marginal note in an unknown hand

*The Root-vault, four hundred feet beneath the Oakhaven quarry. Hearthday, noon.*

---

His hands were shaking.

He knelt on the boards with the needle in his fingers and looked at them as though they belonged to someone else, which in a way they did; they were the hands that had signed a procurement order without reading it, and drawn the third revision of a calibration sequence in a rented room at twenty-four, and held a bulkhead against a flood of glass while three boys got into a drainage conduit. They had never once, in all of that, shaken.

Lyra was looking at him.

She could not speak; she had not spoken in five days; and she could not have been heard in any case over the grinding of the bore ten feet away. So she did the only thing left to her. She turned her head a little to the side, and lifted her chin, and offered him her throat.

*Get on with it.*

He got on with it.

"Closer, Engineer," said Ignatius Crane, from the catwalk. "I must be able to see."

Kaelen shifted a half-pace on his knees, angling his shoulder so that the Inquisitor had a clear view of the collar, and the needle, and the ivory spool — and not, if the light stayed where it was, of the place beneath the jaw where his left hand would be. The glass hand. He held it flat against the side of her neck, thumb along the line of the muscle, the way the Tuning Men had done it. The glass was cold. She flinched and made herself still.

He set the collar around her throat first, as the rubric required. Brass, cool, the width of her neck; the tuning pegs at the quarters; the two eyelets at the front. He drew it to the second notch, which was one notch looser than it should have been, and buckled it there.

Then he took the wire on the needle.

The Rite called for the needle to go in above the collar, at the notch of the throat, angled up and back into the fold of the voice itself — you learned the anatomy in the second year at the Conservatory, in a room with a sheep's larynx on a tray — so that the wire lay along the vibrating edge and could be tuned, and so that every breath after that came out at the one pitch and no other. That was the Rite. He had signed for forty collars and forty spools and forty needles, in one line on one order, among two hundred.

Kaelen put the needle in a fold of skin.

He did it fast, because slow would look wrong: caught a pinch of skin along the side of her throat under the shadow of her jaw with the glass thumb, drove the needle through it, brought it out, and drew the copper wire after it with a little jerk exactly like the one a Tuning Man would use to seat the wire on the fold. Lyra's whole body went rigid. Her hands closed on the edges of the stool until the knuckles went white. She did not make a sound, because she could not, and because she had spent four days learning not to.

Blood came at once. It welled up along the wire and ran down under the brass and made a bright line on the white wool of the shift.

*Good*, Kaelen thought, with the sick clarity of a man doing arithmetic. *It has to bleed.*

He knotted the wire at the left eyelet, and carried it across, and knotted it at the right, so that it lay in a shallow bight against her throat looking for all the world like a wire seated in a fold. He took the tuning key from the case and set the pegs — one, two, three, four — turning each a half-turn, and with every turn the wire tightened against the skin, and Lyra's eyes watered and did not close.

The Inquisitor came down the ladder from the catwalk.

He came all the way to the platform, and crouched, and looked at the collar from a foot away with his hands on his knees and the censer's chain wrapped around his fist. Kaelen did not move. He could hear his own arm ringing under the felt, high and fast.

"Blood," said Ignatius. He sounded satisfied. "Good. It should bleed a little at the seating. The Foundry girls used to bleed for days." He straightened. "Speak, daughter."

Lyra's eyes flicked to Kaelen's for the length of a heartbeat.

Then she opened her mouth, and what came out was not speech. It was a sound pressed flat, every syllable ironed onto one pitch, no rise and no fall, the way a voice comes out of a throat that has been tuned like a string — the way Kaelen had heard forty voices come out for three hundred days.

"I. Am. Fortunate."

One hundred and seventy-four, all the way through. Not a hair off.

Kaelen had to put the heel of his right hand against the boards for a moment.

"She is," said Ignatius Crane, pleased, and blessed her with two fingers, and went back up the ladder.

---

The Hold began at noon.

Tobiah Marsh called the mark from the gauge platform — "Head at one span! One span and holding!" — and the bore-master brought the chains down another notch, and the grinding changed its note, and up on the catwalk the Inquisitor lifted the censer.

"One note," he said. "One law. One hand."

The chain creaked.

And Lyra Ashgrove, sitting on a stool on a platform four hundred feet under Oakhaven in a white shift with blood on the collar, began to sing.

One hundred and seventy-four. Clear, and hoarse, and absolutely steady.

Kaelen stood at the calibration rail with his toolbag at his feet and listened to her do it, and understood two things at once: that she was very good — better than any of the Foundry girls had been, because she was not a wired instrument but a Cantor, and a Cantor can hold a note the way a mason holds a plumb-line — and that she had four hours of it ahead of her with a torn throat and a wire through her skin, and that she would not stop, and that this was somehow worse.

The Root heard her in the first minute.

He felt it in the arm before the gauges saw it. The vast slow breathing under everything — the in, and the out, and the five-note lullaby stumbling around and around — checked. Hesitated. And then, very slowly, it began to move.

"Pitch coming up," said Tobiah, at the gauges, delighted. "It's coming up to her, sir. It's — gods, look at it — it's matching."

"Mark it," said the Factor from the rail, and wrote in her own book.

The lullaby stopped.

Kaelen stood at the rail with his hand on the cold iron and felt the thing in the stone stop singing to whatever it had been singing to for ten thousand years, and turn, and listen to the one small human voice holding its note for it. Holding it *steady*. Holding it *up*.

And he thought, with a horror that came up out of his stomach like cold water: *Oh. Oh, no. That's what this is.*

---

He had read Revision Seven four times.

He had read it in the Greenhall in front of the Factor, and twice at the burner's hut with a lamp and a rule, and once in the night-shift office with Tobiah's charts beside it, and he had read it as an engineer reads: for the load paths, for the failure modes, for the assumptions hidden in the arithmetic. He had noted where they had improved his mathematics. He had noted the small pair of scales in the margin, and not understood it.

He had noted, and not understood, the little line at the bottom of Stage Nine in the same small beautiful hand:

*On failure of the hold, for any cause, the Sequence is not to be halted. Slippage is to be absorbed by the source.*

He had read that as a note about the machine. Every engineer writes notes like that. *If the buffer fails, dump the load into the flywheel.* It had not occurred to him — it had not once occurred to him in five days — to ask what the *source* was in a stage whose source was a person.

He looked at Lyra on her stool, with the wire in her throat and the blood on her shift, singing.

If the hold failed, the Sequence would not stop. The slippage — the whole shuddering harmonic recoil of a bore in a living casing — would go into the source. Into her.

She was not the hand that steadied the patient.

She was the fuse.

And that was the small thing, the engineering thing, the footnote. The great thing was standing in the room with him, and he could feel it through his arm, and it was this: that no one had needed a Cantor to drill a hole. Three bore-heads had shattered this month and the Consortium had gone on drilling; slippage was a cost, and the Consortium was very good at costs. You did not bring a Church, a warrant, an Inquisitor who had officiated at the Dawning, and a wire from the Organum, four hundred feet down a shaft, to save yourself a bore-head.

You brought them to ask a question.

*Is it time?*

He had heard it asked once. He had heard what the asking cost. Somebody in the Arbiterium, with beautiful handwriting and a pair of scales for a signature, had read the reports of the First Fracture and had not seen a catastrophe at all. They had seen an experiment. And they had spent seven years refining it, and shipped it to a quarry in the Reach in a black case, to find out whether it would work twice.

The pitch on the gauge was coming up, and up, and steadying.

Kaelen picked up his toolbag and walked along the collar walk to the drum.

---

"Sir?" Tobiah, trotting after him. "Sir, the readings are perfect, they've never been—"

"Slippage on the lock," said Kaelen. "I can hear it."

He could not, and the boy would know that he could not; the lock was singing like a bell in tune. But Tobiah Marsh had been fourteen when he read *On the Calibration of Resonant Bores*, and had underlined whole paragraphs in two colors of ink, and there was nobody in the world he was less able to contradict.

"Yes, sir," said Tobiah. "What do you want?"

"Spanner. Sit on the rail there and call the pitch to me every ten seconds."

The plate over the phase port had eight bolts. He had promised himself forty seconds and he took fifty-one, kneeling on the collar walk with the drum's warm brass against his shoulder and the bore grinding a yard from his ear, and the whole time the boy sat on the rail calling numbers and Lyra sang and the Inquisitor's chain creaked and the Factor watched from the platform rail with her green lenses, and nobody stopped him, because he was the supervising engineer, and he was doing what supervising engineers do.

The plate came off. He laid it down quietly.

Inside: the timing gear of the Vance Sequence, brass and glass and hair-fine wire, the machine that told the bore when it might bite. It had been built from his drawings. He knew every tooth of it the way you know a word you have said ten thousand times.

He put his hand into his toolbag and took out the reverse-phase coil.

"Sir," said Tobiah. His voice had changed. "Sir, what's that?"

Kaelen looked up at him.

The boy was standing now, on the rail, with his charts under his arm and the face of someone watching the man out of the book kneel down in front of an open port with a thing in his hands that was not in any procedure he had ever been taught.

There was a moment — Kaelen thought about it afterward a great deal — when he could have said something. *Get down. Get to the cage. Go now and don't look behind you.* He had perhaps two seconds and one sentence.

He said, "You'll want to hold onto something."

And he seated the coil in the port.

---

The lock threw.

It made a sound that nobody in the chamber had ever heard, because there had never been one: the Carrow Bore's harmonic lock, which held the bit a hair's breadth beneath the pitch at which the Root wished to fail, went suddenly and violently out of phase with itself. The drum screamed. The chains came up taut and rang like a struck anvil, and the whole bore-head, thirty tons of iron with its teeth buried in living wood, tried to turn and could not, and the shaft of it twisted, and twisted, and the crown of glass teeth snapped off inside the Root with a crack that went through the chamber like a bell breaking.

Then everything happened at once.

The platform lurched. A chain-drum ran away with a shriek, spun its cable off the sheave and came down through the scaffolding, and the scaffolding went with it. Men were shouting. A glass channel split lengthwise and a tongue of green-gold glass went across the platform in a wave two inches deep, and where it touched the deck-boards the boards smoked and sang.

On the catwalk, the Inquisitor was on his knees with the chain wrapped round his fist.

"*The hold!*" he was shouting. "*Hold the note! Daughter — hold the note!*"

Because the note *was* still going. Kaelen heard it under the wreck of everything: one hundred and seventy-four, hoarse and thin, and unbroken, because she was a Cantor and the last thing a Cantor does is stop.

And the thing in the Root, which had drawn in and in and in until it was nearly the size of a question, stayed drawn in.

*Now*, thought Kaelen. *Now, or it does what the Light did.*

He got up off the collar walk, and put his glass hand on the drum, and shouted across the shaking platform in a voice he had not used since the Foundry:

"LYRA! *SING IT DOWN!*"

---

She let it fall.

He heard the moment the note bent: the Root note sliding down a whole fifth, slow as a leaf coming off a branch, in a torn hoarse voice with blood on the collar of her shift — and then climbing, and falling again, the small patient village trick out of the margin of a dead man's notebook. *Down. Go back where you came from.*

The chamber changed.

The Root's pitch, which had come up to her and locked there like a hand closing, *slipped*. The bore's teeth, already broken, lost what little purchase they had left. The whole vast presence in the stone — the listening, the drawing-in, the terrible attention of something ten thousand years old waiting to hear whether its relief had come — wavered.

And then, very slowly, like a man who has stood up too fast and sits down again, it let go of the question.

Through the glass in his arm, Kaelen heard the lullaby start up again. Badly. Stumbling. From the beginning, from the first of the five notes, the way you begin again when you have lost your place.

He shut his eyes for exactly one second, which was all he could afford, and then the Steam Golems came onto the platform.

---

They were haulage machines, not soldiers: brass and boiler-iron, twice a man's height, built to lift bore-heads and carry chain, with grapples for hands and a single dim glass eye. The Consortium kept four at the casing, and the Factor turned them all at once, which meant she had made up her mind about him somewhere between the coil going in and the drum screaming.

"*Engineer Vance*," said Adela Vosk from the platform rail, quite calmly, through a speaking-trumpet, "*step away from the drum.*"

The nearest Golem swung a grapple at him that would have taken his head off his shoulders.

Kaelen went under it and hit the thing in the knee-joint with his glass fist.

He had never fought anything in his life until three days ago, and he did not fight now. He broke things. He had spent ten years learning exactly where machines wish to fail, and a haulage Golem wishes to fail at the knee, where the steam-line crosses the joint; the glass went through the brass housing and the line let go and the whole machine sat down in a screaming cloud of white vapor and did not get up.

The second one caught him across the chest with a grapple and threw him eight feet into the platform rail.

Something went in his ribs. He got up anyway, because the platform was on fire in two places and Lyra was still on her stool ten feet from a broken bore-head with a wire through her throat, and he could hear, under everything, the note she was still making.

He drank the second Golem.

That was what it felt like, afterward, when he tried to think about it: he got his glass hand flat on the boiler and the arm *pulled*, the way it had pulled the bell out of a Legionnaire's helmet, and the machine's own working noise — the hammer of the piston, the shriek of the valve, the ring of brass under strain — went into him. The Golem shuddered and went quiet and dead, and Kaelen's arm rang so loud and so full that men on the far side of the platform put their hands over their ears, and something in his shoulder went cold, and the cold ran down into his chest.

*The tideline*, he thought, distantly. *That will have cost me.*

The third Golem stopped where it stood. So did the fourth.

"*Enough*," said the Factor. The green lenses were fixed on him with an expression he could not read at all. "Engineer, I would very much like to know what you thought you were—"

And then the speaking-tube on the platform stanchion began to shriek.

---

Every man on the platform knew that sound. It was the three-note scream of the purge alarm, which was tested on the first Rootday of every month and had never once been run in earnest, and the whole chamber turned toward it — the shift-men, the Legionnaires, the sisters, the Inquisitor on his catwalk.

Kaelen got to the stanchion first and pulled the flap open.

His father's voice came out of it, small and tinny, from four hundred feet above.

"—*sealing the vault. Governor's authority, under the works charter. Factor, I'm sorry. The bleed-back's coming up the shaft and it's coming fast and there's four hundred feet of men above it.*" A pause; the sound of somebody being argued with, a long way off. "*Sixty seconds on the reservoir gates. Anyone below, get to the cage.*"

Kaelen put his mouth to the tube.

"Da."

The silence on the other end lasted three of the sixty.

"*...Kaelen.*"

"Don't run the purge. The Root's wounded — if you dump forty thousand gallons of raw glass into an open casing it will not seal, it will—"

"*I know what it will do,*" said his father. "*I've a bore-master here telling me exactly what it will do, and I've a Factor's clerk telling me the other thing, and I've got Tam's widow's grandson on the day shift on the third terrace and I have to choose between a hole and a town.*" His voice did not rise. It had not risen in years. "*Get to the cage, boy. I'm counting.*"

"Da—"

"*Forty-one*," said Aldric Vance. "*Forty.*"

---

Afterward Kaelen could never lay the next part out in order, which was a thing that had never happened to him before. He had always been able to lay things out in order.

He remembered the Inquisitor coming down the ladder from the catwalk three rungs at a time with his red robes burning at the hem where the glass had run, shouting for the Rite, for the case, for the collar, for the daughter — and the Factor taking him by the arm and saying something short, and Ignatius Crane looking, for one instant, exactly like a man in a Foundry with a censer chain in his fist and everything going wrong.

He remembered Tobiah Marsh helping a shift-man with a crushed foot toward the cage, and turning, and looking back at Kaelen with his charts still under his arm.

He remembered getting the collar off Lyra's throat: the buckle at the second notch, and the wire coming out of her skin easily because it had never been anywhere but her skin, and the blood, and her face, and her saying something he could not hear over the alarm and the running feet, and then saying it again with her hand on his jaw, which was: *"The pumps."*

And the pumps.

There were twelve men on the pumps. The cage held eight. When the alarm went, the shift-men ran, and the Church ran, and the Legionnaires walked, and the Hollows kept pumping, because nobody had told them to do anything else, up and down, up and down, with a rhythm like clockwork, while the platform burned behind them.

He remembered Lyra in her white shift going along the line and taking them off the handles one at a time by the wrist, and each of them stopping when she touched him, the way a cart stops when you unhook the horse, and standing there. He remembered getting four of them into the cage with Tobiah and the crushed foot and the sisters, and the cage going up, and the bell ringing for the next, and the next not coming, because the winch-house had already been cleared.

He remembered the man with the wedding ring and the scar through his eyebrow looking at Lyra's hand on his wrist with his lips moving.

And then, four hundred feet above, at the top of the world, the reservoir gates opened.

---

It did not sound like a flood.

It sounded like the largest bell in the world being poured full of sand. Forty thousand gallons of raw Aether-Glass came down the shaft in a column, and the noise of it arrived first, and the light arrived with it — green-gold, rising up the chamber walls, bright as a summer field — and Kaelen grabbed Lyra by the arm and put her behind a chain-drum and got the glass arm up over them both.

The column struck the platform and went through it and hit the open wound in the Root.

And the Root drank.

That was the thing. That was the thing he could never explain afterward to anyone who had not been in the chamber. The raw glass did not smother the wound; it did not seal it, or cool it, or quench anything. It went *in*, all forty thousand gallons of it, into a hole that had been bored into the flank of a living thing, and for a heartbeat every vein in the walls of that chamber blazed like a lamp with the wick turned all the way up —

— and the thing in the stone, which had been singing a lullaby badly for ten thousand years, stopped.

Then it screamed.

It was not a sound. It came up through the boards and through his boots and through his teeth and through the glass in his arm, which rang so violently that the bones behind it shrieked, and four hundred feet above, in the town of Oakhaven, every iron-oak from the quarry road to the Flume rang at once like the bells they had taken. Lyra's hands were over her ears and her mouth was open. The chamber's walls moved. The ridged grey-brown flank of the Root, the wall that was the room, flexed like a flank — like something rolling over in its sleep — and the first crack opened along it from the wound to the roof, thirty feet of it, and out of the crack, instead of glass, came green.

Green shoots. Growing. Fast, and fast, and faster, in the dark, out of a wound four hundred feet below a town.

"Up," Kaelen said. "*Up* —"

And he got her onto the ladder of the service-stair with the Root coming apart behind them and the whole shaft above them beginning, very gently, to fill with leaves.
