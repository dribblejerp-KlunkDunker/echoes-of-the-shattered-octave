## CHAPTER FIFTEEN
### Statistical Anomaly

> *The Veil keeps three rules. Write what happened. Write who it happened to. Do not comfort yourself in the margin.*
>
> — Precept of the Silent Veil

*Whisper-Grove. Memoryday night.*

---

They moored at the Cloister road sky-dock an hour after dark, and Kaelen went down into the city with Lyra and a lantern and found out what a law looks like after it has been switched off.

The Reach was dying of too much. Whisper-Grove had died of not enough, three years ago, in a single night, and then had gone on standing there.

Nothing had fallen down. That was the first wrong thing. The streets were swept; the shutters were latched; there were flowers in a window-box on Bell Yard, dead flowers, three years of them, one summer's crop fallen and the next grown up through the rot and fallen in its turn. There were carts in the carters' yard with their shafts up, and horses long since gone, and a cat — a perfectly ordinary tabby cat, fat and glossy, which came out of a doorway and walked alongside them for forty yards and then lost interest. Animals did not take the Forgetting. Nobody knew why. The Office had a paper about it.

The people were in the streets.

The first one they passed was a woman in a good coat walking from a pillar to a basin and back. The second was a lamplighter with his pole, going along Bell Yard lighting lamps that had no oil in them and had not been filled since 416, touching the taper to the wick and waiting, and moving on to the next one, and at the end of the row turning around and coming back to the first.

Lyra stopped in front of him. She had been silent for five days — not by choice; there was still nothing in her throat but air — and she stood in the fog with her lantern up, watching an old man light a lamp that would not light, and Kaelen watched her hands open and close twice.

She got out the slate. She wrote, and turned it to him.

*CAN THEY EAT?*

"Sable's paper says they feed themselves if food's put where their loop goes," said Kaelen. "The Office kept four thousand of them alive for two years at Whisper-Grove to work the pumps. So yes."

She wiped the slate with her cuff and wrote again, harder, so the chalk squealed.

*I MEANT ARE THEY HUNGRY.*

He did not have an answer to that. He had not, in fact, thought of the question, and he stood in a dead street in the fog and understood that for three days he had been thinking of four hundred glass spheres in a hold as *cargo to be delivered somewhere useful*, which was precisely the phrase the Catalogue used, and that this was how it happened, and that it took about a week.

The Weeping Plaza glowed at the end of the street like a fire under a door.

---

They went down the Moot-house steps into a foot of lit water, and the mist took the lantern-light and gave it back tenfold, and out in the middle of the plaza, under the colonnade, a woman in grey was reading aloud.

She was small, and thin, and her hair was cut off blunt at the jaw the way a clerk cuts her own hair in a mirror. She stood in the water in sodden skirts with her back to them. And beside her, at her left elbow, at exactly the height of a writing-desk, an open book hung in the air with nothing under it, and its pages were turning by themselves.

"Entry forty thousand, one hundred and nine," she said. "Ketter Dell, nineteen, apprenticed to a fuller. Taken on the fourth day. He gave his age as twenty so as not to be sent to the back of the queue."

"We're looking for the scribe," Kaelen said. "The one who wrote the catalogue."

"You have found her, Kaelen Vance," she said, without turning round. "Chief Resonant Engineer, Consortium of Bores, badge four-oh-eight-A. Conservatory of Bores, 402 to 406, first in your year. Author of the Vance Sequence, revisions one through three." The Ledger turned a page for her. "Last recorded at the Siphon Foundry, Sol-Luminary, on the morning of the Great Foundry Rupture, year 412. Office estimate of survival for persons at your station on that gantry: two percent."

She turned around.

Her face was much younger than her voice and very tired, and there was ink to the second knuckle on both hands.

"You are a statistical anomaly," she said. "I wrote you into the ledger of the dead nine days after the Fracture, and I have never had cause to correct an entry of that kind before or since. Do you know how few people that is? Out of seventy-four thousand and six hundred and eleven here, and eleven thousand at Sol-Luminary, you are one of two."

"Who's the other?"

"I don't know," said Sable. "She fell into the Spire."

---

She would not come.

He put it to her three ways in ten minutes, and he was not, he realized somewhere in the middle of it, doing it well; he was doing it the way an engineer specifies a part.

"There are four hundred and thirty-two cores in the hold of that ship," he said. "Whisper-Grove stock. The tags run in the forty-one thousands. They were consigned to a Carillon garrison in the Reach and the garrison is under a wood now, and the man who was carrying them has decided not to deliver them, and I need to know whether what was done to them can be undone."

"No," said Sable.

"You wrote the catalogue—"

"I wrote the *catalogue*." She said the word as though handing him a tool and correcting his grip on it. "I am a list, Engineer. I am not a cure. I recorded what was taken, in the order it was taken, with the name and the age and the trade and any last thing the subject said that I could hear from the door. I have never operated a press. I could not tell you the first thing about how one works, except what it sounds like from twelve feet away, which is like a tuning fork struck against a rib." She paused. "Which you may find you already know, because I am told your Sequence assumes a hundred and seventy-four hertz, and the Office's press is tuned to seven hundred and forty-one, and they are cousins."

"Then come and look at one with me."

"There is a harvest-house four miles north of us with two presses in it and eleven staff, and I have been within a quarter-mile of it twice." Her voice did not rise at all. "I went back the second time because I had begun to believe I might be brave. I stood in the trees for two hours, and then I came home and read twenty names."

Lyra came forward through the water then, and held up the slate.

*HOW MUCH DOES IT COST YOU TO PUT ONE BACK?*

Sable looked at it. Then at Lyra — at the white shift under the borrowed coat, and the mark under her jaw where a wire had been, still scabbed, still weeping a little.

"Unison hold," she said. "Stage nine. Collar and wire." She said it as a clerk reads a form. "You are the first person I have met who has come off one alive. The Office's carriage records list forty collars sent to Sol-Luminary in 411 and four returned." She looked back at the slate. "You are asking the right question and the answer is that I do not know what one costs, because I have never dared do it. Weight for weight. A name out of the book and into a head, and something of mine goes onto the scale to balance it. I have three things left that I am certain of. I can name them. That is not a great deal of currency for four hundred and thirty-two people, or for seventy-four thousand."

"Then—"

"Then nothing, Engineer. Then I am a woman with a book who reads twenty names a night, which is what I can do, and what I can do is not enough, and I have made my peace with that in the only way anyone ever does, which is badly."

She turned away, and the Ledger turned with her.

"Get your ship out before dawn," she said. "The Office has proctors in the Bell Yard. They came for me and now they will come for you, and you have children on that deck; I could hear them from the steps."

And then Halder Wick, who was setting his table forty feet away in the glowing water, stopped setting his table.

---

Kaelen's arm heard them before anyone saw them.

It went up a quarter-tone — the glass pulling toward something the way it always did, a hard high sympathy — and he had time to say "Lyra" and get his back to the nearest pillar before the first proctor came out of the mist behind Sable with a rod already swinging.

They were not soldiers. That was the thing he understood in the first two seconds and never forgot: nobody was shouting, nobody had a sword, and there was no noise at all. Three figures in grey oilcloth with masks of fine silver mesh, moving through knee-deep water without splashing, because they had been trained in a city where sound brings the Cacophony and a screaming subject lengthens the queue.

The rods were lead-cored and wrapped in felt. They were made to break a person quietly.

The first blow took Sable across the shoulder-blades and put her face-down in the water.

The Ledger did not fall. It hung exactly where it had been, at the height of a writing-desk, open, its pages turning, over its drowning owner — and the proctor reached for it with his free hand and his hand went through it like smoke, which told Kaelen something useful about the book and nothing useful about the next four minutes.

He came off the pillar and hit the man in the side of the head with the glass.

Ribs. His ribs went off like a dropped tray the instant he swung, and the pain folded him a half-beat early, so what should have been a killing blow went in shallow and low and took the proctor across the jaw instead of the temple. The mesh mask tore. The man went sideways into the water and came up on one knee with his jaw hanging visibly wrong, and he *still did not make a sound*, and he went for his belt.

What came off the belt was not a weapon.

It was brass, about the size of a hand, shaped like a squat two-tined fork with a glass ampoule seated between the tines, and Kaelen had seen the drawings in a supply schedule and had never in his life seen one held.

A hand-press.

The proctor struck it against the pillar.

The tone went through the plaza — a clean bright seven hundred and forty-one, held and swelling, the same note the drowned Anchor under this city had held for ten thousand years — and every piece of glass within thirty feet answered it, including the piece Kaelen was wearing.

His arm shrieked.

It was not pain in the arm; there was no pain in the arm and had not been since 412. It was pain in the *seam*, the frost-line across his chest where glass met him, and in the bone behind it, and it went through his shoulder and down into his gut, and his left arm locked rigid from fingertip to collarbone like a bar of iron and would not answer him at all.

He went down on one knee in the water with a dead arm and three broken ribs and a man coming at him with a press in his fist and eight seconds of contact between him and being nobody.

---

Lyra hit the proctor with a marble finial.

It was the ornament off the top of the basin's corner post: a ball of white stone the size of a small melon, three years loose in its socket, and she got it up out of the water with both hands and brought it down on the back of his skull as he bent over Kaelen.

The mesh mask did nothing. The stone went through it into the bone and there was a sound like a boot in gravel, and the man dropped into the plaza and the water went dark lilac around him and kept going dark, spreading.

Lyra stood over him with the finial still in her hands, breathing through her mouth.

She had never in her life hurt anything larger than an apple-grub.

She dropped the stone and turned and was sick into the water, and while she was doing it the second proctor came around the pillar at her with his rod up.

---

Here is what a Cantor is for.

She could not sing. She could not scream a warning. But she had spent thirty-three years learning to hear a room the way other people see one, and for five days, on a crowded deck, with no voice at all, she had been doing nothing but listen.

She heard him coming. Not his feet — he moved through water without splashing, which was the whole of their training. She heard his *breathing*: a man working hard through a mesh mask, a fast wet rasp with a catch in it, coming from her left rear quarter at about eleven feet.

She dropped flat into the water.

The rod went through the space where her head had been, and the proctor's own momentum took him a stride past her into the open, and she came up out of the lilac water with her hand already going into the coat pocket where a Cantor keeps the thing every Cantor carries.

Not a knife. A tuning key: four inches of square steel, heavy in the hand, with a socket at the end for a peg.

She put it into the side of his knee.

He went down at last with a sound — the first sound any of them had made, a short flat grunt bitten off at once — and rolled, and got up, and she was already moving backward, because she was not a fighter and knew it, and what she did next was the only thing left.

She put two fingers in her mouth and whistled.

The falling fifth, sharp as a thrown stone, at a volume the whole plaza could hear: the Root note and down, twice, the signal she had invented on a doorstep for a seven-year-old boy.

Kaelen's head came round.

She pointed. Not at the man in front of her — at the third proctor, the one nobody had seen yet, who had come around the colonnade behind Kaelen with his press already in his hand and was four feet away and closing.

---

Kaelen could not lift his left arm.

He turned on his knee in the water and drove his right shoulder into the man's hips, which is not a technique, and the two of them went down together into a foot of glowing water among the legs of the colonnade.

The press went out of the proctor's hand and skittered away.

Then it was the worst kind of fight there is, which is two men on the ground who both want the same object, in the dark, in water, with one of them wearing an arm that will not move. The proctor got a thumb into the corner of Kaelen's eye. Kaelen got his right hand into the mesh of the mask and pulled until something in it tore and then kept pulling. They rolled, and the water went over Kaelen's face, and he swallowed some of it and it tasted of cold stone and lilac and old drowned marble. The man was stronger than he was, and rested, and unbroken, and his knee was grinding down onto three cracked ribs with the patient weight of someone who has done this before and knows that it works.

Kaelen's vision went grey at the edges.

And out of the grey, quite close, at about the level of the water, a voice said, very clearly:

"Emory Wain."

The proctor stopped.

It was not a flinch. It was a full stop, an absolute hitch in a machine, and it lasted perhaps a second and a half, and in that second and a half the man on top of Kaelen turned his head toward the voice with an expression that could be seen even through a torn silver mask.

Sable was on her hands and knees in the water eight feet away with blood coming out of her nose and down her chin, and the Bound Ledger hung open above her, and she read from it.

"Emory Wain. Twenty-six. Of Cooper's Rise in the Iron Peaks. Admitted to the service of the Office of the Catalogue in the spring of 414 under the standing terms, which require the surrender of the name and the family, so that the proctor may not be leveraged." Her voice was thick and flat and she did not hurry. "Your mother was Hanne. You had a sister called Ivy. You asked the intake clerk whether you could keep the name until the end of the week, and the clerk wrote it in the margin as a matter of interest, and the clerk was me."

Kaelen got his knee up between them and threw the man off and rolled on top of him and hit him in the mask with the only hand that worked until he stopped moving.

---

It took four minutes altogether. He knew, because Tobiah Marsh, who had come down the Cloister road at a dead run with a lantern and arrived in time for none of it, had heard the whistle and looked at his own watch, because he was that sort of young man.

The tally:

One proctor dead in the water by the basin, killed with a marble finial by a village Cantor who had never hurt anything in her life, and who would sit on the Moot-house steps afterward for a long time with her hands hanging between her knees.

One proctor — Emory Wain, twenty-six, of Cooper's Rise — unconscious, jaw broken, wrists bound with his own belt, and still, when he came round an hour later, absolutely silent.

One proctor gone. The second man, the one Lyra had put the tuning key into, had got up and limped away into the mist during the last of it, and by the time anyone thought about him he was three streets off, and he had a name and a number for the ship and four hundred and thirty-two crates' worth of reasons for the Registrar-General to send everything he had.

Kaelen's ribs: two of the three had gone from cracked to broken, and his left arm did not come back until the small hours, and when it did it came back wrong, ringing a full tone flat, and it stayed that way for two days.

And Halder Wick, fifty-eight, joiner, of the Bell Yard, was face-down in the water by his table with his skull broken, because during the fight he had walked into it — had walked into it, at the pace of a man going to answer his own front door, because his loop took him across that corner of the plaza at about that hour and nobody had told him not to — and one of the proctors had put a rod through the back of his head without breaking stride, the way you would push a chair out of a doorway.

The four stones were still on his table. The fifth one, the loaf, had fallen into the water.

Sable waded out to him and knelt down in the lilac glow and turned him over and closed his eyes with her thumb, and then opened the Ledger and wrote the entry while kneeling in the water with the book hanging in front of her: date, name, number, cause, and the last line of it, which Kaelen read over her shoulder because he could not stop himself:

*He set a table for four every evening for three years. I do not know who he was expecting. There is no one left to ask.*

---

She would still not come.

She said it standing on the Moot-house steps at midnight with a broken nose and her skirts black to the waist. "Take the ship out. Now, tonight, before that man reaches the harvest-house. Go south, go to the Archipelago, go anywhere the Office's writ is slow. I will not be on it."

"Why?" said Kaelen.

"Because there is a press four miles north of here in a building I helped design the queue for," said Sable, "and while I am in this city I am a woman who might one day be brave enough to go and take it, and the moment I get on a ship I am a woman who left." She picked up her lamp. "Good night, Engineer. I am sorry about your ribs. Your Cantor saved your life twice and she will not sleep for a month; see that somebody sits with her."

She went down the steps into the water and began to walk out across the plaza toward the colonnade, where two hundred people were walking their loops in the glow with a dead man among them, and Kaelen stood at the top of the steps with his arm hanging ringing and flat and called after her.

"There are four hundred and thirty-two of them in that hold."

She kept walking.

"Forty-one thousand and six," he said, louder. "Annet Sowerby. Six years old. She talks to the boy who sleeps against the third crate. She asked him to leave the lamp on."

Sable stopped.

She stood in the middle of the lit water with her back to him, in the fog, with the Ledger hanging at her elbow, and she did not turn around and she did not answer, and she stood there for a very long time.
