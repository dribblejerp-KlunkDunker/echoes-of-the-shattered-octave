## CHAPTER SIX
### The Listening Hour

> *For lost lambs, the calling-song. Sing it full, or not at all. A lamb in the dark will not follow a whisper.*
>
> — T. Ashgrove, Cantor's notebook, 401 SR

*Oakhaven. Massday.*

---

Her voice came back the way the tide comes back into a harbor: not all at once, but by degrees, filling the low places first.

Lyra tested it at dawn, sitting on the edge of the loft bed with a clay jar pressed to the hollow of her own throat, the way Cantors had tested themselves for three hundred years. She hummed into the jar. What came out was thin, and rough at the edges, like a string tuned too far and let down again — but it was there. The Root note. Hoarse, and true.

She sat and held the jar against her throat for a long time afterward, feeling the note go out of her into the clay and back into her, and did not know whether she was relieved or afraid. Both. It was always both.

*Every mending borrows.*

She had asked her father once, when she was small, *borrows from what?* And he had laughed and said, *From the Cantor, lark. What else is there?* and ruffled her hair, and she had not asked again. She thought now that he had not answered the question. She thought now that he had known the answer and had not wanted her to have it yet.

---

Hetty Bram came to the door at the first hour with Davin Hale's boots.

She said nothing. She held them out, tied together by their laces, and Lyra took them, and Hetty looked at her for a moment with her big square face working, and then turned and went back up Cooper's Row toward the square. The Cantor sang the burials. The Cantor took the boots. That was how it had been in Oakhaven for two years.

There would be no burial. There was nothing to bury. There would be the boots, and the song, and a stone in the churchyard with a name on it and nothing underneath.

She carried them down to the Hollow.

Orla Hale sat in her chair by the window, where she always sat, where she would always sit. The shoots along the chair-arms had grown in the three days since Rootday; some of them had put out a second leaf. The window was open a crack, and the morning came in pale and cool and lay across the old woman's rooted hands.

Lyra did not have to say anything. She set the boots on the floorboards at Orla's feet, side by side, the way she had found them, and knelt down beside them.

Orla looked at them for a long time.

"He sang it," she said at last. "Didn't he."

"He sang it."

"I heard him." A rustling breath. "Clear across the square. I was sitting here with the window open, and I heard my boy sing the harvest home." Her eyes were wet and quite steady. "Was he any good?"

Lyra thought about it. She owed her the truth.

"He couldn't hold the fourth line," she said.

Orla laughed. It was a terrible sound — a laugh with the dry whisper of roots moving beneath it, like wind in barley — and it went on too long and turned into something else, and Lyra put her arms around her as well as she could, around the chair and the shoots and the pale web of roots, and held on.

"Never could," Orla said into her hair. "His da couldn't either. Forty years I tried to teach the pair of them." A breath. "I heard it again, after. Did you know that? Out past the Hedge. Somebody singing it back." She was quiet a moment. "It had his voice. It didn't have *him*. You can tell, can't you, when you've known a voice all its life. It had the voice, and there was nobody inside it."

Lyra said nothing.

"You'll still sing me down," said Orla. "Proper."

"Proper."

"Before they come with their fire, Lyra." The old woman's voice was very calm. "Not after. Promise me that. I'll not have them singing their one note over me while I burn in my chair. I want the harvest home. All the lines." A pause. "Even the fourth."

Lyra promised.

---

Old Tam was on the bench outside the cooperage in the midday sun, weaving a basket with his long knobbed fingers, and he looked up as she passed and patted the bench beside him, and she sat, because you did not refuse Tam.

He was eighty-one. He had been a night-hummer since the first autumn of the Hedge, and a basket-maker for sixty years before that, and he had outlived his wife and both his sons and most of his friends. He had a face like a walnut and a voice, when he hummed at the crossings, that could not reach any note above the middle of the stave.

"Saw you Nullday," he said, in the Narrow, weaving. "At the Weavers' Cross. Walked right past it." He nodded to himself. "Your da'd be proud. Cool as milk. Never turned your head."

Lyra looked at her hands.

"I hear 'em too," Tam said, after a while. "Nights. I'm on the Flume crossing most nights now, down by the tannery culvert, where the Hedge is thin. You hear 'em clearer there." He pulled a withy tight. "Voices I know."

"Whose?"

"The missus, mostly." He smiled with half his mouth. "Forty years dead, near enough. The pox took her the year the old king went into the glass. She used to call me *Tammy*. Nobody else ever did, before or since. And now something out past the thorn calls me *Tammy*, three or four nights in the eight." He wove. "I don't answer. Never have. Never will."

The withies creaked in his fingers.

"But I listen," he said. "Some nights. I'll be honest with you, girl, since you're a Cantor and a Cantor ought to know these things. Some nights I sit on my stool and I stop humming, just for a bit, and I listen to her say it." He did not look up from the basket. "Is that wicked, do you think?"

Lyra could not answer him for a long moment.

"No," she said at last, in her new hoarse voice. "I don't think it's wicked."

"Nor do I," said Tam. "But I stop humming, and that's wicked enough for a night-hummer." He tied off the rim. "Flume crossing tonight. Come by, and I'll give you a basket for your da's reeds. Keep the damp off 'em."

---

She had taught Pim the whistle the day before, on the Carders' step, in the hour before she went to the Boar & Dulcimer with Kaelen.

She had had no voice then. Only breath. So she had sat with him in the sun and put her lips together and whistled — very softly, in the Narrow — the falling fifth. The Root note, and down. Two notes, like a bird that knows only one thing to say.

"If you're ever lost," she had whispered, with what was left of her whisper, "or frightened, or you can't find your mam, you whistle that. Only that. And I'll whistle it back. Then you'll know it's me."

Pim had tried. He was seven; his whistle came out mostly air and spit, and they had both laughed, silently, the way Oakhaven children laughed. Then he got it. Two notes. Down.

"Only you and me know it," she had told him. "It's ours."

She had not told the stone behind the cooperage. She had not been to the stone since the forty-first night. That was the point of it. She had thought about that, sitting on the step in the sun, with a cold sick feeling in her stomach that she had not let herself look at.

*Only you and me know it.*

---

The Listening Hour came down on Oakhaven with lamplight and the soft sound of doors, and Lyra walked to the Hollow to look at Pim's hands.

She was halfway down the lane behind the tannery when she saw the blue door standing open and Nell Carder in it, with a lamp in one hand and the other hand over her mouth.

"He went for water." Nell's whisper was so tight it barely came through. "The pump by the Flume, before the Hour, like always. Fifty steps. It's *fifty steps*, Lyra. I watched him go. And he didn't come back, and I went, and the bucket's there, and he's—" She seized Lyra's arm with a grip like a trap. "He said he heard you."

Lyra stood very still.

"Before he went. He said, *Lyra's singing, Mam. She's down by the Flume. She says she's got something for my hands.*" Nell's eyes were enormous. "I told him you'd come by the house later. And he said you were singing the one — the falling one — the one you sing for his hands — and he wanted—" She shook Lyra's arm. "You weren't there. Were you. You weren't at the Flume."

"I was at home," Lyra said.

She heard her own voice say it, hoarse and small and torn, and understood, in the same moment, everything.

Forty-one nights. Forty-one nights on a stone behind the cooperage with her back to the thorn, talking. Telling it about her day. The patients. The Carder boy's hands, and the falling fifth, and the notebooks, and the note in the margin. Talking and talking, in her own voice, a hand's width from its mouth, while it listened.

It had never needed to learn her voice. She had given it her voice. Every night. For forty-one nights.

She was already running.

---

The Flume crossing was where the tannery lane met the bank of the green aqueduct-river at the lowest corner of the town, and there the Hedge came right down to the water. The tannery's runoff went out beneath it through a stone culvert with an iron grate, into the water-meadow beyond. A child could not lift the grate. A child did not need to. Its bars had rusted through at the bottom two winters ago, and nobody had got round to it.

Tam was on his stool.

He was humming *The Miller's Daughter* through his nose, missing the high notes, with his blanket over his knees and his eyes shut, and when Lyra came pelting down the bank he opened his eyes and stopped, and looked at her face, and then, slowly, at the culvert.

The grate was pushed aside. A small bucket lay on its side in the mud beside it, a trickle of water running out of it into the reeds.

"I didn't see," Tam said. His face had gone grey. "I didn't — Lyra, I was listening. I'd stopped. Just for a bit. I was only—"

From the far side of the Hedge, very clearly, a woman's voice began to sing.

Lyra's voice.

Her voice — her *old* voice, her whole voice, the voice she had had before she broke it on a hearth-rug before dawn on Rootday. Clear and warm and perfectly in tune, singing low and sweet and patient, in the Narrow: the Root note, and down. The falling fifth. Over and over. And between the notes, gently, the way she spoke to the children in the Hollow, it murmured:

"*Come on, love. Come here. Let me sing to your hands.*"

And a small voice, further off than it should have been, uncertain, answered:

"*Lyra? I can't see you. Where are you?*"

"*Here, sweetheart. Just a little further. Hold your hands out stiff, the way I showed you.*"

She had said that. On the stone. *I've shown him how to hold his hands out stiff, Da, so they won't touch anything.*

Tam's hand closed on her wrist.

"Don't," he said. "Lyra. It's the Hour. You can't. Don't."

She drew her wrist out of his hand, very gently, and went down on her knees in the tannery mud, and crawled into the culvert.

---

It was eight feet long, and low, and thick with the stink of lime and rotten hide, and when she came out of the far end on her hands and knees into the water-meadow, she was on the wrong side of the Hedge for the first time in two years.

The world outside was very quiet.

That was the first thing. Inside the Hedge there was always a sound: hush-lamps whistling, night-hummers humming, somebody's cradle creaking two houses down. Out here there was nothing. The meadow lay in a long grey slope of wet grass and mist, down to the tree-line of iron-oaks at the forest's edge, and the Flume ran alongside it, green and slow and silent, and there was no lamp anywhere, and no voice but the one.

*Come on, love. Just a little further.*

Pim was halfway across the meadow.

She could see him in the dusk: a small figure in his dead father's cut-down shirt, walking slowly through the wet grass toward the trees with his hands held out stiff in front of him, the way she had taught him. He was not running. He was walking the careful, uncertain walk of a child going toward someone he trusts in the dark.

And at the edge of the trees, waiting for him, stood her father.

---

It was his height. That was the first thing. It was his height, and his stoop — the long-backed forward stoop Tobin Ashgrove had carried from forty years of bending over reed-rows — and it stood the way he had stood when he listened to the orchard: weight on one leg, head a little to one side. She would have known that stance across a valley. She had known it all her life.

It was made of the air.

She could see the iron-oaks through it. Its outline wavered the way air wavers over a road in high summer, restless, never quite settling on an edge, and where its face should have been there was no face — only a place where the dusk rippled like water with a wind on it. It had no eyes. It did not need them. It had its head tilted toward the boy coming across the meadow, listening, the way her father had listened, and out of the rippling place where its face was not came her voice.

Her own voice. Singing her own song. Out of her father's shape.

She had thought, for two years, that she knew what the worst thing in the world was. She had not known.

*Come here, sweetheart.*

Lyra put her fingers to her lips and whistled.

The falling fifth. Two notes, sharp and high and far louder than the Narrow — the Root, and down — cutting across the meadow like a thrown stone.

Pim stopped.

He turned. She saw his small white face turn toward her in the dusk, and his hands, still held out stiff. She saw him look back toward the trees. Toward her. Toward the trees. Two Lyras: one whistling behind him in the mud by the Hedge, one singing ahead of him at the edge of the dark.

She whistled it again. *Only you and me know it. It's ours.*

The thing at the tree-line stopped singing.

It tilted its rippling head the other way. Listening. And then, out of the place where its face was not, came a whistle.

Wrong. The first note was right. The second fell too far — a sixth instead of a fifth — and wavered, and slid, the way a child's whistle slides when it has heard a tune once and is trying to catch it. It tried again. Closer. Nearly.

It was learning. There, in front of her, in the space of two breaths, it was learning.

Pim was crying now. She could hear him: thin, and frightened, and much too loud, standing in the middle of the wet meadow halfway between them with his hands held out before him, turning his head from one to the other.

And the thing at the tree-line gave up the whistle and began a different song.

She knew it before the second note. Everyone in the Reach knew it. It was the calling-song — the old song the Cantors had used for three hundred years to bring lost lambs home off the hills at dusk, the song her father had sung at full voice across the Pellows' yard on the last morning of his life, until the hounds came and there was nothing left of him but a lyre by a stile.

It had eaten him while he sang it.

It sang it now in her voice. Softly. Beautifully. In the Narrow. *Come home, come home, the light's going down, come home to the fold—*

And Pim, crying, turned back toward the trees.

*For lost lambs, the calling-song. Sing it full, or not at all.*

Lyra stood up in the mud of the water-meadow, and opened her mouth, and sang.

---

It was the first time in two years that she had sung above the Narrow.

It came out of her like something tearing. Her throat had not healed — it would not heal for days — and the sound that came out of it was not the voice she had had on the stone behind the cooperage, clear and warm and whole. It was hoarse, and cracked, and ragged at the edges like torn cloth, and it was *loud*. It was the loudest sound she had ever made in her life. It went out across the meadow and struck the tree-line and came back off the iron-oaks, and every one of the great trees rang with it, a long low shimmer of bronze from one end of the forest's edge to the other.

*Come home, come home, the light's going down—*

It hurt. It hurt like swallowing glass. She did not stop.

The thing at the tree-line sang too, louder now, matching her, in her own voice, perfect and sweet and unbroken, and for three long notes the two Lyras sang the calling-song at each other across the meadow in the falling dark, one whole and one torn, and a seven-year-old boy stood between them in the wet grass and listened.

Then he ran.

Toward the torn one.

She understood why as he came, stumbling, arms out, face wet. The voice at the trees was the voice she had had *before*. Before Rootday. Before a hearth-rug in the dark, and a whole step instead of a half, and his hands coming down like mouths closing over secrets. It had learned her whole. It did not know what she had spent.

Pim knew. He had been asleep when she spent it. But every word she had said to him since had come out of her in this voice: hoarse, and torn, and his.

He hit her at a run, and she caught him up in her arms, and he wrapped his arms and legs around her and his stiff small root-hands around her neck, and she turned and ran for the culvert.

Behind her, the forest's edge was moving.

---

She did not look back. She did not need to. She could hear them: the jitter coming out of the tree-line and across the meadow, low and fast, a sound like rain beginning on a slate roof, and inside it, from a dozen small wet mouths, every sound they had ever eaten. A baby's laugh. A cart's creak. A man screaming *run*. Davin Hale, a quarter-tone flat: *the last sheaf's in and the barley's high*. And beneath all of them, close and getting closer, her own voice, singing the calling-song, perfectly.

The culvert was thirty yards away. Twenty. She was not going to reach it. She knew it with perfect calm: she was carrying a boy, and her legs were heavy with mud, and her throat was full of blood, and the things behind her were faster than anything in the world.

And then, from the Hedge, from the gap beside the culvert, somebody began to sing *The Miller's Daughter*.

Loud. Very loud. Badly. An old man's cracked tenor, far above the Narrow, going for the high notes and missing every one of them.

Tam came out of the culvert on his hands and knees and stood up in the meadow with his blanket still around his shoulders, and walked away from her. Along the outside of the Hedge, away from the culvert, out into the open grass, singing at the top of his lungs.

*Oh, the miller's daughter, she was fair, and she combed the flour from her yellow hair—*

The jitter behind her swerved.

She felt it go past her — not at her; *past* her — like a gust of wind that has changed its mind, the whole pack of them veering away across the meadow toward the louder voice, the nearer voice, the old man's voice going up and up for a note it had never once in its life been able to reach.

She reached the culvert. She pushed Pim into it ahead of her and heard Nell's voice at the other end, a sob, and small hands being taken, and she went in after him on her knees. At the mouth of it she turned, because she could not help it, and looked back.

Tam stood in the middle of the meadow with the hounds all around him. He was still singing. He had his face turned up to the dark sky and his eyes shut and his blanket round his shoulders, and he was singing *The Miller's Daughter*, and the song was going grainy, and he was going grainy with it, his edges softening and blurring like a drawing someone is rubbing out with a thumb, and still he sang. He came to the high note at the end of the verse. The one he had never, in eighty-one years, been able to reach.

He missed it.

And then there was nothing in the meadow but the jitter, and the mist, and the song — still going, in his voice, a quarter-tone flat — moving away across the grass toward the trees.

---

They found his stool at the Flume crossing, and his blanket gone, and on the stool the basket he had made for her that afternoon, finished, its rim tied off neat.

Nell sat on the bank in the lamplight with Pim in her lap, rocking, both of them silent. Pim's hands were held out stiff behind his mother's back, and he was looking at Lyra over his mother's shoulder with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life. Not fear. Something older than fear. The look of a child who has just learned that there are two of everyone.

People came down the lane with lamps. They stood at a distance. Somebody had seen the Cantor go through the culvert at the Listening Hour; somebody had heard the calling-song at full voice over the whole of the Hollow; somebody had heard Tam. They did not come close. They stood and looked at her, sitting in the tannery mud with blood at the corner of her mouth, and she knew what they were seeing, and she could not blame them for it.

After a while, one of the old women from the dye-works came down the bank without a word, and picked up Tam's stool, and set it straight at the crossing, and sat down on it. She pulled her shawl around her. She began to hum.

The Listening Hour went on. The hush-lamps whistled. Somewhere in the town, a door closed softly.

---

She did not go to the Hedge that night.

She did not have to. At the dead hour, lying in the loft with the basket on the floor beside the bed and her father's seven reed-pipes on their pegs below, she heard it begin. It came from the direction of the cooperage wall, and from the Flume, and from the stretch of Hedge behind Cooper's Row, all at once — as though the thing at the Hedge had discovered, somewhere in the last three days, that it did not need to stand in one place.

It sang to her in four voices.

Her father's, humming *Apples in the Rain*, off-key on the fourth bar.

Kaelen's: the Root note, low, with the thin cold ring of glass threaded through its heart.

Tam's, singing *The Miller's Daughter*, going up and up for the high note, and missing it.

And her own. Not the voice it had used in the meadow — the perfect one, the whole one, the voice she'd had before. The new one. Hoarse, and cracked, and ragged at the edges like torn cloth. The voice she had found in the water-meadow at dusk, singing the calling-song at full volume across the grass to a boy who chose it because it was broken.

It had learned that, too.

And at the very end, just before dawn, softly, in the Narrow, it whistled. Two notes. The Root, and down.

It got the interval exactly right.
