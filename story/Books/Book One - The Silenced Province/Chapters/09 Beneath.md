## CHAPTER NINE
### Beneath

> *The chosen voice shall not speak from the third day, that the instrument be not worn. She shall be given honey and raw egg. She shall be washed. She shall be told that she is fortunate.*
>
> — Rubric of the Rite of Unison

*Oakhaven. Hearthday.*

---

They did not let her speak for the last four days.

It was in the rubric, the sisters explained, on the first morning: the chosen voice is not worn out on ordinary speech. They were two of them, Church women in grey with gold at their collars, and they came up from the Chapel at first light on Memoryday with a basket, and the Legionnaires at the door stood aside for them, and after that Lyra's house was not hers.

They brought honey in a stone pot, and raw eggs, and a little bottle of throat-oil that smelled of almonds, and a slate, and they were kind. That was the part she could not get past afterward. They were not cruel once. They warmed the honey in their hands before they gave it to her. When she coughed in the night, one of them sat up with her and rubbed her back. They called her *daughter*, and they moved about her father's kitchen with the easy competence of women who have prepared a great many instruments, and every few hours, at intervals that she came eventually to understand were also in the rubric, one of them would stop what she was doing, and touch Lyra's hand, and say:

"You are very fortunate."

The first time, Lyra wrote on the slate: *I KNOW.*

The fourth time, she wrote: *DO YOU SAY THAT TO ALL OF THEM?*

The sister read it, and smiled, and wiped the slate with her sleeve, and said, "You are very fortunate," and went back to the eggs.

Over the rooftops, day and night, the Pure Note went on.

That was the other thing. It had not stopped since Dawnday and it would not stop until the Rite: thirty voices in the Chapel passing one note between them, breath by breath, forever. In the first hours the town had come out to stand in the square and listen to it with their hands over their mouths. By the second day nobody was sleeping. By the third, people had begun to plaster their windows with wet cloth and stuff wool in their ears, and children were crying in the lanes for no reason they could name, and old Bettany had come to the door and been turned away by a bell-helm, and gone home and put her head in her apron.

Lyra lay awake under it. It was not a loud sound. It was worse than loud. It was a lid.

---

Surgeon-Grafter Rooke came on Nullday, on the excuse of examining her throat, which he did, thoroughly, with a lamp and a little mirror, and pronounced it — with a flat glance at the sisters — "adequate for four hours, if she is not asked to speak before."

Then he snapped his bag shut, and said, "Cantor, your hands," and took them, and held them in the way a physician holds hands, turning them to the light, and while the sisters were busy at the hearth he read what she had written on her palm in ink an hour before.

He read it twice. His mouth went thin.

*IF THE FIRE COMES BEFORE I'M BACK — ORLA HALE — THE LONG DROPS. LET HER GO QUIET. DON'T LET THEM BURN HER IN THE CHAIR.*

He rubbed his thumb once across her palm, which smudged it into nothing, and put her hands back in her lap, and stood.

"I have never in my life heard of a Cantor dying of a four-hour Hold," he said, to the room in general, "and I have been thirty years in the Office." He picked up his bag. "Good day, sisters. Cantor."

At the door he stopped, with his back to the sisters, and looked at her, and gave her, very slightly, a nod.

It was the only promise anyone made her all week, and it was made with a thumb and a nod, and she held onto it for four days.

---

Kaelen came once, on Rootday night, because the Rite's supervising engineer had cause to see the Rite's voice, and the Consortium's green glass opened every door in Oakhaven.

The sisters withdrew to the far end of the kitchen and did not leave. Lyra sat at her father's table with her slate, and he sat across from her, and for a while neither of them wrote anything.

Then he took the chalk and wrote: *THE BAG IS PACKED.*

She nodded.

*8 BOLTS ON THE PORT PLATE. 40 SECONDS IF THEY DON'T WATCH ME. 3 MINUTES IF THEY DO.*

She took the chalk and wrote: *THEN DON'T LET THEM WATCH YOU.*

He almost smiled. She saw it happen and not finish.

He wrote: *THEY'LL BRING YOU DOWN AT NOON. THE HOLD STARTS WHEN THE HEAD IS ONE SPAN OFF THE CASING. I'LL BE AT THE DRUM ON THE COLLAR WALK. WHEN I MOVE, SING.*

*WHAT DO I SING?*

He looked at the slate for a long moment. Then: *THE FALLING FIFTH. YOUR ONE. THE ONE YOU USE ON THE BOY'S HANDS.*

Lyra stared at the words. Of course. Of course it was that. The little village trick from the margin of her father's notebook, the half-step let down before the string complains — used not on a child's fingers but on a machine the size of a chapel, at the bottom of a shaft, against the Iron Synod and the Church of the Pure Note and a god in a stone.

She wiped the slate and wrote: *IT'S A SMALL SONG.*

*IT'S THE ONLY ONE THAT'S EVER WORKED,* he wrote.

Then he sat looking at her throat for slightly too long, the way a man looks at a road he is going to have to drive a cart down in the dark, and she saw his jaw begin to set, and she took the chalk out of his fingers and wrote, in large letters:

*DON'T SHAKE.*

He read it. He did not look at her.

She wiped it away before the sisters could come and see.

---

They dressed her at dawn on Hearthday.

Not her clothes. A shift of white wool, new, with the seams turned so they would not chafe; the sisters were very particular about the seams. They took her boots and gave her sandals of pale leather. They took her shawl — she had had it eleven years; it had been her mother's — and folded it and set it on her father's chair, and one of them said, kindly, "It will be here when you come back."

They washed her hair. They combed it out. They oiled her throat with the almond oil, working it into the muscle with their thumbs, and buckled around her neck a gorget of thin gold, not the collar, only the gorget, which the rubric required, "that the Light may see the instrument."

Then one of them touched her hand and said, "You are very fortunate," and Lyra found she was not angry anymore. She looked at the woman and understood something with perfect clarity: that this woman believed it; that she had said it in this room for four days and would say it again next year in another room to another girl; and that she was not a monster but a pair of warm hands and a stone pot of honey, and that this was precisely how the thing was done.

Then they took her out into the road.

---

The whole town had come.

That was not the Church's doing. The Church had wanted a procession and had got one: the choir first, holding the Note, and the Flambeaux with their staves of glass-fire in the morning light, and the Inquisitor with the censer, and behind them the Governor in his chain and the Factor in her green lenses, and then Lyra in white between two Church sisters, with two bell-helmed Legionnaires walking at her shoulders.

But the people had come on their own. They lined Cooper's Row and the length of the market road and stood three deep at the mouth of the tannery lane, in their working clothes, on a Hearthday morning when there was work to do, and they did not cheer, because you do not cheer in Oakhaven.

Somewhere by the well-house, an old woman began to hum.

It was very quiet. It was exactly a hand's width of voice, the width the whole town had been taught since the Hedge went up, and it was the Root note — one hundred and seventy-four, the note of the Reach, the note that had been in the ground under their feet since before there were feet.

The dye-works women took it up. Then the carters. Then the men from the tannery, and the baker's wife with flour to her elbows, and Bettany, and Hetty Bram standing in the doorway of the Boar & Dulcimer with her arms folded, and the three Tanner cousins, and old women on stools at the crossings who had been humming all night anyway and now hummed something else.

Under the Church's one enormous holy note, the town of Oakhaven hummed its own, in the Narrow, so softly that no one more than ten feet away could have sworn to it.

The Flambeaux could not do anything about it. That was the beautiful part. There was nothing to punish. It was quieter than speech. It was quieter than praying. It went along the road with her, passed from doorway to doorway, a low warm ugly homely note under the pure one, and Lyra walked through the middle of it in her white shift with her throat oiled and her hair combed out, and wept without making a sound, which was a thing Oakhaven women had learned to do.

At the tannery lane, Lissa Merrow stood at the front with her bandaged forearms at her sides, humming with her mouth shut, and did not look away.

---

The quarry was full of people and iron.

They had built a stair of scaffold down the terraces and hung it with white cloth; they had swept the whole upper terrace; the censer-platform stood ready with its brazier lit. The night shift and the day shift had both been kept on, and they stood along the rails in their drab with their caps off. Along the top terrace, a line of Legionnaires. In the middle of it all, in front of the headframe, on a table with a cloth over it, lay the case of black leather with the Church's sunburst on the lid.

A young man in Consortium drab carried it down the scaffold stair ahead of her, with both hands, the way you carry something at a funeral.

He caught her looking at it, and went scarlet, and then — because he was young, and because nobody had told him not to — he said, "It's an honor to meet you, ma'am. Truly. I'm Tobiah Marsh. I'm on the night shift."

She could not answer. She had not spoken in four days.

"My mother used to take me to hear the Sky Choir," he said, in a rush, keeping pace. "Before. On the Pilgrims' Stair at dawn. When I heard it was to be a Cantor from the Reach I told everyone I'd — well. It's a great honor, ma'am. For all of us." He hesitated. "They say it doesn't hurt very much. After the first part."

Lyra looked at him, at his freckles and his sleepless eyes and his careful hands on the black case, and thought, with a clarity that surprised her: *You are going to remember this morning for the whole of your life, and one day it is going to take the skin off you.*

She smiled at him, because there was nothing else she could give him, and he went scarlet again, and carried the case on down.

---

The cage took them down four hundred feet, and the world changed twice on the way.

First the stone, sliding past in the lamplight, cut and drilled and rung with old quarry-marks. Then clay, grey and sweating, so close she could have touched it. And then the shaft opened, and they came down into the chamber, and Lyra forgot to be afraid, because she had never in her life seen anything so alive.

It was a room made out of a root.

The wall of it curved up and over them like the inside of a bell, grey-brown and ridged, and every ridge had veins in it — green-gold, glowing, thick as her arm at the bottom and fine as thread at the top — and the veins pulsed. Slowly. In, and out. The air was warm and wet and so full of the smell of sap that it coated the back of her mouth like syrup, green and sweet and dizzying, and under the sap was the sharper smell of hot iron.

And in the middle of it hung the bore.

She had seen it from the terraces as a black shape on chains. Down here it was a machine the size of a barn, and its head was buried in the wall of the Root to the collar, and it was turning, and where it went in, the Root bled.

The glass came out slow and thick. It ran down the grey-brown ridges in a broad tongue, guttering into the channels cut in the platform floor, and along the channels to the pumps, where a dozen men in grey smocks worked the handles in pairs, up and down, up and down, with a rhythm like clockwork.

One of them was an old man with white hair. One of them was perhaps nineteen. One of them had a wedding ring on and a scar through his eyebrow, and his lips were moving.

Nobody looked at them. Not the Church, not the Consortium, not the Legionnaires. They were part of the machinery, and you do not look at a pump.

Lyra broke away from the sisters before anyone could stop her, and crossed four yards of platform, and put her hand on the wrist of the man with the wedding ring.

He did not stop pumping. He did not look at her. But she was a Cantor, and her hands knew a body's business the way a carpenter's know wood, and she felt what was underneath: a pulse, and warmth, and a small arrhythmia in his breathing she could have named if she had been allowed to speak.

So she hummed instead.

Barely anything. Not even a note, with her throat as it was — a thread of the Root note, breathed rather than sung, straight down into the bones of his wrist.

The man's lips stopped moving.

For about the length of a breath, he was *there*. She felt it come up through his arm the way you feel a fish take a line. His head turned an inch toward her. His eyes, which had been as empty as a pond, made an effort — an enormous, terrible effort, like a man trying to lift a cart off himself — and something came up behind them nearly to the surface.

Then the sisters had her by both arms and were drawing her back, murmuring *daughter, daughter, the instrument, mind the instrument*, and the man's lips began to move again, and he went on pumping, up and down, up and down.

"Interesting," said Factor Vosk, from the rail. She had her green lenses on and she was looking, not at the man, but at Lyra. "Did you see that, Inquisitor?"

"I saw a Cantor be gracious to a tool," said Ignatius Crane, "which is an error, but a small one." He lifted the censer. "Bring her to the casing."

---

They set a stool for her on the casing platform, ten feet from the bore's collar, where the chain-drums came down and the noise was so great that men had to shout into each other's ears.

And there, sitting on a stool in a white shift with her hands in her lap, with the grinding of the Carrow Bore filling the whole world, Lyra Ashgrove heard, for the first time, what was underneath it.

She had known there would be something. Kaelen had told her, on Rootday, in a voice that made it clear he had never said it aloud to anyone. *There's something in it. I can hear it through the glass. It breathes.*

He had not told her — perhaps he had not known how — that it was singing.

It came up through the platform and through the soles of her feet and into the bones of her legs: enormous, and slow, and so low that it was less a sound than a pressure behind her eyes. Under the grinding. Under the pumps. Under everything.

Five notes.

Down, and down, and up, and a little down again, and back to the beginning. Over and over. A phrase with a shape her hands knew before her mind did, the shape her father's hands had made in the air when he taught it to her in the reed-rows when she was four years old. A tending note held long, and let fall, and gathered.

It was a lullaby.

It was a *Cantor's* lullaby, or the thing that Cantors' lullabies had come down from — the one that every mother in the Reach hums over a cot without knowing where she learned it, the one you use when the child will not settle and you are too tired to be clever, the five easy notes that go around and around until somebody sleeps.

And it kept losing the tune.

That was the part that undid her. Every twelfth or thirteenth time around, one of the five notes would come late, or flat, or not at all, and the phrase would stumble, and there would be a pause — a terrible small pause, like somebody catching themselves at the top of a stair — and then it would start again, from the beginning, patiently.

She knew that sound. She had made that sound herself, in her own kitchen, at four in the morning, with her father dead a month and a feverish baby in her arms and nothing left in her at all, humming five notes over and over because they were the only five she could still hold onto.

She had been brought down four hundred feet in a white shift, with her throat oiled, to hold that steady. To pin it. So the bore could go deeper.

Lyra looked at the wound in the Root, and the tongue of glass coming out of it, and the men pumping it away, and understood three things in the space of about four heartbeats.

That the thing in the stone was not a spirit, and not a law, and not an engine.

That it had been singing a lullaby to something, or someone, or everything, for a very long time, and it was so tired it could not hold the tune.

And that, in a little while, they were going to ask it — in her voice — whether it could stop.

---

Above her, on the catwalk, the Inquisitor began the rubric.

"Holy daughter. You are the hand that steadies the patient. You hold the Light still, that it may be relieved of its excess." The censer swung; the chain creaked at the top of its arc. Somewhere above, at the shaft-head, four hundred feet up, the choir was still holding the Pure Note, and a thread of it came down the shaft like a wire. "Blessed is the single note. Blessed is the throat that holds it. Blessed is the hand that keeps the throat."

The black case was opened on a cloth beside her.

She saw the brass collar, and the four tuning pegs, and the ivory spool of copper wire, and the long silver needle curved like a new moon, lying in its own fitted groove.

"Engineer," said Ignatius Crane.

And Kaelen Vance came across the platform and knelt down on the boards in front of her stool and picked up the needle, and Lyra looked at his hands.

They were shaking.
