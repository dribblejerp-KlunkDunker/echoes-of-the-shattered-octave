## CHAPTER SIXTEEN
### Manifest

> *Registry OK-114. Consignment: twelve crates. The carrier attests that the consignment travelled dry, secure and unopened.*
> *Signature of carrier: (mark).*
>
> — Carriage note, Office of the Catalogue, Greenleaf 417

*The Cloister road sky-dock, Whisper-Grove. Before dawn.*

---

Vane had spent the night doing sums, which was not his trade, and doing them badly.

Twenty-one survivors out of a town of four hundred. Nine children. Two hundred gallons of water for thirty-odd people. Eleven feet of deck-plank pulled up and thrown overboard with a man in it. Four hundred and thirty-two crates' worth of somebody else's people in the hold, twelve crates to a load, eleven loads, and a number underneath all of it that he had refused to total since the first night out of Oakhaven and which had been totalling itself anyway, quietly, in the back of his skull, the way a leak fills a bilge.

He was at the stern rail with the empty bottle when Mags put a hand on his shoulder and pointed down the mast-ladder.

A woman was climbing it. In the dark, in a sodden grey skirt, with one hand on the rungs and the other arm held against her body wrong, and her face — when she came up over the rail and into the lantern-light — was a mess: nose broken, both eyes going black, a split lip, blood dried in a fan down her chin and throat.

At her left elbow, keeping pace with her the whole way up a hundred feet of ladder, an open book hung in the air with nothing holding it.

"Captain Vane," she said. "Registry OK-114."

"That's the ship."

"That is not the ship. That is the *carriage registration*." She put her good hand on the rail and stood breathing for a moment. "The ship has had four names. The registration has had one, and it is on eleven of my consignment notes with my initials beside it. Do you keep your carriage notes?"

"In the chart locker."

"Then you know exactly who I am," said Sable, "and we can save each other an hour."

---

He took her down into the hold.

He did not decide to. He was aware, going down the ladder ahead of her with the lantern, that he had not decided anything; he had simply stood aside from himself and watched a man lead an archivist of the Silent Veil into his own crime, which was a thing he had been half-hoping somebody would make him do since the night he opened the third crate.

The hold was as it always was: pine, straw, cold glass, and the murmur.

Sable stopped at the bottom of the ladder.

She stood with one hand still on the rung and looked down the length of the hold at twelve crates in two stacks, and the sound came up around her — the man counting, the woman giving directions to somewhere, the laughter at a joke told a long time ago, and four hundred other things underneath — and Vane watched her face do something that he did not have a word for and that he did not think he would ever forget. It was not horror. It was recognition. It was the face of somebody meeting a room full of people she knows by name at a party where nobody can see her.

"Bring the lamp," she said.

She went along the stacks reading the stencils. Then she knelt at the third crate, the open one, and looked at the tags on their strings without touching a single sphere, and read them out under her breath the way a woman reads a shelf of preserves in her own larder.

"Forty-one thousand and four. Ivo Renn, forty-one, cooper, of Ashlane; his wife is at the bottom of this row, they were taken within the hour. Forty-one thousand and nine, Ketter Dell, nineteen, fuller's apprentice, who said he was twenty. Forty-one thousand and twelve. Forty-one thousand and thirteen — those are the Aldritch brothers, they came in holding hands and the clerk had to write the second one's details twice because the ink ran." Her voice did not change at all. "Forty thousand nine hundred and ninety-one. That is Mirren Salk, twenty-six, wheelwright's widow. She was carrying her husband's coat and they let her keep it as far as the door."

"You've got the book open," said Vane. His voice came out wrong. "You're reading it off the book."

"The book is at my elbow and it is closed, Captain," said Sable, "and I have not looked at it since I came down that ladder. I wrote seventy-four thousand six hundred and eleven of them in eleven days and I have read twenty of them aloud every night for three years and I am not going to pretend to you that I need a book."

---

She stood up. She put her good hand flat on the lid of the third crate.

"Now," she said. "How many?"

"Four hundred and thirty-two. It's on the—"

"Not in the hold, Captain. How many altogether."

He had known the question was coming since the mast-ladder. He had known it, if he was honest, since a clerk with ink on his cuffs had counted his crates at Oakhaven and said *they do carry on, don't they*, pleasantly, and written twelve in a tally-book.

"Thirteen hundred," said Vane. "About."

"One thousand three hundred and forty-two," said Sable.

The murmur went on around them in the straw.

"Eleven consignments between Greenleaf of 417 and Ashfall of this year," she said. "Twelve crates, then sixteen, then twenty-four and twenty-four and twenty-four and twenty-four; the carriage office increased your load after the fourth run because you had not complained about the noise. There is a note to that effect in the file. It says: *carrier OK-114 is reliable and does not ask questions.*"

Vane put his hand out and found a crate and sat down on it.

"I want to do something for you," said Sable, "and I want you to understand before I do it that it is not a kindness, and that I have thought about it for the whole of the climb up your ladder, and that I am going to do it anyway."

"Do what?"

"Read you three."

"I don't—"

"You have been carrying one thousand three hundred and forty-two people for two years as *cargo*," she said, "and the reason you were able to do it is that cargo does not have a mother or a trade or a last thing it said at a door. I can give you three of them back. Not to them. To you. It will not help them in the slightest and it will not absolve you in the slightest, and you will carry them for the rest of your life, and that is the point, Captain. Somebody should be carrying them properly."

Vane sat on a crate in his own hold with an empty bottle in his hand and looked at a woman with a broken nose in the lantern-light.

"Go on, then," he said.

---

The Bound Ledger came round in front of her and opened itself, and she did not look at it.

"Entry thirty-nine thousand, eight hundred and two," said Sable. "Corwin Aye. Fifty-three. Cooper of the Ashlane, and a good one; there is a barrel in the Moot-house cellar with his mark burned into the head and it has not leaked in nineteen years. Taken on the fourth day of the harvest, in the ninth hour. He was pressed in the second room because the first room had a queue. What he said at the door was: *will I be able to keep my hands?*" She paused exactly long enough. "He carried in the second consignment, Captain. Your sixteen crates. He was in your hold for eleven days."

Vane did not move.

"Entry forty thousand, six hundred and ten. Mirren Salk. Twenty-six. Wheelwright's widow, of the Bell Yard, four months a widow when the drills came. Taken on the ninth day. She was carrying her husband's coat over her arm and she asked whether she might keep it, and the clerk said that she might, and it was taken off her at the door of the pressing room and put in a bin with eleven hundred other coats." Sable's voice stayed perfectly flat. "She is in the stack behind you. Fourth consignment."

The lantern hissed.

"Entry forty-one thousand and six," said Sable. "Annet Sowerby. Six years old. Daughter of Hesper and Tam Sowerby of the Cloister road. Taken on the eleventh day, in the hour after noon, with the rest of the little ones from the Cloister of the Lost Choir, because the Office had a requisition for small clear voices and the Cloister had forty-one of them in a choir under one roof." A beat. "She was third in the line. She would not let go of the hand of the boy in front of her, and rather than make a scene in front of forty children, the proctors took the two of them into the room together and pressed them in the same hour. I wrote both entries. Hers is legible. His is not, because my hand had begun to shake by then, and I have never been able to read the surname since, and there is no one left in this city to ask."

Vane heard, from somewhere very close by, the sound of a man's breath going out of him and not coming back in.

It was his own.

"She's in the third crate," he said. "Second row, corner. She talks to a boy. He's seven. He's asleep against that crate right now."

"I know," said Sable. "Your engineer told me. It is why I came up the ladder."

---

He got up at some point and stood with both hands on the edge of the open crate and made himself look at thirty-six spheres of lilac glass in good straw packed by somebody careful.

"Can it be undone?" he said.

"I don't know."

"You said you were the list, not the cure. Fine. Somebody knows."

"Yes."

"Who?"

"The press," said Sable. "There are two of them in the harvest-house four miles north of this dock, in the old malting on the Cloister road, and there are eleven Office staff in that building, and a proctor limped out of the Weeping Plaza four hours ago who will reach it by dawn and tell them the registry number of this ship." She said it without any change of tone at all. "So we have perhaps a day. I have wanted to walk into that building for three years and I have never once been able to make myself do it, and now there is a ship at this dock that can carry a book, and forty-one children of the Cloister choir in your hold, and one of them is talking to a boy through the side of a crate. So I find that I am going to be able to do it after all. That is not courage, Captain. That is arithmetic."

"And when we've got the thing?"

"Then I read the book to it," said Sable, "and we find out what the press does when you turn it the other way round, and whether anything comes back through it, and how much of me it costs to send one. And if the answer is that it costs three, then three people get their names back out of one thousand three hundred and forty-two." She looked at him. "Which is not enough. Which is why I have spent three years not doing it. I would rather you understood that before you decide anything."

Vane turned the empty bottle over in his hands.

"Three out of thirteen hundred," he said.

"One thousand three hundred and forty-two."

"Aye." He set the bottle down on a crate. "Well. I'm a smuggler, and smugglers don't do sums we like the look of. We do the one in front of us and then we do the next one." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "You'll want the press moved. It'll be iron and brass and a stone bed under it, I should think, and bolted, and you'll not carry it out under your arm with a broken wing. That's a ship's job. That's my job."

Sable looked at him for a moment.

"Your ship is down by the head and overloaded and there are twenty-one refugees and nine children on her deck," she said.

"Yes."

"And you are going to fly her at an Office building."

"I'm going to fly her *near* an Office building," said Vane, "which is a different thing entirely, and which I will explain to you at some length when I've worked out what the difference is."

---

Pim Carder was awake when they came back down the hold.

He had not been asleep for a while, Vane suspected; he had the particular rigid stillness of a child who has been listening to adults and has understood some of it. He sat up in the straw with his hands out stiff in front of him and looked at the woman with the broken face and the book floating at her elbow with no fear whatever, because he was seven and had already seen the worst thing in the world twice.

"Are you the one who writes the names?" he said.

Sable stopped.

"Yes," she said.

"Annet's frightened," said Pim. "She can hear people moving about but she can't hear what anyone says unless you're right up on the box. I told her we were going home and she said which home." He looked up at Sable. "Can you tell her? She'd believe you. You've got the book."

The archivist of the Silent Veil stood in the hold of a smuggler's skyship for a moment with her one good hand at her side.

Then she got down on her knees in the straw — awkwardly, one-handed, with her ribs and her face and her arm — and leaned in until her mouth was a hand's width from the pine of the third crate, and the Ledger came down and hung beside her head, and she said, in the flat clear voice of a woman reading a will:

"Annet Sowerby. You are six years old. Your mother is Hesper and your father is Tam, and you live on the Cloister road, and you sang alto in the little choir under Mistress Dray, and you would not let go of the hand of the boy in front of you." She stopped, and her voice did not change, but she put her hand flat on the wood. "You are entry forty-one thousand and six. You are not lost. You are *filed*. And I am four miles from the place where they put you in there, and I am coming with the ship."

Inside the crate, quite close, a small voice said:

"*Is that you, Mistress Dray?*"

Sable shut her eyes.

"No, love," she said. "It's the clerk."
