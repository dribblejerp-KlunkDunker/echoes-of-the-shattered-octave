## CHAPTER THREE
### Burl

> *The patient must not be permitted to cry out. Administer the mute-drops a quarter hour before the first incision, and remind the family that silence is a mercy extended to the whole street.*
>
> — *Handbook of the Grafting Office*, Oakhaven Works

*The Hollow. Rootday.*

---

Lyra went to the Carders' before dawn.

She had told Nell Hearthday. She had lain awake after the Hedge, after the forty-first night, after she had told the voice everything, and listened to the hush-lamp and counted the hours to Rootday. An hour before first light she got up and put on her shawl and went out, because Hearthday had been a lie, and she had known it when she said it.

Nell opened the blue door at the first soft knock. She had not been asleep either.

Pim had. He lay curled on the pallet by the hearth with his hands held out stiff in front of him even in sleep, the way he'd been taught, and his roots had grown in the night. Not back to what they had been. But longer. Reaching, very slowly, for the damp of the hearthstones.

Lyra knelt beside him, cupped his small hands in hers, and began.

The falling fifth. The Root note, and down, slow as a leaf; and up; and down. In the Narrow, into the hollow of her own palms. She did not wake him. A sleeping child's body did not fight you. She sang for most of an hour, while the window went from black to grey, and the roots came down. A half-step. Then — she had not known it could — a whole step. The seams at his fingertips drew closed over the white hair-roots like mouths closing over secrets, until all that showed was a faint pale line beneath each nail, like the mark a ring leaves.

By the time she stopped, the sky was pink and Nell was crying in the corner without a sound, and when Lyra tried to say *there*, nothing came out of her throat at all.

Not a whisper. Not a rasp. Air.

She put her hand to her throat. It was hot, and it hurt the way a torn muscle hurts, all the way down.

*Every mending borrows.*

She had borrowed everything.

"Lyra—" Nell whispered.

Lyra shook her head. She found a stub of chalk on the mantel and wrote on the hearthstone, in a hand that shook more than she liked: KEEP HIS HANDS WARM. DRY. WHEN THEY LOOK, HE HOLDS STILL.

Nell read it and nodded, and nodded, and could not stop nodding.

---

The Grafters came up the quarry road at the second hour.

They came in a long white wagon drawn by two grey horses whose harness had been wrapped in felt so that nothing on them jingled, and whose hooves were booted in leather. On the wagon-bed rode a brazier of banked coals in an iron cage, a rack of instruments under oilcloth, and three Grafters in white. A Carillon Legionnaire walked at the tailboard with its bell lifted to the sky.

Lyra watched them come from the mouth of the Hollow, where the lane dipped toward the tannery. She had watched them come every second Rootday for three years, and she had never got used to how quiet they were.

The Grafters wore long aprons of white leather over white smocks, white gloves to the elbow, and masks: a shell of stiffened leather over the whole face, with round glass eyepieces and, over the mouth, a lattice of fine wire, so that their breathing came through as a soft continuous hiss, exactly in the Narrow. They trained in the Organum to work in the Narrow. It was said they could take a leg off at the hip without making a sound louder than a kettle.

It was true. Lyra had seen it.

The lead Grafter climbed down from the wagon and, as he always did when he saw her, unbuckled his mask and took it off.

Surgeon-Grafter Anselm Rooke was fifty or near it, with a long clever face creased like old paper, ink on his cuffs, and eyes that had been tired for a very long time. He had come to the Reach from the Organum four years ago. In those four years he had cut more limbs off more people than any butcher in the Reach had cut off pigs, and he had done every one of them with enormous care.

"Cantor," he said.

She nodded.

He looked at her face a moment. Then at her throat. His mouth thinned.

"Ah," he said softly. "You went ahead of me."

She nodded again.

It was an arrangement they had, she and Rooke, which neither of them had ever said aloud. She went ahead of him. He went slowly.

"Well," he said. "Let's see what you've left me."

---

He went house by house down the Hollow with his sawboys behind him and his instruments under his arm, and she went with him, because she always did, because somebody had to hold their heads.

Hob Wendle first: a field-hand of forty, big-shouldered and sheepish, whose roots had come out through the soles of his feet in the summer, so that for weeks he had walked on them without knowing it and left little white threads in the dust of every lane. Three of his toes had gone to wood. Rooke studied them through a brass lens for a long time, and pressed them with a gloved thumb, and listened to the pressing — Lyra had never known what he listened for — and then nodded to a sawboy, who brought the brown bottle.

The mute-drops. Three on the tongue, and a quarter hour. Hob's eyes went glassy and calm, and then his throat worked, and worked, and nothing came out of it, not even the sound of breath. The drops did not take the pain. They took the voice. The Grafting Office was very clear about that.

Lyra held his head against her shoulder while they did it. The hush-saw was a fine-toothed thing with a felted grip and a blade so thin it flexed, and it went through the wooden toes with a sound like someone whispering *shh* into a pillow. The sap-iron came after, from the brazier, and that was the only true sound of the morning — a small sharp hiss, and the smell, green and scorched — and Hob's whole body went rigid against her, his mouth wide open, perfectly silent, his fingers digging into her back hard enough to leave marks she would find that night.

She could not hum to him. She had nothing to hum with. She pressed her lips to his hair and breathed the Root note, soundless, only air in the shape of the note, and she did not know whether he could feel it.

Afterward he wept with his mouth open and no sound came out, and his wife held him.

"Good," Rooke said quietly, peeling off his gloves. "Clean. He'll walk."

---

Then the Carders.

Nell had Pim sitting on the hearth-rug with his hands resting on a hot brick wrapped in flannel. When the Grafters came in, he looked at the masks, and the glass eyepieces, and the hissing wire mouths, and his lip began to shake, and he did not move.

Rooke knelt in front of him. He had not put his mask back on; he never wore it for the children. He took Pim's hands very gently by the wrists and turned them to the window and bent over them with his brass lens, one finger at a time.

It took a long time.

Lyra stood by the door with her fists in her shawl and did not breathe.

"Early," Rooke said at last. He was looking at the faint pale lines beneath Pim's nails. "Very early." He turned the small left hand over, and back. "And receding, I'd have said. If I didn't know better." He glanced over his shoulder at Lyra by the door.

She looked back at him.

"Unusual," said Rooke, after a moment. He let go of the boy's wrists and made a mark in his book. "Recheck next month. Keep him out of the damp." He stood, his knees cracking, and patted Pim on the head in passing — a dry, awkward, fatherly pat — and went out.

Nell made a sound behind her hand that was very nearly above the Narrow, and caught it, and folded over it. Pim looked from his mother to Lyra and back again without understanding, and Lyra went to him and knelt and put her arms around him and held on.

---

Then Orla Hale.

Mother Hale was seventy-three. She had delivered half the Hollow before Bettany took over, and she had sung the harvest home every autumn for fifty years in a voice like a rusty gate that everyone in Oakhaven loved. Her roots had started in the spring, in her fingertips, the way Pim's had. She had been too proud to let anyone see and too poor to stop working, and by the time her son Davin found her sitting very still in her chair by the window one evening at midsummer, unable to lift her hands, it was far too late.

She was in the chair still.

She had grown into it. Her hands lay along its arms, and her fingers had split and put out roots, and the roots had gone into the oak of the arms and through them and down the legs and into the floorboards, a pale web of them, fine as lace, pinning her where she sat. And the chair, which had been dead wood for forty years, had answered. Along its arms and up its back, small green shoots had broken through the varnish, and a few had put out leaves, tender and new, the color of spring.

It was, Lyra thought every time she saw it, the most beautiful and the most terrible thing in the Hollow.

"Here's my girl," said Orla Hale, and smiled with half her mouth. "And the sawbones. Come in, come in. I'd get up, but." She lifted her eyebrows at her hands, which could not lift. "I'm afraid I've put down roots."

She said it every time. Every time, Rooke smiled at it.

Not this time. He knelt by her chair and took a brass ear-trumpet from his bag and set its bell to her chest, below the collarbone, and listened. Lyra watched his face. She watched it go still.

She could hear it herself from the doorway, now that she knew to listen. Under Orla's breathing, which was slow and even and peaceful, there was another sound. A dry sound. A faint whispering rustle, in and out with every breath, like wind moving through a field of barley.

Roots. In the lungs.

Rooke took the trumpet from her chest and sat back on his heels and said nothing. He did not need to. He reached into his apron and took out a stick of chalk.

Green chalk.

*Let me try.*

Lyra had crossed the room before she knew it. She was kneeling at the other side of the chair with her hands on Orla's rooted hands and her face close to Orla's chest, and she opened her mouth and pulled up everything she had from the very bottom of herself for the falling fifth—

And nothing came.

A rasp. A dry, cracked scrape of air, like a bow drawn across a slack string. She tried again, and her throat seized, and she tasted blood.

She had spent it. Before dawn, on a hearth-rug, in the dark. She had spent every note she had on Pim Carder's fingers without once thinking there might be somebody else, and here was somebody else, and she had nothing left to give her.

*Every mending borrows.*

She had not known, until this moment, that it could borrow from other people.

"Don't, lovey," said Orla Hale.

She could not lift her hands. So she leaned her head sideways instead, until her grey cheek rested on the top of Lyra's head, and stayed there.

"It doesn't hurt," she said, very softly. "I thought it would. It doesn't. It's like—" She searched for it. "It's like being held. From underneath. Like the whole of the ground's got its arms round me." A soft rustling breath. "I'm not frightened. I was, in the spring. I'm not now."

Lyra shut her eyes.

"But you'll sing me down," Orla said. "When it's time. Won't you? Proper, mind. Not in a jar. Not in the Narrow. Proper, the way I sang the harvest home." A breath. "You promise me."

Lyra could not speak. She nodded against the old woman's cheek.

"Good girl," said Orla Hale.

Behind them, quietly, the Surgeon-Grafter got to his feet, and went to the door, and drew a green circle on the outside of it with a line struck through.

---

In the lane afterward, Lyra caught his sleeve.

She could not ask. She pointed at the door behind them, at the green mark, and looked at him.

Rooke looked at the mark too. He put his gloves in his apron pocket and did not put his mask back on.

"The sap-mark," he said. "It means *past cutting*. That's all it has ever meant. It meant I would come back each month and keep her comfortable, and at the end, when it was time, I'd give her the long drops and you would sing, and her son would bury her." He spoke very low, even for the Narrow. "The Inquisitor has asked for the list."

She stared at him.

"He's four days out," Rooke said. "He carries a warrant from the High Patriarch for the purification of the Hollow, and the warrant names the marked houses. My marks." He looked down at his hands, clean and white and very steady. "I have been told," he said, "that if I stop making them, someone will be sent to make them for me. Someone less careful."

She let go of his sleeve.

"My saws leave people alive," said Surgeon-Grafter Rooke. "At least there's that. Most days there's that."

He buckled his mask back on over his tired face, and the wire lattice took his breath and made it a hiss, and he walked on down the lane to the next house, and his sawboys followed with the brazier.

At the end of the lane, on the step of the house with the red shutters, Lissa Merrow sat in the sun. She was eleven. Her forearms ended in neat white bandages a hand's width below the elbow, and she was holding a tin cup of water between them, pressed carefully from both sides, and drinking from it, slowly, without spilling a drop. She watched the Grafters go by with an expression of perfect, terrible calm.

Lyra made herself look at her until the Grafters had passed. Then she walked home.

---

It was a strange thing, to be a Cantor with no voice.

People came to the door all afternoon. A carter's wife with a feverish baby. The sexton, about a burial on Massday. One of the Tanner cousins, pale and shaking, because her mother had begun to hear a ringing in her ears, and wasn't that the first sign of glassing, didn't Lyra think, and couldn't she come and listen? Lyra stood in the doorway and opened her mouth and shook her head, and pointed at her throat, and wrote on a slate, and watched each of them understand, and watched each of them go away with the look of people who have come to a well and found it dry.

In the evening, washing at the basin, she found two root-hairs growing from the crease of her right palm. She pulled them one after the other. The second bled green for a long time.

She was sitting at the table with the hand wrapped in a rag and a cup of cold tea in front of her when someone knocked on the door.

Not the soft Oakhaven knock, two knuckles, one tap. Three raps, firm, the way people knocked in towns where knocking was not a risk.

She knew who it was before she opened it. She could hear the other sound under the knock. High, thin, and fast. Much faster than usual.

---

He stood on her step in his good coat, with his arm in its felt sleeve, and behind him the lane was going blue with dusk, and the lamplighters had not yet come.

"The gate-warden let me in," he said. "Before you ask. The gate opens for Consortium glass now." He held up a disc of green glass stamped with the drill-and-wheel, then put it back in his pocket. "Which tells you everything you need to know about who owns the gate."

She looked at him.

He had not been inside the Hedge after dark since there was a Hedge. He had not been to this door in seven years. She had gone to him — to the stile, to the clearing, a dozen times, a hundred — and he had never once come to her, and she had told herself it was because of the town, and the gate, and the hounds, and she had known it was not.

He was looking past her, at her father's chair.

She stepped back and let him in.

He stood in the middle of the room as though he did not know where to put himself. He looked at the chair, and the notebooks on the shelf, and the seven wind-reed pipes on their pegs by the door, and she saw him recognize them, and saw it go into him like a splinter. Under the felt, the arm was ringing so fast and so high that the reed in the hush-lamp began to buzz in sympathy.

His jaw was set so hard she could see the muscle jump.

She came and stood in front of him.

"You're doing it again," she whispered. It was all she had, a whisper, torn at the edges. The afternoon had given a little of it back. "Clenching your jaw until your molars splinter. You think if you don't make a sound, the pain doesn't exist."

"Save your voice."

"Haven't got one." She held out her hand. "Give me your wrist."

"Lyra—"

"Give. Me. Your. Wrist."

He gave it to her.

She unlaced the felt sleeve at the shoulder and drew it down, and the room filled with the sound of the arm: a high, clear, unending note, like a finger on the rim of a wine glass, so pure it made her back teeth ache. In the lamplight the glass was the color of the sky on a winter morning, pale and blue and perfectly clear, and inside it she could see the long shadows of his bones, the two bones of the forearm and the little bones of the hand strung like beads. It was beautiful. It was always beautiful. That was one of the worst things about it.

At the shoulder, where the glass ended, the frost of the tideline had crept inward past the joint, along the collarbone, onto his chest.

She had seen it at midsummer. It had barely cleared the joint then.

She looked up. He had seen her see it.

Neither of them said anything.

She had no voice to hum with. So she did the only other thing she could think of. She lifted his glass hand in both of hers, and bent her head, and set her lips to the inside of the glass wrist, where a pulse would have been, and breathed the Root note into it. Not sound. Only breath, shaped the way the note was shaped. One hundred and seventy-four. The first note her father had taught her, and the note she had taught Kaelen on a stile when he was nine and she was eight and he had been so hopeless at it.

The glass took it.

She felt it go in: a warmth under her mouth, and then a color, a faint green warmth rising through the pale blue like dye through water, up the wrist and the forearm and into the elbow, and wherever it went the ringing slowed. Softened. Came down and down, from a scream to a keening to a low clear hum, the way a struck bell comes down when you lay a hand on it.

Kaelen let out a breath that went on for a long time. His shoulders dropped an inch.

"I can still hear it," he said, very low. "The day it broke. Every time it's bad, I hear it again." He was looking at his own arm, green-shot in her hands. "It didn't sound like thunder. Everyone thinks it must have. It sounded like a wine glass snapping inside your ear."

She waited.

He said nothing else.

She had never asked. Not since the stile, that first winter. She had told herself it was kindness, and some of it was. She did not ask now. She drew the felt back up over the quieter glass and laced it at the shoulder, and pushed him down into her own chair — not her father's — and poured him the cold tea, and sat down across the table, and waited.

---

He told her about the Greenhall.

He told it like an engineer, in order, without adjectives: the Legionnaire at the gate, the Factor with the green lenses, the bore plan, the nine days. Hearthday next. The harmonic slippage, the three shattered bore-heads, the two lost shifts — *attrition*, the Factor had called them. The Consortium's offer. The Inquisitor four days out with a warrant for the Hollow.

Then he told her about Stage Nine.

"*Source: Cantor, local*," he said. "*One*." He looked at her across the table. "There's one Cantor left in the Reach, Lyra."

She had known where it was going since he said *unison*. It still went into her like cold water.

"For four hours," she whispered.

"They'll put a collar on your throat," Kaelen said, "and a silver needle through your vocal folds, with a copper wire behind it. They'll tune the wire to the Root note, and after that you won't be able to make any other sound. Not a word. Not a scream. Every breath you take will come out as one hundred and seventy-four. And after four hours, or when the casing yields, which could be much longer, they'll cut the collar away and draw the wire back out." He stopped. "It doesn't come out, Lyra. It tears."

The hush-lamp whistled in the silence.

"How do you know?" she said.

"I've seen it."

"Where?"

He did not answer at once. She watched him decide how much to say.

"Sol-Luminary," he said.

*The year it broke.* The words hung unsaid between them. She could have said them. She could have leaned across the table and asked him, at last, what he had been doing at the Dawning Spire when the sky went pale, and what he had been supposed to hold together, and why he had never once come to her door. She felt the question in her mouth, whole and ready.

She saw him see it there. She saw him brace.

"Who did you see it done to?" she whispered instead.

Something in his face gave way, very slightly.

"A girl," he said. "In the choir. She stood on the top tier, at the end. She'd have been nineteen, maybe twenty. On the ninth morning her collar was bleeding. Down the front of her shift. Not a lot. Just — steadily. I came down to check the resonance at the risers, and I saw it, and I—" He stopped.

"And you?"

"I went back up the gantry," said Kaelen.

He said nothing more about her, and Lyra did not ask.

"I'm going to break the bore," he said after a while, in his engineer's voice again. "Before Stage Nine. At the casing, where the Sequence runs narrowest. I know where it's weak. I wrote the thing. I know where it's weak better than anyone alive. There's a dampener coil in the mechanic's depot at the quarry — a reverse-phase coil, they use them to bleed off slippage — and if I can get it into the bore-head's resonance chamber at the right moment, it'll throw the lock. The bit seizes. The Sequence fails. They can't restart without a new bore-head from the Organum, and that's a season at the least."

"And then?"

"And then they've no reason to wire anyone." He laid his right hand flat on the table. "There's a skyship at the quarry mast. A smuggler with a Consortium contract. I'm going to buy passage south. Three berths. You, and Nell Carder, and the boy."

She stared at him.

"The Inquisitor will be here in four days," Kaelen said. "With a list. You know whose names are on it. Pim's hands—"

"Pim's clean," she whispered. "Rooke passed him. This morning."

"For this month. He'll be marked in one month or two, and you know it, and so does Rooke. Get them out. Get yourself out. Go south, where the sky's still—"

"And you?"

"Somebody has to be in the shaft when it breaks."

"No."

"Lyra."

"*No*." It came out torn, and too loud, louder than the Narrow, a cracked scrape of a word. They both froze, and listened. Outside the window the hush-lamps whistled and the oaks were still and nothing beyond the Hedge lifted its head. She brought her voice back down to nothing. "No. I'm not getting on a pirate's bucket and flying south while Orla Hale burns in her chair. I sat with her this morning. She's rooted into it, Kael, right down through the floor. They can't move her. They'll have to burn the house around her." Her whisper shook. "And I'm not leaving you in a hole in the ground with a machine you built, so you can die underneath it and call it paying."

"I'm not—"

"You are. You've been doing it for seven years. You're only doing it faster now." She leaned across the table. "You don't get to carry this one alone. You don't get to carry any more of them alone."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"The coil might not be enough," he said at last, slowly. "If the Sequence is running on a unison hold, even a partial one, the lock will be too strong for a coil alone. It would need a counter-note. Something pulling against the bore from the other side. Something live."

"A Cantor's voice."

"Yes." He said it as though it cost him. "If you had one."

"I'll have one," said Lyra. "It comes back. It always comes back."

"And the berths?"

"Buy them," she said.

Which was not the same as saying she would use one, and they both knew it. She watched him hear the gap. She watched him decide not to push. It was, she thought, the first time in their lives they had lied to each other in the same moment, both knowing it, and let it stand.

---

He rose to go before the Listening Hour. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at the room — the chair, the notebooks, the pipes — and she saw his jaw begin to set again, and saw him catch it.

And under his breath, the way he always did when something hurt and he thought no one was listening, he hummed.

Low. So low it was nearly under the floor. The Root note, the one she had taught him on the stile. He had it perfectly now. And as he hummed it the glass arm answered, the way glass answers a voice, and a thin high ring went up inside the low note and threaded through it like a silver wire drawn through wool. A low warm drone, with a bright cold ring at the heart of it.

Lyra went still.

She had heard that sound before.

Not from him. Not in this room, or on the stile, or in the clearing. She had heard it in the dark, at the Hedge, behind the cooperage — on some of the forty-one nights, not all of them. Under her father's voice. A low drone running beneath his words the way a Cantor's drone runs beneath a song, with a thin cold ring threaded through its heart. She had thought it was the thorn creaking. She had thought it was the oaks, chiming somewhere far off.

It was not the oaks.

"What?" said Kaelen.

She had to tell him. She opened her mouth to tell him, and understood, in the same instant, everything she would have to say after it. *I've heard your note at the Hedge. I've heard it because I go there. I go there at the dead hour and sit on a stone with my back to the thorn and talk to it, and it talks back in Da's voice, and I tell it everything. I've told it about you.*

She closed her mouth.

"Nothing," she whispered. "Go home, Kael. Before the Hour."

He looked at her a moment longer, the way the Factor with the green lenses must have looked at him, as if he could see something through her that was a different color from her face. Then he nodded, and went.

---

She did not go to the Hedge that night.

It was the first night in forty-two that she did not. She lay in the loft in her clothes with her wrapped hand on her chest and listened to the hush-lamp whistle, and the night-hummers change at the crossings, and the bore grinding on and on beneath everything, and she did not get up.

At the dead hour, it began.

Very faint. From the direction of the cooperage wall, a street and a half away, under the whistling of every lamp in Oakhaven. She would not have heard it if she had not been listening for it. She would not have known what it was if she had not heard it, three hours earlier, in her own doorway.

A low hum. The Root note, perfectly held. And threaded through the heart of it, thin and bright and cold, the ring of glass.

It did not call her name. It did not use her father's voice. It did not need to. It only hummed, low and patient, the way you would hum to a child who was pretending to be asleep, over and over, all through the dead hour and on toward dawn.

Lyra lay very still in the dark and let it, and did not go, and understood for the first time what she had been talking to for forty-one nights.

It had not been waiting for her.

It had been practicing.
