## ECHO I
### The Thread Where She Answered

> *A loom has no opinion about the cloth. That is the first thing they teach you at the Sandglass, and it is a lie told to children, and they know it is a lie, and they teach it anyway, because the alternative is to tell a nineteen-year-old that he is going to spend his life watching.*
>
> — Ilias Marren, exile of the Loom-Wardens

---

You will want to know who is talking.

My name is Ilias Marren and I was a Warden of the Loom at the Sandglass Bazaar until they put me out of it, and I am not in this story yet. I will come into it in the second book, in a glass city that is living the same twenty-four seconds over and over, and you will like me, I think, for a while.

I am going to show you a thread that was not taken.

Understand what that means before you go any further. This did not happen. It also did not *un*happen; nothing does. Every road not taken is still standing out there in the dark with the lamps lit and people walking up and down it, and the only difference between that road and this one is that you are on this one, and the thing that decides which road you are on is almost always small, and almost always a Tuesday.

In the thread you have just read, Lyra Ashgrove went out to the cooperage wall on the forty-first night, and sat on her stone, and told a monster about a child's hands.

On the forty-second night — the night Kaelen Vance came to her door and hummed his note in her kitchen and she recognized it — she did not go.

That is the whole of the difference. One night.

Here is the thread where she went.

---

She goes out at the dead hour, because she always does, and she sits down with her back to the thorn, and it says *there's my lark*, and she says *hello, Da*, and it asks about her day.

And on this night — because the difference between the threads is one night, and this thread is one night further along than the one you know — it asks her something it has not asked before.

*Come through,* it says. *Lark. There's a gap down by the cooperage drain, where the water goes under. It's not even a squeeze. Come through and sit with me a bit. It's cold out here and I've been on my own two years.*

And she says no.

She says no eleven times over the next three hours, which I want you to know, because it matters later and nobody in that thread ever finds out. She is not weak and she is not a fool; she is a thirty-three-year-old woman who has been doing triage for two years with nothing in the account, and the thing on the other side of the thorn has her father's voice and has been practising for seven hundred and thirty nights.

On the twelfth time, it says: *All right, lark. Then I'll come through to you.*

And she hears the thorn move.

And she is so frightened — not of dying; of *that*, of the shape of it coming through the hedge into Oakhaven where the Carders are, where Bettany is, where ninety-one children are asleep — that she stands up and says the only thing she can think of that will keep it out there.

*Don't. I'm coming.*

---

I have watched that moment four hundred and eleven times.

I want to be precise with you, because precision is the only decency available to me: in two hundred and six of those threads she stays on her side of the thorn. In a hundred and ninety-five she does not. The variable is not courage. It is whether she slept the night before, and whether the Carder boy's roots came back that afternoon, and whether old Tam was humming at the Weavers' Cross as she passed, which is a thing that depends on the state of his knees, which depends on the weather in a week that is now three years gone.

That is what a thread is. You are a soup, and a thing in the dark is patient, and some nights you have had enough sleep.

---

She goes through the drain on her hands and knees.

It takes eleven seconds. I am not going to describe what is waiting, because you have met it now, and because there is a difference between dread and cruelty and I am trying to stay on the right side of it.

I will tell you the part that matters.

It does not kill her. It does not, in any sense you would recognize, *hurt* her. It takes four minutes, and what comes back through the drain into Oakhaven at the end of those four minutes walks up Cooper's Row in Lyra Ashgrove's boots and lets itself into Lyra Ashgrove's house and lies down in her bed, and in the morning it gets up and goes to look at Pim Carder's hands.

And it is very good.

That is the thing nobody in that thread ever works out, and I have watched them not work it out four hundred and eleven times. It has her whole voice. It has two years of her nights — every patient, every fear, every small stupid thing she told it on a stone behind a cooperage. It knows the falling fifth. It knows that Nell Carder takes two sugars and that Bettany's hip hurts in the wet and that the Tanner cousins are not speaking. It is kind, because kindness is a sound and it has heard a great deal of it.

It cannot sing the Root note into a clay jar and make a baby breathe. But in a town where the Cantor has been running on empty for two years, nobody notices for nine days that the mending has stopped working, and by then it does not matter.

---

Here is the rest of it, quickly, because you have a book to finish.

Kaelen Vance comes to her door on the forty-second night with a bore plan under his arm and a Stage Nine to tell her about, and the thing wearing her face lets him in, and he sits at her table and tells it everything — the Sequence, the coil, the phase port, the eight bolts, the forty seconds — and it listens with her eyes, and asks the questions she would have asked, and at the door he hums his note and it does not recognize it, because there is nothing in there that was ever eight years old on a stile.

He goes away believing he has a plan and an ally.

On Hearthday they take her down four hundred feet in a white shift and put a brass collar round her throat, and Kaelen Vance kneels in front of her and threads a wire through a fold of skin instead of the fold of her voice, and his hands do not shake at all, because the woman on the stool is perfectly calm and that steadies him.

Then the Hold begins, and the thing in the stone draws in and listens.

And the thing on the stool, which eats voices and has never in its existence had the slightest reason to care what a dying god does with its law, holds the note.

It holds it beautifully. It holds it for four hours and nineteen minutes with blood on its collar, and nobody shouts *sing it down*, because the engineer at the collar walk is waiting for a signal from an ally who is not there, and when he finally understands — at four hours and eleven minutes, by her face, by something behind her eyes that is not fear — he goes for the drum anyway, and gets five bolts out of eight before Factor Vosk's Legionnaires put him on the boards.

The Root is relieved.

It has been singing a five-note lullaby for ten thousand years and a voice outside it holds its note steady for four hours and nineteen minutes, and it believes — the way the Light believed — that the thing it was promised has come.

It lets go.

The Greening in that thread is not four miles across. It is the whole Resonant Reach, from the Flume to the Greenroot road to the edge of the Shifting Dunes, in eleven days: the second Fracture, Growth following Light, and the Arbiterium's grey folder vindicated at a scale that makes a Supreme Arbiter weep with relief in a locked room at the Organum, which I have also watched, and which I would not have believed if I had only heard about it.

Kaelen Vance dies under the purge, in the vault, with his father's voice coming out of the speaking-tube counting down from sixty.

Vane cuts the mast-line at the quarry and runs, because there is nobody at the North Gate worth going back for, and lives another nine years, and drinks, and dies on the Rust Coast of something ordinary.

Sable reads twenty names a night in a drowned plaza until the Office finds her in the spring.

And the thing wearing Lyra Ashgrove goes on walking around inside the Greening, singing, for a very long time, because there is a great deal to eat.

---

Four hundred and eleven times.

I have a reason for showing you this one, and it is not the one you think. It is not *look how lucky they are*. Luck is not a thing I have ever been able to find in the cloth; I have looked.

It is this.

In the thread you are reading — the one where she did not go out on the forty-second night — she is alive at the end of Book One, and so are four hundred and thirty-two people in a hold, and so is a child in a crate who is frightened of the dark. And every single one of those outcomes rests on a woman recognizing a low note with a ring in its heart, in her own kitchen, at the end of a day in which she had already spent everything she had.

That is all. That is the whole margin. A tired woman heard something, and did not go out.

I have watched a great many threads, and I can tell you with some authority that the world is never saved by the people who are fresh.

---

One more thing, and then I will leave you to the second book.

You should know that I am going to be in this story, and that I am going to be useful, and that I am going to like them — the Cantor especially, who reminds me of somebody, and the archivist, who does what I do and has not yet discovered what it costs.

And you should know that in every thread where I say nothing at the right moment, they reach the Organum together and all of them are taken into the Hush, and are a very long time being digested, and the world ends in the way that does not have a sound.

In one thread, somebody sells them at the gate.

Some of them live.

I have looked at four hundred and eleven of these, and I have not yet found a third option, and I am still looking, and I would like you to remember, when we get there, that I was looking.

*— the Tide-Weaver*
