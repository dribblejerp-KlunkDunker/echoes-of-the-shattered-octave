## PROLOGUE
### The Cracked Bell

> *Q. What is holiness?*
> *A. One note, held without end.*
> *Q. What is sin?*
> *A. The second note.*
>
> — Catechism of the Pure Note

*Sol-Luminary. The Siphon Foundry at the foot of the Dawning Spire.*
*The three hundredth sunrise of the Rite of Pure Unison. 412 SR.*

---

On the three hundredth morning of the Rite, Solenne Aubade woke already singing.

She had stopped noticing somewhere around the eightieth sunrise, when the wire took over. That was what the Tuning Men had promised the day they threaded it. *After a while, girl, you won't have to try. The copper remembers for you. You breathe; it sings.* They had said it kindly, the way a groom tells a horse the bit will stop hurting once the mouth goes numb.

The mouth had not gone numb. But she no longer had to try.

She lay on her pallet in the choir-loft with her eyes shut and listened to herself: the note leaving her on every breath, thin and exact, five hundred and twenty-eight beats to the second, the holy number, the Light's own pitch. She counted the voices around her in the dark. Thirty-nine. There were supposed to be forty. Ilse had died on the risers on the two hundred and eleventh morning, still singing, and the Inquisitor had had her carried out without the note so much as wavering, and the line had closed over the gap the way water closes over a dropped stone.

At night the loft sounded like a hive. Thirty-nine sleeping girls, humming. Solenne had lain awake through the early weeks listening to it, sick with it, and then one night she realized she could no longer remember what the loft had sounded like before, and after that she slept.

When she had tried to speak (she did not anymore, but in the first weeks she had tried) the words came out on the same pitch, every syllable pressed flat onto the one note like a flower in a book. You could say *I love you* or *I am dying* or *please*, and the wire made them all the same prayer. So the girls had built a hand-speech instead, small and quick, done under the hems of their white shifts where the Flambeaux could not see. Solenne's hands had said a great many things in three hundred days.

Her mouth had said one.

---

The Siphon Foundry had grown around the foot of the Dawning Spire the way a scab grows around a nail.

It was a cathedral of black iron, ribbed and buttressed, thrown up against the Spire's flank in a single summer by the Consortium of Bores: gantries, catwalks, cable-drums, the long channels in the floor where the glass ran, the tanker bays, the censer platform, the gallery for the nobility, and the risers where the choir stood. Its roof was open. It had to be. There was no roof in the world high enough to close over the thing it had been built against.

The Spire went up out of the mountain, out of the Foundry, out of the clouds, and past the place where the eye gave up. It was white, not the white of marble or of snow but the white of a clean bone, and it was seamless, and it was singing. It had always been singing. Solenne had grown up beneath it, in House Aubade's tall cold rooms on the Upper Tiers, and she had heard it every day of her life without hearing it, the way you do not hear your own blood.

Only on the risers, with her own voice pinned to its pitch, had she begun to hear it properly.

The Church taught that the Spire sang one note: the Pure Note, the Law of Light, five hundred and twenty-eight. The Church was wrong. Under the choir, under the grinding of the bore, under everything, the Spire sang a chord so wide you could only hear its edges, like standing inside a bell someone had struck before the world began and never stopped striking. And inside the chord, if you listened long enough, there was *grain*. Texture. Something like breath. Once, around the hundred and fortieth sunrise, she had heard something in it catch, the way a voice catches on a word it doesn't want to say, and for days afterward she could not stop thinking a thought she had no right to think.

*It sounds tired.*

The bore did not sound tired. The bore sounded like the end of the world being ground very slowly out of stone. The Carrow Bore was thirty storeys of black iron and runework, lowered down the Spire's flank on chains thicker than a man's body. It turned all day and all night like a mill-wheel, and at its tip a crown of glass teeth bit into the white stone and made it bleed.

The blood was blue. Not the blue of the sky, or of a vein, but the blue at the very bottom of a flame. Aether-Glass, running liquid out of the wound, down the cut channels in the Foundry floor and into iron tankers that men in leather aprons wheeled away every hour. The Synod said the glass was surplus: the Light's generosity, overflowing.

Solenne had watched it come out of the Spire for three hundred days. It did not overflow. It came in pulses.

Like something with a heartbeat.

---

The choir's work was to hold the note still.

That was the Consortium's theory, and the Church's blessing on the theory, and the reason forty girls of the Sky Choirs had been brought down from the heights with copper in their throats. A Spire could not simply be drilled. It rang, and a ringing thing shatters under a bit. But if its note were held perfectly steady from outside, if a unison of human voices sang its own pitch back to it without a tremor, it would stand still for the bore the way a patient stands still for a surgeon.

"You are the hand that steadies the patient," Inquisitor Ignatius had told them on the first morning, walking the line of risers with his hands folded. "Holy daughters. You hold the Light still so that it may be relieved of its excess."

He stood on the censer platform above the choir every dawn, a tall man, young for his rank, with a face like a statue someone had loved too hard, in robes the red of a banked coal, and he swung the great brass censer on its chain, back and forth, back and forth. The chain creaked at the top of each arc, and that creak was the metronome of the Rite. Solenne had heard it in her sleep for three hundred nights. She would hear it, she suspected, for the rest of her life.

He believed. That was the worst thing she could say about him, and she'd had three hundred days to find worse.

Above him, behind a rail of gilded iron, was the gallery, and in the gallery, every morning, in white, stood her sister.

Seraphina was sixteen. The Church had chosen them both on the same day, in the same hall, with their mother weeping proudly in the front pew: Seraphina for the Sun-Blade, Solenne for the wire. One daughter to carry the Light, one to hold it still. The Blade lay on a cushion of cloth-of-gold beside Seraphina's knee, waiting for the end of the Rite, a long pale sword the Church had carved from a splinter of the Spire itself. Every morning Seraphina stood beside it through the Morning Swell with her eyes lifted to the Spire and her face as clean and certain as a candle.

And every morning, under the hem of her shift, Solenne's hands said: *Look at me.*

And every morning her sister did not.

---

There was one other note in the Foundry.

It belonged to the engineer.

He came up the gantry every dawn with the Tuning Men to calibrate the bore: a young man, not yet thirty, sleeves rolled to the elbow, soot in the creases of his knuckles, a long steel tuning fork carried the way other men carry a knife. When he worked, when he thought no one could hear him under the roar, he hummed.

Low. So low it was nearly under the floor. A note like wet earth, like a root drinking. A *Reach* note, her mother would have said, with a sniff; the Reach, where they still sang to their apple trees. It was nowhere near five hundred and twenty-eight. It was, strictly speaking, a second note, and in the Foundry of the Pure Unison a second note was very nearly a sin.

Solenne had come to wait for it the way prisoners wait for the hour the sun touches their window.

He had looked at her once. On the ninth morning, when the wire was still new and she was bleeding into the collar of her shift, he had come down from the gantry to check the resonance at the risers, and his eyes had gone to her throat, and he had flinched. Not looked away. *Flinched*, the way you flinch from a stove. Then he had gone back up the gantry and never looked at the risers again.

In three hundred days, he was the only one who ever flinched.

---

"Today," said Inquisitor Ignatius, "the bore reaches the casing."

Dawn was coming up behind the Spire the way it always did in Sol-Luminary, not rising so much as *arriving*, the whole white length of the tower catching fire from the top down. The Foundry smelled of hot iron and rosin and the sweetish mineral stink of raw glass. On the gantry the Tuning Men were calling numbers to one another. The engineer stood at the calibration rail with his fork in his fist.

"Three hundred sunrises," said the Inquisitor. His voice was trained for cathedrals; it carried without seeming to try. "Three hundred sunrises you have held the Light for us, daughters, and today the Light will give us its heart. Sing as you have never sung." He lifted the censer. "One note."

The chain creaked.

"One law."

Creak.

"One hand."

And the Foundry answered him with the only word it had, which was the note.

The Morning Swell began. Thirty-nine voices rose on the one pitch until the air on the risers went thick and bright with it, until Solenne could feel it in her teeth, in the bones behind her eyes, in the copper ring of the tuning collar around her throat. On the gantry the Tuning Men called the match, *five-twenty-eight, holding, five-twenty-eight, bore to five-twenty-eight*, and the Carrow Bore dropped the last span on its chains with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the crown of glass teeth went into the Spire, and the Spire bled.

And underneath them, its chord changed.

It did not grow louder. It grew *closer*. That was the only way she would ever find to say it, afterward, in the dark. The way a room changes when someone in it stops pretending to be asleep. The vast chord that had been sounding since before there was a world to hear it drew in, and in, narrowing, until it was nearly a single note, until it was nearly *their* note, and Solenne understood with her whole body that it was listening.

To thirty-nine girls holding its pitch.

Holding it *for* it.

And it asked.

Not in words. The Spire had no words; she would have sworn to that before this morning, and she would swear to it after. But you do not need words to know you have been asked a question. The whole Foundry *was* a question. The air was. The copper in her throat was. It went through her the way your own name goes through you when it is called across a crowded square, and it was so old, the question, so worn smooth with waiting, that it had calluses.

*Is it time?*

She did not know how she knew what it meant. She knew what it meant.

*Is it time? Have you come? Are you, at last, are you singing it for me?*

On the gantry, the engineer's tuning fork fell out of his hand.

She heard it hit the iron, a clean, bright, useless note, and she saw his face, and she knew that he had heard it too.

"*Stop the bore.*" His voice cracked straight across the Swell. A Reach voice, too loud. "Stop — lift it, lift it *now*, it's not — Inquisitor, it's *letting go*—"

Inquisitor Ignatius did not look at him.

"Hold the note," he said.

And they held it.

Gods help them all, they held it. Thirty-nine voices, perfect, pure, not a tremor, because three hundred days of copper had made it impossible to do anything else, and the Spire heard its own note sung back to it, steady as stone, by someone other than itself, for the first time in ten thousand years.

It made the only mistake a thing that tired could make.

It believed them.

It let go.

---

The chord stopped.

For the length of a heartbeat there was no sound in the Foundry but thirty-nine girls, and even that seemed small, because what had stopped was not a sound. It was the floor under all sound. Solenne had never heard silence before. No one in Aethelgard ever had. It was not quiet. Quiet is a sound. This was the absence of the thing that let sound exist, and she felt it in her inner ear as a falling, as if the whole Foundry had dropped an inch.

Then the light came down the Spire.

It came the way water comes when a dam goes, all at once and from everywhere, pouring off the white stone in sheets. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen for exactly as long as it took to reach the floor.

Where it touched, it took.

The iron tankers went first. They did not melt. They went *pale*, the black of the iron washing out to grey, to white, to the white of a dream you can't quite remember, and then they were simply less. Thinner. As if someone were erasing them from the edges in, until nothing was left where they had stood but a bright place on the floor. A Tuning Man at the foot of the gantry put up his hands against it, and Solenne watched his hands go white, and then clear, and then she could see the bones in them, and then she could not see the bones.

It did not burn. That was the thing she would never be able to make anyone understand. There was no heat, no smoke, and at first no screaming, because the people it touched were too busy being taken apart to scream. It was hungry. That was all. For ten thousand years, light had given. Now nothing was holding its law, and it did the only other thing it knew how to do.

It took everything back.

It came for the risers last, and it came for the throats first, because the copper was bright and bright things called to it, and because, she understood later, the throats were where the song had been, and it was looking for the song. It went into Hesper's mouth like a hand into a glove. It went into Aude. It went into little Coralie, fifteen, who had stood at Solenne's left shoulder for three hundred days and signed jokes to her under their hems, and Solenne felt the wire in her own throat go hot with it—

—and through the white she saw the engineer.

He had not run. He had come down the gantry ladder in something closer to a fall than a climb, and he had the apprentices, three gantry boys, soot-faced, none of them older than twelve, by their collars, and he was throwing them one by one into the drainage conduit that ran under the tanker floor: a black iron throat with a grate over it, the one place in the Foundry the light had not yet found. Above the bore, the glass reservoir burst. A wall of liquid Aether-Glass came down the channel, blue as the bottom of a flame. He got the bulkhead door over the conduit half-shut and held it, shoulder and left arm braced against a door built to be closed by a winch, while the glass came over him.

She watched it pour over the arm, and she watched the arm stop being an arm. It went blue and still and clear to the shoulder, like something stopped in amber, and he screamed. Even screaming, he screamed on his note, the low one, the Reach one, the wet-earth note, and Solenne thought, stupidly, with the light in her mouth: *I'll never hear that again.*

"*Dissonance!*"

The Inquisitor was on his knees on the censer platform with the chain wrapped around his fist. The light had taken the platform's rail and was taking the hem of his robe, and his face was not afraid. It was sure.

"The choir has broken the unison — it is the *choir* — Flambeaux! Purify them! Purify the Rite!"

Along the walls, where they had stood for three hundred days with their fire-staves grounded, the Flambeaux lifted their staves.

In the last instant before the fire reached her, Solenne looked up at the gallery.

Her sister was there.

The light had taken the gilded rail, and the carpets, and the old Canon who had stood at Seraphina's side each morning with his hands folded over his belly. It had not taken Seraphina. She stood in a small circle of untouched color in her white armor, both hands closed on the hilt of the Sun-Blade, and the light parted around her like a river around a stone. As if it knew her. As if it knew what she was holding.

She was looking at the risers now. At last. At Solenne.

Her hands did not open.

*Look at me*, said Solenne's hands, and the fire took the risers.

---

The blast threw her backward, off the top tier, over the glass channels, into the only true dark left in the Foundry.

The wound.

The seam the bore had opened in the Spire's flank was a black crack in the white, and the light would not go into it, because the light had come out of it, and nothing goes back willingly into the place it has escaped from.

She fell.

She fell for a long time. Longer than the Spire was wide, longer than the mountain was deep. She fell out of the light and out of the heat and out of the sound of the Foundry dying, down into a dark that was not the dark of a room at night, or the dark behind closed eyes, or any dark that has another side to it.

And in the dark, the wire in her throat went on singing.

Thin. Exact. Five hundred and twenty-eight beats to the second. The last note in the world.

She could not hear her heart. She could not hear her breath, or the rush of her own falling. There was nothing in the dark to carry a sound but her, and so there was nothing but the note—

—until, far below her, patiently, something opened.

She would never be able to say how she knew it was a mouth.

It took her note in, the way the Spire had. Tasted it. Held it.

And then, carefully, perfectly, pitch for pitch, it sang her note back to her, the way a child repeats the first word it has ever been taught.
