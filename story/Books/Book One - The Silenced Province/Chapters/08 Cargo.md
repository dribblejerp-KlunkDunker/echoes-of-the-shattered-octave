## CHAPTER EIGHT
### Cargo

> *Item: engram cores, standard, in straw. Twelve crates. Consignee: Oakhaven Works, Carillon garrison. The consignment is not to be opened in transit. The Office is aware that carriers sometimes hear the consignment. Carriers who report hearing the consignment will not be engaged again.*
>
> — Bill of lading, Office of the Catalogue

*The* Drift-Kestrel*, at the Oakhaven mast. Driftday night.*

---

There were twelve crates in the hold of the *Drift-Kestrel*, and now there was also a woman and a boy.

Mags had brought them up the mast-ladder at three in the afternoon in full view of the quarry, which was, Vane had explained at some length in hand-cant, the single stupidest thing anyone had ever done aboard his ship. Mags had watched his hands to the end, with the patience of a woman letting weather happen, and then signed back: *Nobody looks up.*

She was right, of course. That was the infuriating part. In twenty-five years of carrying things he should not have been carrying, Vane had learned that nine-tenths of smuggling is knowing that people only ever search at the level of their own eyes. Nobody in the Oakhaven works had looked up at a mooring mast all week. They had a Rite to build and a Church to feed and a bore four days from the casing, and a small boy going up a ladder a hundred feet above their heads was about as visible as a bird.

He went down into the hold at dusk to lay out the rules.

Nell Carder sat on a coil of cable with her back against a crate, and her son lay asleep with his head in her lap and his hands held out stiff in front of him, even sleeping. In the lantern-light she looked like a woman who had not stopped moving in four days and had just realized she could.

"Three rules," said Vane. "One. You don't go on deck. Not for air, not for the view, not if the ship's on fire. Two. If anybody comes aboard — anybody — you go in there." He pointed at the sail-locker, a triangular cupboard in the bow stuffed with spare canvas. "Both of you, fast and quiet, and you stay until Mags comes for you. Not me. Mags. If I come for you it means somebody's watching me."

Nell nodded. She was looking at the crates.

"Three," said Vane. "Don't let the boy listen to the cargo."

Her eyes came up. "What's in them?"

"Glass," said Vane.

It was the third time he had said it that week, and it went down worse every time, like a drink you keep ordering out of spite.

---

The paymaster's clerk came up the ladder an hour later.

He was a narrow young man in Consortium drab with ink on his cuffs and a tally-book under his arm, and he climbed the hundred feet without once looking down, which impressed Vane against his will, and arrived on deck already talking.

"Captain. The Factor's compliments. There's been a delay in the invoice — no, don't, I've heard it, I agree with you, I've said as much to the Factor herself — but I'm to tell you the garrison will take delivery on Hearthday. After the Rite." He consulted the tally-book. "The Legion's quartermaster wants the crates counted before he signs. I'm to count them now so there's no argument later."

"Now," said Vane.

"Now."

Mags was at the hatch. Vane looked at her for one long second, and she looked back, and then she went down the ladder ahead of the clerk at a pace that could plausibly be called helpfulness and was in fact the fastest Vane had ever seen her move.

He gave her a count of twenty. He gave it to her by talking: about the wind, about the state of the Reach, about a tariff dispute in Port Cantabile that he invented as he went, about the criminal price of drift-glass. The clerk waited it out with the polite glaze of a man used to being talked at by captains, and then went down the ladder into the hold.

The sail-locker door was shut. The coil of cable was empty. There was, Vane noticed with a lurch, a child's boot lying on the deck-boards by the third crate, and he put his own boot over it and left it there.

The clerk counted. Twelve. He wrote twelve in his tally-book.

And in the quiet, as he wrote, the hold murmured.

It was doing what it always did: the man counting, the woman giving directions to somewhere, the laughter at a joke told long ago. In the lantern-light the clerk's pen did not so much as pause.

"They do carry on, don't they," he said, pleasantly, and shut the book.

Vane said nothing at all.

"My first posting was the harvest-house at Whisper-Grove," the clerk said, tucking the book under his arm. "Three years. You get used to it. My mother won't have a glass in the house now, not even a drinking glass, but that's mothers." He smiled, and it was a perfectly ordinary smile, the smile of a young man who has come up a ladder and done his job. "Hearthday, then, Captain. After the Rite. Oh — and the mail-cart brought this up from the Greenroot road. It's been chasing you a fortnight."

He handed over a letter, and climbed back down the ladder, and did not look down that time either.

---

Vane took the letter to the stern rail and read it by the glow of the lift-tanks.

It was in Oona Salt's own hand, which meant she had not wanted a scribe to see it, which meant nothing good. The Harbor-Queen of Port Cantabile wrote the way she spoke, which is to say as though every sentence cost her money.

*Vane.*

*Four hundred and eighty sovereigns against the Kestrel, and you have paid me sixty since the spring. I have been patient because patience is cheaper than lawyers. I am not patient now. Bring me two hundred by the turn of the season or bring me the ship.*

*Also: a woman came to the Salt Stair asking after you. Pale. Came in off the water in the evening, which nobody does. Eyes gone violet, the way they go on people who have been down where the trenches open. She was very polite. She asked which captain it was who cut his tow over the trench off Cape Dissonance in the year of the Roaring Tide, and where he might be berthed these days.*

*She paid in old coin. She paid a great deal.*

*I told her you were up in the Reach on the Catalogue's runs.*

*Business is business, Vane. You'd have done the same, and you have.*

*— O. Salt*

He stood at the rail for a while with the letter in his hand and the wind coming up off the quarry, smelling of wet stone and cut wood and, underneath, the sweetish mineral stink of the Root.

*She paid in old coin.*

Dead men's money, they called old coin on the Rust Coast. Bottle-money. What you got when you sold something to somebody who had been in the water too long.

The sensible thing, obviously, was to cut the mast-line.

He worked it out standing there, the way he had worked out every escape of his adult life: she could not be more than three or four days behind the letter, and the *Kestrel* had lift for two days' hard running north-east if he dumped the water, and the Iron Tramway valleys would hide a small ship from a coastal woman who did not know the inland lanes, and by the time anyone at the works had finished arguing about invoices he would be over the Peaks. He had a boy and a woman aboard who could be set down at any village with a purse and a story. He had twelve crates that were not his. He had a Cantor with a ruined voice and a glass-armed man who had paid him in the Consortium's own scrip to do a thing that was going to get everybody involved hanged, and he owed neither of them anything at all beyond eight days of berths, which were, if you looked at it squarely, only four days more.

He turned the letter over and looked at the back of it, which was blank, and thought about a woman standing in the door of a tavern on the Salt Stair with the evening behind her, asking politely.

Then he went below to check the cargo, because he always did, at night.

---

Pim Carder was sitting on the deck-boards between the third and fourth crates with his ear against the pine.

The lantern was where his mother had left it, hanging on the hook by the ladder, and Nell was asleep on the cable-coil with her mouth open and the exhaustion of four days on her, and her son had got up without waking her, the way children do, and crossed the hold in the dark, and put his head against a crate, and was talking to it.

"—no," he was whispering. "No, that's the sound the ship makes. It does it all the time. It isn't anything." A pause, his head tilted, listening. "I *know*. But it's not dark-dark. There's a lamp. I'll leave it on, if you want."

Vane stood on the ladder and could not move.

"Boy," he said.

Pim looked up, unstartled, the way he might have looked up from a game.

"Her name's Annet," he said. "She's six. She's scared of the dark."

Vane came down the last two rungs.

"She kept saying *hello, hello, is anyone there*," said Pim. "She's been saying it a long while, I think. So I said hello. And she asked what my name was, and I said Pim Carder, from the Hollow." His face changed then, all at once, and the child came up through it, and he looked at Vane with real fear for the first time since he had come aboard. "Was that wrong? Lyra says you never answer a voice you can't see. But she's *in a box*. She's not in the dark outside, she's in a box on a ship, and she's six, and nobody's said anything to her for — I think for a long time."

Vane looked at the crate. Stencilled on its side, in black, in letters he had been not-reading for two years:

OFFICE OF THE CATALOGUE • RESONANT ENGRAMS • HANDLE IN SILENCE

"Go and sit with your mother," he said.

"Will you tell her I'm still here? Annet? If she asks?"

"Go and sit with your mother, Pim."

The boy went. Vane waited until he heard him settle, and the small sounds of a child pretending to sleep, and then he took the lantern off its hook and set it on the floor and got the pry-bar out of the locker under the ladder, and opened the crate.

It came up easily. Consortium nails, driven by clerks.

Straw. Good straw, packed by somebody careful. And bedded in it, in four rows of nine, thirty-six spheres of pale lilac glass, each the size of a small apple, each tied with a paper tag on a string.

He lifted one out.

It was cold, and lighter than it looked, and there was a fog inside it that moved a little when he turned it, like breath on a window. The tag was written in a fine clerk's hand, the kind of hand they teach at a Catalogue desk: **WG-41,004.**

He put it down. He lifted another: **WG-41,006.** Another: **WG-41,007.**

WG. Whisper-Grove.

Numbered in the order they were taken.

He knelt on the boards of his own hold with a lantern and thirty-six numbered people in front of him, and he lifted out the one from the corner of the second row, which was the one nearest where a boy's head had been resting against the pine, and he held it up to the light.

*—hello,* said the small voice in the glass, very close and very politely. *Hello? Pim? Are you still there? I don't mind about the lamp. I only wondered.*

He did not laugh.

That was how he knew how bad it was. He had laughed when the trench opened under the *Wave-Cutter* and he had laughed in the Boar & Dulcimer with a hound's mouth coming at his boots, and he had laughed at his own reflection in a brothel mirror in the Screaming Year with a girl dying in the next room, because that was the thing his body did when it was more frightened than it could hold.

He sat down on the floor of the hold with his back against a crate and the core of a six-year-old girl in his two hands, and he did not make any sound at all.

---

Mags came down at some point. She looked at the open crate, and at the tags, and at him, and sat down on the bottom rung of the ladder with her forearms on her knees.

Vane put the core back into the straw, in its place, in the corner of the second row. He was careful about it. Then he lifted his hands and signed.

*We're not delivering.*

Mags watched his hands. Then she looked at the crates for a while. Then she signed, slowly:

*Twelve crates. Four hundred-odd heads. Where do they go?*

*Whisper-Grove,* Vane signed. *Back where they came from. There's a scribe, they say. The one who wrote the catalogue. If anyone alive knows how to put a name back in a head, it's whoever took it out.*

*They say.*

*They say.*

Mags spat, neatly, into the bilge, which Vane had asked her a hundred times not to do.

*The Consortium will hunt us,* she signed.

*Yes.*

*The Catalogue will hunt us.*

*Yes.*

*The Church too, if the Cantor's business goes wrong.*

*Almost certainly.*

Mags considered this. Then she signed, without any particular emphasis, the way she might have noted a change in the wind: *Good.*

She had a sister, he remembered. Somewhere. She had mentioned it twice in nine years, both times drunk, both times in a hand-cant so fast he had not been entirely sure he'd read it. He did not ask.

*How many people can we lift?* he signed instead. *With the crates aboard. Get Pennick.*

---

Pennick did the figures on a slate by the lift-tanks, twice, and then a third time because Vane did not like the answer.

"Six," he said. "Seven if it's children, and if they're not — you know. Eating." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. He had not slept properly in three days; the tanks had been singing sour again. "With the cargo aboard, Captain, six. That's with the water down to a day and no ballast at all, and if the wind's foul over the Peaks we'll be crawling."

"The Cantor wants twenty."

"The Cantor," said Pennick, "is not an engineer."

"She's a Cantor. They think in *ought*." Vane looked at the slate. Six. Eighty-four souls in the Hollow, and nineteen of them children, and a warrant with a date on it. "What goes over the side?"

Pennick blinked. "Captain?"

"To get to twenty. Tell me what goes over."

The boy stared at him, and then, slowly, began to write. Water, most of it. The spare envelope canvas. The chain cable and half the anchors. The brandy, which was eleven cases and worth more than the rest of the ship's contents together. The iron ballast in the keel-sling, which was what kept her from flying like a leaf in a squall, and which meant they would ride high and mean and ugly and anyone with a decent glass would see they were running light.

"And the long gun," Pennick finished, not looking up. "The swivel, and the carriage, and the shot. Eleven hundredweight. That's the last of it, and even then it's — it's eighteen, Captain. Maybe nineteen. It isn't twenty."

Vane looked at the swivel gun in its tarpaulin on the foredeck. He had bought it in Driftwood Cay eleven years ago with the first real money he ever made, and he had fired it nine times, and it had saved his life twice, and the *Kestrel* without it was a fat brass goose over open country.

"Put it over the side," he said.

"Now?"

"Tomorrow, in the dark, over the slag-pit, so nobody hears the splash." He handed the slate back. "Eighteen it is. Tell the Cantor eighteen and let her argue me up to nineteen; she'll want to think she's won something."

Pennick took the slate. He looked at it, and then at the captain, as though he were recalculating something that had nothing to do with lift.

"Aye, Captain," he said.

---

Later, when the ship was quiet and the Pure Note came drifting up faintly from the town below — they had been singing it over Oakhaven for two days now, one note, without a break, and you could hear it even up here, thin and holy and endless — Vane went down into the hold one last time.

Pim was awake. Of course he was.

"Captain?"

"Boy."

"Is Annet coming with us?"

He could have said a great many things. He had made a career of saying a great many things, and most of them had worked.

"Yes," said Vane.

"Where to?"

"Home," said Vane. "Whisper-Grove. We're taking the lot of them home, and there's a woman there who's going to give them back their names, and I'm going to make her do it if I have to carry her up the ladder myself."

Pim thought about this with great seriousness.

"You should tell her," he said. "Annet. She'd like to know. She can't hear me unless I'm right up close."

So Captain Vane of the *Drift-Kestrel*, forty-one years old, master of a stolen ship and a hold full of stolen people, got down on the boards of his own hold in the lantern-light, and put his mouth close to the pine of the third crate, and said:

"Annet. You're going home."

Then he went up on deck, and took Oona Salt's letter out of his coat, and held a corner of it in the lantern flame until it caught, and dropped it over the rail, and watched it fall a hundred feet into the dark, burning, until the wind put it out.

He had cut one line in his life.

He found, somewhat to his surprise, that he was not going to cut another one tonight.
