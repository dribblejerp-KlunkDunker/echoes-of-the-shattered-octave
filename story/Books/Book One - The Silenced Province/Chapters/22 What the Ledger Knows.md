## CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO
### What the Ledger Knows

> *Rule the page before you write on it. A margin is not decoration. A margin is the place where you are not permitted to put your own opinion, and leaving it empty is the discipline of the whole trade.*
>
> — P. Quist, to a nineteen-year-old apprentice, c. 405 SR

*The Mausoleum of Chords, deep Spectral Woods. Nullday night.*

---

There were two things in the inventory now, and Sable could not stop counting them.

She did it at the foot of the willow where she had put her bedroll, in the dark, with the ship's hull creaking forty feet away and no fire because a fire in these woods is a lamp hung out for the Cacophony.

"One. The scriptorium at the Veil-house. Cold stone, tallow, wet wool, vinegar, because we cleaned the desks on the last day of every month. Fourteen desks. Mine by the second window."

Four hundred yards away, through the trees, the Veil-house itself stood with no roof on it and eleven hundred years of records rotting in its cellars, and she had not gone to look, and was not going to.

"Two. My name is—"

Still there.

And then the place where the third thing had been, which was not an absence. That was what nobody understood who had not had it done to them, and she had written seventy-four thousand entries about it without understanding it herself: it does not feel like a gap. It feels like a shelf. You run your hand along it and it is smooth and complete and there is nothing missing, and the only reason you know is that you wrote down, three years ago, that there were three.

Warm, she thought. It had been warm, and it had had hands in it, and she had been able to describe it at breakfast this morning.

She sat in the dark with the Bound Ledger closed on her knees and worked out what that had cost, in the ledger-keeping way, which was the only way she had.

One fragment for one name. Two fragments remaining. Four hundred and thirty-two people in a hold, one thousand three hundred and forty-two in total, seventy-four thousand six hundred and eleven in the book.

And the thing she had learned in the pressing room, which she had not yet said out loud to anybody and which she was going to have to:

That it does not work. Not the way they wanted it to. That the Office had never put one back because the Office had never needed to find out, and now she knew what the finding out had cost a woman who was lying sedated in a canvas sling in that hold saying *put it back, put it back, put it back*, and would be saying it, in all likelihood, for the rest of her life.

Sable had written the pamphlet on the queue.

She was going to have to write this one too.

---

She found Kaelen Vance on the far side of the clearing, sitting against a tree with his coat off and his shirt open, doing something to his own chest with a piece of string.

He had pulled the collar back to the shoulder. In the dark the glass was a pale shape, like the moon under water, and the frost of the tideline had crossed the collarbone and come down onto the chest in a ragged crescent, and he was measuring it: string from the point of the shoulder, thumbnail at the mark, string held up against a knot he had tied in it some days before.

He stopped when he saw her and pulled his shirt closed.

"Don't," said Sable. "I have seen worse this morning."

She sat down four feet away with her back against the next tree, which was as close as she got to anyone, and put the papers on the moss between them.

She had taken them off the archive table while he was on his knees on the floor with his hand flat on the flagstones, saying nothing, with his eyes shut, somewhere she could not follow. She had been loading a hand-cart with one arm and a cracked face and she had seen a box open on a table with a lamp beside it, and she had done what she always did, which was read.

Three hundred daily calibration returns, initialled *K.V.*

A procurement order, two hundred lines, line ninety-four, the same initials.

The casualty return of the Great Foundry Rupture: eleven thousand and forty, with thirty-nine listed as SKY CHOIR (CONSECRATED).

And the Inquisitorial report, filed by one I. Crane, Flambeau of the Synod, in the ninth month of 412, which recorded that the unison had been broken on the three hundredth morning by *a dissonance from the gantry*.

"I have read them," she said. "I have read them twice. Do you want to tell me anything?"

Kaelen looked at the papers on the moss.

"No," he said.

"You walked the bore down. Three hundred days, a hair under the failure-pitch, which they tell me was the finest piece of work in forty years."

"Yes."

"And you signed for the collars."

"Line ninety-four."

"And the Church's own report says that at the end there was a dissonance from the gantry." She let that sit. "There was one man on that gantry, Engineer. The report is the only document in that box that says anybody tried to stop it, and it was written by somebody who meant it as the charge against you."

"I know what's in the report," said Kaelen.

"Then you know that I have in front of me the whole of the evidence against you and precisely one line in your favour, and that the line in your favour is being used as the accusation." Sable tilted her head. "Which is tidy. Almost too tidy. I spent eleven years in that Office and I have never once seen a file that complete and that one-sided that did not have something taken out of it."

He said nothing at all.

She watched him not say it. She was extremely good at watching people not say things; it had been, for eleven years, the largest part of her job.

"You are carrying something inside your shirt," she said, "and it is not the string."

"Sable."

"I will not ask again. I am recording that I asked once." And she opened the Ledger, and wrote, in the dark, by feel: *Asked. Declined.*

---

She took the papers to Lyra an hour later.

The Cantor was sitting on the keel-block the crew had chocked under the bow, with a blanket round her shoulders and the slate on her knees, watching the Aegis Sentinel.

The machine had not moved since they landed. It stood at the edge of the clearing facing back along the line of their flight with the red cloak folded in its fist, and every so often the tick in its chest went fast and then settled.

Sable sat down on the moss in front of the woman she had come to ruin, and laid the papers on her lap, and said:

"These are from the Catalogue archive at the Malting. They are the Consortium's file on the Rite of Pure Unison at the Dawning Spire, and they are in his hand, and I took them off the table in front of him while he was not looking, and I am giving them to you."

Lyra did not pick them up.

She looked at Sable for a long moment with an expression that Sable, who had spent three years reading faces that had nothing behind them, could not parse at all. Then she wiped the slate with her sleeve and wrote, and turned it round.

*I ALREADY KNOW.*

Sable sat back.

"How?"

*IT TOLD ME. THIS MORNING. AT THE CLOISTER.*

*IN HIS VOICE.*

---

It took a while, after that, because the Cantor could only write.

Sable learned to wait, which she had never been good at. She watched the chalk go across the slate, and the sleeve wipe it, and the chalk go again, and she sat on the moss in the dark and took the whole of it: the fog, the porch, the slate held out into the white, *lark, I can't read*, the hymn sung back seven times in her own whole voice, the boy's whistle with too much air in it.

And at the end, after a long pause in which she wrote nothing at all, this:

*I STOOD IN THE DOOR AND LET IT TELL ME.*

*I COULD HAVE WALKED AWAY. IT CAN'T COME IN WHERE PEOPLE ARE. IT WANTS YOU ALONE.*

*I STAYED AND I LISTENED TO THE WHOLE THING.*

Sable read it twice.

"Why?"

The Cantor's hand shook slightly on the chalk.

*BECAUSE IT WAS THE ONLY WAY I WAS EVER GOING TO HEAR IT.*

---

They sat with that for some minutes. In the hold, muffled, four hundred and thirty-two people went on murmuring, and Bettis Renn had stopped screaming an hour ago and started something quieter and worse.

Then Lyra picked up the papers.

She read them by the light of a shuttered lamp held an inch above the page, with her hair falling forward, slowly, the way a woman reads a thing she intends to be able to recite afterward. The returns. The order. Line ninety-four, with the initials against it, between the lamp oil and the scaffold clips.

She stopped at the casualty return and stayed there a long time.

Then the Inquisitor's report.

And then she wrote, and her hand was quite steady now:

*DOES ANYTHING IN HERE SAY HE SHOUTED?*

"Only that," said Sable. "*A dissonance from the gantry.* Crane wrote it as the cause of the catastrophe. There is no other record of it anywhere in that box, and there will not be one anywhere else, because the only other people on that gantry are dead."

*SO THE ONLY PROOF HE TRIED*

The sleeve. The chalk.

*IS THE THING THEY'RE BLAMING HIM FOR.*

"Yes."

Lyra put the papers down on the moss and put both hands over her face and sat like that.

Sable did not touch her. She did not know how, and she suspected it would have been unwelcome, and she had learned a great deal that day about doing things to people for their own good.

"I want to tell you why I brought them," she said instead, to the dark above the Cantor's head. "It is not kindness and I would rather you did not mistake it for kindness.

"I stood in a doorway at a place four miles from here for eleven days with an intake sheet in my hand and watched forty-one children go into a room, and I told myself that what I was doing was *witnessing*. That the recording was the moral act. That so long as the book was true, I was not one of them." She heard her own voice do something unfamiliar and went on through it. "I have had three years in a drowned plaza to find out what that was worth, and the answer is that it was worth nothing whatsoever, and that a woman who watches and writes it down is simply a woman who watches.

"So I am not going to do it again. I know a thing, and the person it belongs to is you, and I am not going to sit on it and feel complicated about it. That is all this is."

The Cantor lowered her hands.

She wrote one word, and showed it, and it was not the one Sable expected.

*THANK YOU.*

---

Afterward, when Lyra had gone across the clearing toward the tree where Kaelen Vance was sitting with his shirt closed and his string in his fist — walking slowly, with the papers held down at her side, in no hurry at all, the way a woman walks toward a thing she has decided to do — Sable went and stood in front of the Aegis Sentinel.

It was a clear night. Through the willow-tops the stars were out, and the Spire of Decay's end of the sky had that faint wrongness to it that everybody had stopped mentioning years ago.

"I know what you are," she said.

Tick. Tick-tick. Steady.

"You are entry forty-one thousand and five. You were taken on the eleventh day of the Ashfall harvest in the year 416, in the hour after noon, out of the Cloister of the Lost Choir, with forty other children of a charitable choir under Mistress Dray, because the Office had a requisition for small clear voices and your choir had them under one roof." She kept her hands at her sides. "You were third in the line, or near it. The girl behind you would not let go of your hand. The proctors took the two of you into the room together rather than make a scene in front of forty children, and you were pressed in the same hour, and I wrote both of your entries.

"Hers is legible. She is in the third crate on that ship and her name is Annet Sowerby and she is six years old and she is frightened of the dark.

"Yours is not."

She made herself say the rest of it standing up straight.

"By the eleventh day my hand had begun to shake. I have looked at your entry perhaps four hundred times in three years. I can read the number. I can read *alto*. I can read the first letter of the given name and it is either a W or an M and I have never been able to decide, and it is one syllable and might be anything, and the surname is under the blot, and there is no one left in the city who could tell me, because I catalogued all of them."

The lilac point behind the grille did not move.

"I am telling you this," said Sable, "because you are owed it, and because I am not going to stand here and let you be a machine for my convenience." She opened the Ledger. It came round and hung at her elbow, and the pages turned themselves to a place she had found four hundred times. "And because I am going to find it. There is a duplicate schedule in the Organum, in the Hall of Carriage, third floor, and the Registrar-General told me himself this morning that no data has been lost. So there is a copy of your name in a building nine hundred miles east of here, and I know the room, and I know the shelf.

"That is a long way and I am forty-one years old with a broken arm and two things left in my head. But I have been reading twenty names a night to people who cannot hear them for three years, and I would like, at least once before the end, to say one to somebody who can."

She wrote, standing, in the dark, under the entry that was not legible:

*I am going to find it.*

The Aegis Sentinel's head came down.

It was not a nod — it had no vocabulary for a nod, and it would be another eleven months before it learned one — but it came down, slowly, until the great bronze grille was level with the face of the small grey woman standing in the moss in front of it.

And then it opened its left hand and gave her the red cloak.
