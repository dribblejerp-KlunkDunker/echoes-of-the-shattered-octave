## CHAPTER EIGHTEEN
### The Harvest-House

> *Queue discipline is the whole of the work. A subject who has been standing for two hours and has been told three times what will be asked will answer in under nine seconds. A subject who is surprised will take a minute and upset the line behind her.*
>
> — S., *Notes Toward an Efficient Intake*, Office of the Catalogue, 416

*The Malting, Cloister road. Nullday morning.*

---

She had written the pamphlet.

It was four pages long. It had no author's name on it, only an initial, because the Office did not put names on procedural documents; and it had been copied out and sent to every harvest-house in the Spectral Woods and then, in 417, to the Iron Peaks and the Caldera, and Registrar-General Quist had told her at the time, kindly, that it was the most useful thing anyone in his department had produced in twenty years.

Nine seconds. That was the figure she had found. A subject who has been prepared answers the three questions in nine seconds; an unprepared one takes a minute; a minute per subject across four thousand subjects is sixty-six hours of daylight you do not have, and a queue that stands overnight in the cold will start asking each other questions and then you have a different problem altogether.

So: shelter over the queue-yard. Benches for the old. Water. A clerk walking the line every twenty minutes telling everyone, pleasantly, what would be asked. Name. Age. Trade. A second clerk at the door of the first room to repeat it. And a man at the end — she had specified this, in writing, on page three — to take coats and bundles and give a numbered ticket, because a subject holding a possession will hesitate at the threshold and a subject who has handed it over will not.

She had solved the whole thing in a fortnight. She had been proud of the benches.

---

The Malting had been a brewer's before it was a harvest-house: a long brick range with two kiln-chimneys at the north end and a cobbled yard behind a wall. From the orchard she could see the shelter over the queue-yard — her shelter, built to her specification, tarred canvas on a timber frame, forty feet by twelve — and the benches under it, and the water butt, and the man at the trestle with the ticket-book.

There was a queue under the shelter.

Sable stood in the wet grass with the fog coming off the orchard and looked at it for a long moment and could not make it make sense. Whisper-Grove had been finished in 416. There was nobody left in the Spectral Woods to stand in a queue.

Then the wind moved, and she saw what they were wearing, and she understood.

Reach clothes. Homespun and sacking and good boots caked to the ankle in dried green; two carters' smocks; a woman in the grey apron of a dye-works. Forty, perhaps fifty people, sitting on her benches with bundles between their feet, in a queue-yard with a painted board at the head of it that read, in fresh Office lettering:

**RELIEF STATION — REACH DISPLACED**
**FOOD · SHELTER · TRANSIT**
**REGISTRATION REQUIRED**

"They walked," said Kaelen beside her. His voice had gone somewhere flat and strange. "Out of the Greening. Down the Greenroot road, that's — that's four days on foot. They'd have come out at Wend's Ford and the Office would have—" He stopped. "They're registering them."

"They are harvesting them," said Sable. "Registration is the first room."

The shift-bell rang in the Malting, the seventh hour, and the queue shuffled up two feet, patiently, the way a well-run queue does.

---

The plan was Vane's and it was not elegant.

Kaelen, Vane and Mags would go in at the kiln end, where the Office's freight door stood open for the wagons and where, according to a three-year-old requisition, the presses were bedded on stone. They would take one. Mags could not lift with a broken collarbone, so Mags would hold the door and look enormous, which she could still do. Tobiah had come down the road with them and would run the tackle.

And Sable would walk in the front.

"Say that part again," Vane had said.

"Eleven staff," said Sable. "Nine of them are clerks and two of them are proctors, and every one of them has been told for three years that the Office's great outstanding irregularity is a scribe who walked off with a bound ledger. If I present myself at the clerks' hall, every person in that building who is not physically holding a subject will come and look at me. Including the Registrar-General, who arrived this morning; that is his carriage in the yard."

"And when they take you?"

"Then they take me in the clerks' hall, which is at the south end, a hundred and ten feet from the kiln floor, behind two doors." She had looked at him. "Captain, I have been within a quarter-mile of this building twice in three years and both times I went home. I am not able to creep in at the back of it. I have discovered that I am able to walk in the front. Let me use the only door I can get through."

---

The clerks' hall smelled of ink and wet wool and, faintly, under it, of the vinegar they cleaned the desks with on the last day of every month.

That was the thing that nearly stopped her on the threshold. Not the ledgers, not the shelves, not the intake sheets pinned in their hundreds on the boards — the *vinegar*, which was one of the three things she still had, and which she had been saying out loud to herself every night for three years in a plaza full of drowned marble, and which was suddenly in her nose, in a room, being real.

Nine clerks looked up.

"Good morning," said Sable. "My name is not on the door of this Office any more, but you will all have seen it at the foot of the intake procedure. I have the Bound Ledger of Whisper-Grove with me. I imagine somebody should be sent for."

The Ledger came round from her elbow and hung in the air, open, turning its pages, and forty feet of clerks' hall went absolutely silent.

Registrar-General Pellam Quist came out of the inner office nine seconds later, which she noted, because nine seconds was her own number.

He was a small, neat, elderly man with soft white hair and half-moon spectacles and ink on the side of his hand, and he had taught her how to rule a page when she was nineteen years old.

"Oh," he said, and his face did something complicated and unguarded, and for one horrible moment she thought he was going to be glad. "Oh, my dear girl. You look *dreadful*. Somebody find her a chair — Renshaw, a chair, and hot water. Your nose is broken."

"Registrar-General."

"Sit *down*, Sable."

She sat down, because it put her in a chair a hundred and ten feet and two doors from the kiln floor, with nine clerks and a Registrar-General looking at her, and because her legs had stopped being reliable somewhere around the vinegar.

---

He did not threaten her. She had known he would not; she had told the others so, in the orchard, and had not been believed.

He sat on the edge of a desk, which he had always done, and he talked to her for eleven minutes in a soft reasonable voice while a clerk cleaned her face with hot water, and he did not once raise it.

He said that the theft of the Ledger had been a great blow, but that the Office's own duplicate schedules were intact, so no data had been lost, which was the main thing.

He said that he had never blamed her. That the eleven days had been hard on everybody, that two of the clerks had never come back to work afterward, and that he had personally seen to it that both of them kept their pensions.

He said that the work in this building was relief work; that the Reach displaced were being fed, sheltered and moved on; and that registration was a requirement of the transit warrant, and that yes, a proportion of the registrations would result in a requisition, because the Legions' cores burn out at a rate of eleven a month per garrison and the alternative to requisition was conscription, and had she ever, in all her years of objecting, proposed an alternative that fed and housed the displaced?

He said: "You never once wrote down an alternative, my dear. You wrote down the queue."

And then he said the thing she had not braced for, because she had braced for cruelty and got carpentry instead.

"There is a desk free at the second window," said Pellam Quist. "You may have it back this afternoon. Nobody will ask you to attend a pressing; I know you never could. You could go on doing what you are doing now — you are reading names to them, aren't you, in the plaza? The proctors report it. You could do that with the Office's own ledgers open in front of you, and be warm, and eat, and not have a broken nose." He took his spectacles off and polished them. "I have wanted to say that to you for three years. I am aware that it sounds like a trap. It is not a trap. It is a desk."

Sable looked at the boards of pinned intake sheets, and the shelves, and the door at the far end with the brass plate that said PRESSING ROOM I.

"Registrar-General," she said. "Would you show me a pressing?"

Quist put his spectacles back on.

"Why?"

"Because I have written seventy-four thousand entries about a thing I have never watched," said Sable, "and I find that I am not able to accept a chair from you until I have."

---

He took her himself.

Pressing Room I was the old malt-store: whitewashed, clean, well lit by two high windows, with a drain in the floor and a smell of hot brass. There was a stone bed in the middle of it with the press bolted down on top: a frame of iron the size of a loom, and in the frame, mounted at the height of a seated person's breastbone, the brass fork — two tines a forearm long, with the glass ampoule socketed between them and a striker on a lever.

There were two staff and a subject.

The subject was a man of about thirty in a Reach smock, sitting on the stool with his hands on his knees, and he was being talked to, kindly, by a clerk with a clipboard.

"Name."

"Ansel Hay."

"Age."

"Thirty-two."

"Trade."

"Ah — I did the sluices. On the Flume." He swallowed. "Is it long, sir? Only they said there's food after."

"There's food after," agreed the clerk. "Sit back. Shoulders against the rest. That's it."

The second man brought the fork down until the tines rested on either side of the sternum, one on each side of the breastbone, not hard.

"Deep breath," said the clerk, "and hold it."

And struck the lever.

Sable had heard the note before at a distance of twelve feet through a shut door, forty or fifty times, in 416, while she worked in the next room. She had never heard it from inside the room.

Seven hundred and forty-one hertz. A single clean tone, perfectly held, much longer than a struck fork has any business sounding, because the ampoule between the tines is not glass but Aether-Glass, and it does not ring — it *drinks*, and what it drinks came out of the Anchor under this building ten thousand years ago and knows what a person is.

Ansel Hay's eyes went wide.

The tone went on. Eight seconds is not a long time to read about. It is a very long time to stand in a whitewashed room and watch a man's face while it happens: the first second, surprise; the second and third, a kind of straining, as though he were trying to remember something urgently; and at four, five, six, the thing she would have given every year of her remaining life not to have seen, which was that he began to look *relieved*.

At seven he stopped looking like anybody.

At eight the clerk lifted the lever, and the ampoule in the fork had gone from clear to a soft lilac, and the man on the stool sat with his hands on his knees and his eyes open and his mouth a little apart, and did not get up, and did not need to be held, and went on breathing.

"Gently now," said the clerk to the second man, and they took him by the elbows and walked him out through the far door into the yard, where he would be given food, because a Hollow eats if the food is put where its loop goes.

The whole thing had taken about a minute and a half. She had costed it at ninety seconds in the pamphlet. They were exactly on her figure.

---

"Now," said Pellam Quist, at her shoulder, not unkindly. "You have seen it."

"Put one back," said Sable.

"I beg your—"

"The press took it. The press can return it; the schedule of the machine is symmetrical or it could not have been designed at all, and you have known that for twenty years, and there is a line in the maintenance manual that says the polarity is not to be reversed under any circumstances, which is not a line you put in a manual about a thing that cannot be done." She was already walking to the shelves along the west wall, where the day's cores stood in their racks with their tags. "You have never done it. You have never done it because of the Office's *object*, which is the cores, and a returned name is a core spent. Not because it is impossible."

"Sable."

"Bettis Renn," she said. "Forty thousand one hundred and two. She is in the yard of this building. I saw her carrying baskets on my way in. Her core is in the long-term rack because her husband's was requisitioned and the pair were broken up and the Office does not like to break a pair, so hers has been sitting on that shelf for three years." She found it. WG-40,102, in a clerk's hand, on a string. "And I am going to put her back, and you are going to let me, because there are nine clerks in this building and two proctors and one of the proctors is in the yard and the other one came in from Whisper-Grove at dawn with a smashed knee, and the woman in front of you has nothing left to lose and a book that cannot be taken off her."

Quist looked at her for a long moment over the half-moon spectacles.

"You have never seen it done the other way," he said.

"No."

"No," he agreed, softly. "Nor has anyone else alive. Go on, then, my dear. I have wanted to know for thirty years, and I was never going to be the one who tried it."

---

They brought Bettis Renn in from the yard.

She came without any trouble at all, because Hollows do. She was thirty-eight years old, in a Whisper-Grove apron three years filthy, and her hands moved as she stood there: the small repeated gesture of a woman setting something down and picking it up again, which was, Sable knew, because she had written it in the margin of the entry on the ninth day of the harvest, the gesture of a woman putting a coat over her arm.

They sat her on the stool.

Sable put the core into the ampoule socket herself. It seated with a small click. She brought the fork down until the tines rested either side of the woman's breastbone, and her hand on the lever was perfectly steady, which surprised her.

Then she opened the Bound Ledger, which came round and hung in front of her at the height of a writing-desk, and found the entry she had written three years ago in a room twelve feet from this one, and read it aloud in the flat clear voice of a woman reading a will.

"Entry forty thousand, one hundred and two. Bettis Renn. Thirty-eight. Wife of Ivo Renn, cooper, of Ashlane. Taken on the fourth day of the harvest, in the sixth hour. She asked to go second."

She struck the lever.

---

The Ledger took its price before the tone had finished.

She had expected pain. There was none. There was a small internal event, like a page turning somewhere behind her sternum, and then a gap, and the gap had no edges, and she stood in a whitewashed room with her hand on a lever and knew, with absolute clarity, that there had been three things in the inventory and that there were now two, and that she could no longer say what the third one had been.

*The scriptorium. Cold stone, tallow, wet wool, vinegar. Fourteen desks; mine by the second window.*

*My name is —*

Still there. Both still there.

Something else had gone, and she would never, for the rest of her life, know what it was, except that she was fairly sure, standing there, that it had been warm and that it had had hands.

And then Bettis Renn began to scream.

---

Nobody had thought about the screaming.

Sable had thought about the moment of recognition; she had allowed herself, walking up the Cloister road in the dark, perhaps four seconds of imagining a woman's eyes coming back on and a hand going up to a mouth. She had not thought past it, because nobody in the history of the Office had ever had to.

Bettis Renn came back into her own head at the age of forty-one, having been six feet from her own front door with her husband's coat over her arm on a Tuesday in 416, and being now in a whitewashed room with a drain in the floor and a fork on her chest and three years missing — and not missing at all, because the whole of it was in there: the queue, and the benches, and the kind clerk who had told her what would be asked, and the eight seconds, and then every single day since. The basket-carrying. The rain. The standing.

That is the thing nobody in the Office had understood, because the Office had never put one back. The Hollow is not asleep. The Hollow is a person with the top lifted off, and underneath, the rest of her goes on standing in the rain for three years and cannot say so.

She remembered all of it.

She came off the stool at Sable with her fingernails, screaming in a voice that had not been used for three years and tore itself apart in the first ten seconds of use, and it took Quist and the clerk and Sable together to hold her, and she was saying words in among it, and the words were: *put it back, put it back, put it back, put it back.*

Sable held a woman she had catalogued on the floor of a pressing room with her one good arm and said, over and over, into her hair, "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm here. Bettis. I'm here. Your husband is on a ship four miles from here, he is in the third stack, he is *whole*—"

Somewhere at the north end of the building, a hundred and ten feet away and two doors off, something enormous fell over on the kiln floor, and men began to shout, and the shift-bell started ringing and did not stop.

Quist, on his knees on the other side of the thrashing woman with his spectacles knocked askew, looked up at Sable across the body of the thing they had between them made.

"Now you understand," he said, quite gently, "why we don't."
