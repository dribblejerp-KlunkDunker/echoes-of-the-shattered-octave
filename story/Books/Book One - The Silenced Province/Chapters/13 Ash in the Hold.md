## CHAPTER THIRTEEN
### Ash in the Hold

> *A structure does not fail where it is weakest. It fails where the load finds it.*
>
> — K. Vance, *On the Calibration of Resonant Bores*

*Aboard the* Drift-Kestrel*, north-east over the Reach. Hearthday night into Massday.*

---

There were twenty-one of them on deck and nowhere to put any of them.

The *Drift-Kestrel* was sixty feet of working deck and a hold full of crates, and by the second hour of the night it had become a field hospital run by an eighty-year-old midwife with no lamp oil. Bettany had the wounded along the port rail under a spare staysail: a man with a crushed foot from the winch-house; a woman with a broken collarbone from the line; four people with hands flayed to the meat by forty feet of knotted rope; a girl of eleven who had no hands to flay, who had been carried up roped under the arms, and who had not spoken since.

Hetty Bram had the children in the lee of the deckhouse in a nest of canvas, counted twice an hour, which Kaelen understood was not about safety.

Nell Carder had her son.

And at the stern rail, wrapped in Vane's own coat with her white shift stiff and black down the front, sat Lyra Ashgrove, who could not make a sound.

Not a whisper. Not a rasp. Bettany had looked into her throat with a lamp and a spoon and then had sat back on her heels and said nothing for a while, and then said, "Rest it. Don't try. Not for days," in the flat voice she used when she was frightened.

Kaelen went to her twice.

The first time he crouched down beside her and said, "Bettany says days. Not weeks. She's seen worse," and Lyra looked at the black country going by underneath and did not turn her head.

The second time, an hour later, he brought her the slate.

She took it, and the chalk, and held them in her lap for a long moment. Then she wrote four words and turned it around.

*YOU SAID DO IT.*

Kaelen looked at it.

"Yes," he said.

She waited. Whatever she was waiting for, he did not have it. He had spent seven years becoming a man who did not have it.

She wiped the slate with the heel of her hand, and gave it back to him, and turned her face to the dark.

---

He went below.

The hold smelled of pine and straw and cold glass, and the crates talked among themselves the way they always did, and he sat down on the boards with his back against the third crate and his glass arm out in front of him and let the murmur go over him.

Through the arm, faintly, four hours behind and four hundred feet down, he could still hear the Root.

He had expected it to be gone. He had done the arithmetic, in the shaft, climbing: forty thousand gallons of raw glass into a wound in a living anchor; a bore-head broken off in the casing; the law running wild across four miles of the Reach. He had expected to hear whatever he had heard at the Dawning Spire: the chord stopping, and the floor going out from under sound.

It had not stopped.

It was singing. Badly, and slowly, and in the wrong place, with two of the five notes missing altogether now, going around and around and losing itself and starting again — and it was, he understood, *still holding*. Whatever had been done to it, the thing in the stone had gone on doing the one thing it had agreed to do a very long time ago, and was doing it with a hole in it.

*Somebody has to hold it,* he thought.

He did not like the shape of the thought. He filed it where he filed everything.

Vane came down the ladder with a bottle and sat on the bottom rung.

"She'll speak again," he said.

"I know."

"I've seen a man lose his voice on a foul tow and get it back inside a month." Vane drank. He did not offer the bottle. "Not the same voice. But a voice."

They sat in the dark with four hundred murmuring people stacked in straw between them.

"Thirteen hundred," Vane said at last.

Kaelen said nothing.

"In two years. Whisper-Grove to the Organum, Whisper-Grove to Fortis, Whisper-Grove to the garrison at Redoubt Zero. Thirteen hundred-odd crates' worth, and I signed for every one, and I knew what was in them by the fourth run, and I kept taking the work, because the *Kestrel* was four hundred and eighty sovereigns and I had sixty." He looked up. "That's the whole confession. You can have it now, because I'd rather you had it from me than from the manifests."

Kaelen reached into his coat and took out the cracked lilac sphere and set it on the lid of the crate between them.

"Tamsin Reed," he said. "She ran a Legionnaire at the quarry. I broke her because she'd seen me lift a coil." He looked at it. "She spent her last three seconds saying her own name so somebody would have it."

Vane looked at the sphere for a long time.

"Whisper-Grove," he said. "There's a scribe. The one who wrote the catalogue, they say — signed it, then stole the ledger and walked off into the mist. If anybody alive knows how to put a name back into a head, it's whoever worked out how to take it out."

"And if she won't?"

"Then I'll be extremely charming," said Vane, "and if that fails, I'll be extremely unpleasant, and if that fails I'll carry four hundred people around in my hold until somebody tells me what to do with them, because I am not giving them to the garrison and I am not putting them in the ground." He drank again. "That's the course. North-east. We'll want water at Greenroot if Greenroot's still there."

He stood, and stopped, with his hand on the ladder.

"You didn't have to say it," he said. "Up there. I'd have cut it anyway. I had the axe in my hand and I'd already done the sum."

"I know," said Kaelen. "That's why I said it."

Vane stood with his back to him for a moment.

"Aye," he said. "I thought that was it." And went up.

---

At the fourth hour, Pennick started screaming.

Kaelen got to the deck four seconds behind Vane and found the boy on his knees at the foredeck with a lantern, tearing canvas away from the planking, and under the canvas the deck of the *Drift-Kestrel* had gone green.

Not moss. Roots. Fine white ones, thousands of them, standing up out of the seams between the planks like hair on a cold arm, and swelling as he watched, and at the center of them, sitting with his back against the deckhouse and his boots stretched out in front of him and a blanket over his knees, was Hob Wendle.

He had been on the line in the second load. Field-hand, forty, big-shouldered, sheepish. Three toes short. Rooke had taken them clean on Rootday with a hush-saw and a sap-iron, and he had walked afterward, as Rooke had promised.

His boots were gone. The bandages were gone. What was left of his feet had come open like split fruit, and out of them, into the good dry oak of Vane's deck, had gone a mat of roots six feet across, and the planks around them had begun, very gently, to put out leaves.

"I did try," said Hob.

He was awake. That was the horror of it: he was perfectly awake, and perfectly lucid, and had been for some time, and had lain there with a blanket over his knees while it happened and not called out, because he was from Oakhaven and it was the middle of the night and there were children asleep eight feet away.

"I kept 'em dry," he said. "Like she told me. I kept 'em warm and dry the whole — but it's in the wood, see. It's got into the wood." He looked up at Vane with an expression of absolutely unbearable apology. "I'm sorry about your ship, Captain."

Pennick was on his knees with his hands in his hair. "It's a *wooden ship*," he said, to nobody, to the deck, to the roots. "It's in the beams. Captain, it's in the *beams*—"

Mags took him by the collar and lifted him up and put him at the rail and stood between him and the foredeck.

Kaelen knelt down and put his hand into the mat of roots. They turned toward the warmth of him, slowly, the way they always did. He followed them with his fingers to where they went into the seam, and in under the plank, and along the beam beneath, and he did the arithmetic he had spent his life doing.

"Two hours," he said. "Maybe three. After that the beams go and she comes apart in the air."

Nobody said anything. In the stern, one of the children turned over in the canvas nest and muttered something.

Lyra was already on her knees beside Hob with the little brown bottle in her hand.

Rooke's bottle. She had carried it up a four-hundred-foot ladder and out through a dying town and up a knotted rope in her fist, and there were, Kaelen saw when she turned it in the lantern-light, three doses left.

Hob Wendle looked at the bottle, and then at her, and his face did something complicated.

"Ah," he said. "Right you are, Cantor."

Lyra opened her mouth.

Nothing came out of it. Nothing at all: not the tending note, not the Root note, not a rasp — only air, moving over ruined ground. She tried again, and her whole body went into it, and the third time her hand went up to the hole under her jaw where the wire had been and stayed there, and her face came apart.

Old Bettany got down on the boards beside her with her knees cracking.

"Give it here, girl," she said. "You hold his hand."

So Lyra Ashgrove held Hob Wendle's hand, and Bettany the midwife gave him the long drops on his tongue, three of them, the way she had watched it done forty times — and then Bettany, who had delivered half of Oakhaven and buried a good deal of the rest, and who had a voice like a saw in a knot, hummed the tending note over him, badly, in the Narrow, for as long as it took.

Kaelen held his shoulders. He weighed nothing by the end.

They cut him out of the deck with a chisel and a hand-saw, and they took up four planks and five feet of good oak beam with him, and they put all of it over the side together into the dark, because there was nothing else to be done with a thing that would grow wherever it was put down.

Vane said the words. He knew them; he had said them a great many times at sea. Then he went aft to the wheel and stood there for an hour and would not be spoken to, and Pennick went round the whole deck on his hands and knees until dawn with a lamp and a chisel, prising up every seam that showed green, and did not find any more.

Not that night.

---

Tobiah Marsh came to him at first light.

Kaelen was at the port rail watching the Reach go by underneath: eight hundred feet down, in the grey, a country that had been fields yesterday. Four miles of canopy where Oakhaven had been, and the green running south down the Greenroot road ahead of the light, following the ditches. Somewhere in the middle of it there was a dirty orange smear that had to be fire — a ring of it, burning in a circle around something, and Kaelen looked at it for a while before he understood.

Firebreaks. Glass-fire, in the square, burning a ring in the green.

The Church had come to Oakhaven to purify ground for a Rite, and was now down there in the dark burning the ground to save what was left of the people it had come to burn. He wondered whether the Inquisitor had noticed that yet. He rather thought the Inquisitor had.

"Sir."

The boy had his charts under his arm. He had been up all night; they all had; but he had the particular grey look of somebody who has been sitting with a thing and turning it over.

"Marsh."

"I saw what you put in the port," said Tobiah Marsh.

Kaelen turned around.

"I was sitting on the rail calling the pitch to you," the boy said. "You asked me for a spanner. And you took the plate off the drum and you put something in the phase port, and forty seconds later the lock threw." He was not shouting. His voice was shaking very slightly, in a way he was working hard to control, and that was worse. "Marten and Old Coyle and the Tanner twins died of slippage, sir. In the summer. I helped carry them up. And you put slippage into that machine on purpose, with two hundred men in the quarry and a Cantor ten feet from the collar."

"Yes," said Kaelen.

"*Why?*"

So he told him.

Not all of it. Not the part that mattered most, which he had never told anyone and was not going to tell a twenty-four-year-old on a deck at dawn. But he told him about Stage Nine, and what a wire does to a throat, and what the Rite of Pure Unison had been for, and what he had understood standing at the calibration rail while the pitch came up and the thing in the Root stopped singing and started *listening*. And last, because the boy was an engineer and would need the one sentence that made the rest of it undeniable, he told him about the note in the margin of his own Sequence, in a small beautiful hand, under the scales.

*On failure of the hold, the Sequence is not to be halted. Slippage is to be absorbed by the source.*

Tobiah Marsh stood very still.

Then he pulled the roll of charts out from inside his shirt — a month of readings, every quarter-hour, in his own small careful hand, and pinned around them with a bootlace, a fair copy of Revision Seven that he had made for himself because he wanted to learn it properly — and he held it out.

"Take them," he said.

"Marsh—"

"*Take them.*" His eyes were streaming; he did not appear to notice. "They'll say it was slippage. That's what they always say, and now there's a whole town under a wood and they'll say the Root was unstable and the bore-head was faulty and the Consortium will pay a bounty to the families of the shift-crews, and in the spring they'll sink another one somewhere else, at Greenroot or in the Peaks, with the same Sequence and the same stage nine and some other town's Cantor." He pushed the roll into Kaelen's hands. "That's the only copy outside the Organum that has the margin on it. I copied the margin because I thought it was clever."

Kaelen took it.

"I'm not going to tell anyone what you did," said Tobiah Marsh. "I want you to know that I thought about it. All night. I sat with the children and I thought about it." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist, once, angrily. "My mother's in Sol-Luminary. In the Umbral Tiers. If they hear my name at the works they'll go and ask her where I've gone."

"Then we won't use your name," said Kaelen.

The boy nodded. He stood there a moment longer, at a loss, the way people are when the thing they have been carrying all night is suddenly out of their hands.

"It was a beautiful Sequence, sir," he said, wretchedly. "That's the worst of it. It's the most beautiful piece of engineering I ever read."

"I know," said Kaelen. "I've thought so for fifteen years."

---

The sun came up somewhere behind the haze, pale as a coin under water.

And out of the canopy four miles behind them — from a country that had no towns left in it, across eight hundred feet of cold morning air, faint and clear and perfectly in tune — somebody began to sing the calling-song.

In Lyra's voice.

Not the ruined one. The whole one. Warm, and round, and patient, the way she had sounded on a stone behind a cooperage on forty-one nights, singing lost lambs home to the fold.

The whole deck heard it. Hetty Bram's head came up. Two of the children began to cry. Mags, who heard nothing, saw every face on that deck change at once and put her hand on the axe.

Lyra was at the stern rail. She did not turn around. She listened to her own voice come up out of the leaves and follow the ship north-east at eight hundred feet, for as long as it went on, and her hands on the rail did not move.

Kaelen came and stood beside her and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that she could answer.

After a while the singing stopped. It did not fade. It stopped, the way something stops when it has finished doing a thing it meant to do, and the sound of the *Kestrel* came back: the hum of the tanks, the creak of the envelope, the wind.

*Once it has your note,* the old rhyme went, *it can find you anywhere.*

---

That night, in the hold, by the light of the lantern, Kaelen Vance found a nub of charcoal in his coat pocket and wrote on the underside of the deck-beam over the third crate.

First the number he had written on a wall in a charcoal-burner's hut every morning for seven years, because it had moved again and there was no wall now to write it on.

*3½.*

Then, under it, because somebody had to have them and because he had watched a young woman spend her last three seconds trying to make sure that somebody did:

*Tamsin Reed*
*Aldric Vance*
*Davin Hale*
*Tam — (no surname. ASK.)*
*Orla Hale*
*Hob Wendle*
*Ada Pellow*
*8 men at the pumps — no names. FIND THEM.*

He sat back and looked at it: nine lines of charcoal on a ship's beam, above four hundred people in straw who each had a number and no name at all, flying north-east through the dark toward the one woman in the world who had written all of their names down and then run away with the book.

He did not yet know what the list was for.

He only knew that somebody had to keep one — and that every single person who had ever kept one before him had been the Catalogue.
