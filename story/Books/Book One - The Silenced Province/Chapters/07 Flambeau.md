## CHAPTER SEVEN
### Flambeau

> *Where the second note has taken root, let the Pure Flame be the only song.*
>
> — Warrant of Purification, under the seal of His Resonance the High Patriarch

*Oakhaven. Dawnday.*

---

He heard them before dawn, through the sand.

He had been lying with the arm buried to the shoulder, not sleeping, listening to the breathing at the bottom of the world, when the arm began, very quietly, to sing along with something.

Not ring. *Sing.* A single note, rising up through the sand in a pure clear line — the glass answering, the way glass answers a voice — and for a moment he did not understand what it was answering, because it was a note he had not heard sung by human throats in seven years.

Five hundred and twenty-eight.

He pulled the arm out of the trough and sat up, and the note filled the hut, bright and high and holy, pouring out of his own flesh. Dawning glass. Light-glass, the blood of the Spire of Dawning, tuned to the Pure Note in the moment it set around his bones. For seven years it had rung at whatever pitch it pleased. Now, for the first time since the Foundry, it had heard its own note sung by others, far off, many voices together, and it was singing back to them like a dog that hears its master's whistle across a valley.

He buried it again, to the shoulder, and packed the sand hard over it with his right hand, and it went on singing, muffled, under the sand. He could feel it in his teeth. He lay there and could not make it stop.

---

They came up the Greenroot road at sunrise, singing.

Kaelen watched from the North Gate, with Harrow and half of Oakhaven's north side packed into the gatehouse and along the inner bank of the Hedge with their hands over their mouths. The whole town, he thought afterward, must have been at a window or a wall. Nobody had heard singing on the Greenroot road in two years. Nobody had heard singing anywhere — aloud, in the open, in daylight — and lived.

There were thirty in the choir, young men and young women in white, walking four abreast with gorgets of gold at their throats, and they were singing the Pure Note in unison. One note. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Held without end, as the catechism said, the breath passed down the line from voice to voice so that the note never broke. It rose off the road and over the fields and filled the whole bowl of the sky above the Reach, bright and loud and unbearably pure.

On either side of them walked the Flambeaux.

Kaelen had not seen Flambeaux since the Foundry either. They had not changed. Tall men and women in robes the red of banked coals, hooded, their faces in shadow, each carrying a fire-stave: a staff of black iron as tall as a man, topped with an iron cage, and in every cage a fist-sized lump of amber Aether-Glass, burning. Glass-fire. It burned white-gold and smokeless and without fuel, and it burned, the Church said, with the Pure Flame of the Light itself.

At the edges of the fields, along the tree-line on both sides of the road, the hounds were watching.

He could see them from the gate: a jitter in the air along the forest's edge like heat above a summer road, pacing, keeping pace. Dozens of them. More than he had ever seen at once. They had come to the singing the way they always came to singing. And they would not close. They paced along the edge of the fields with the procession, a hundred yards off, and whenever one drifted nearer, a Flambeau on that side raised his stave, and the white-gold fire in its cage flared, and the jitter flinched back into the trees.

The Pure Note walked where it liked. That was the lesson, and everyone on the Hedge understood it perfectly.

And at the head of the procession, alone, in red, swinging a great brass censer on a chain, walked the Inquisitor.

The chain creaked at the top of each arc.

Kaelen heard it from the gate. He heard it from three hundred yards away, over thirty voices and the crowd's breathing and his own arm singing under its sleeve. He would have heard it, he thought, from the far side of the world.

Creak.

He was on the gantry. The fork was in his fist. Forty girls in white shifts on the risers below him, and the one on the top tier with blood in her collar, and the chain creaking at the top of each arc, and—

Creak.

He took hold of the gatepost with his right hand and held on until it passed.

---

They sang all the way to the square and stopped, and the silence afterward was so enormous that people put their hands over their ears as though it hurt.

His father was on the Greenhall steps. Aldric Vance stood in his Governor's chain with his long grey hair combed down over his temple, and Factor Vosk stood at his shoulder in her green lenses, and a line of Carillon Legionnaires stood below them with their bells lifted. His father's face was grey and set and very still: the face of a man attending an execution he has signed for.

The Inquisitor climbed the steps and gave the Governor his hand to kiss.

He was thirty-eight now, Kaelen reckoned. He had not aged the way men age. He had aged the way statues weather — the lines cut cleaner and deeper, the face still beautiful. He wore his red robes as though he had been born in them. On his right hand was the Inquisitor's ring. His left he kept gloved in white silk, and when he raised it to bless the Governor, the silk slid back at the wrist, and Kaelen saw what was underneath.

The skin was gone. Not burned. Not scarred. *Gone* — bleached out, the way the light had bleached the iron tankers in the Foundry, the color washed out of it until it was the translucent white of a skinned almond. You could see the blue veins inside it, and the pale shadows of the tendons, and in bright sun, he thought, you would see the bones.

The light had taken the hem of the Inquisitor's robe that morning. Then it had taken his hand. Then it had stopped. Nobody had ever explained why it stopped.

A herald in white and gold stepped forward, unrolled a warrant with the High Patriarch's great sunburst seal hanging from it on a ribbon, and read it aloud.

Aloud. In the square. In a voice trained for cathedrals, going up off the stones and over the rooftops, and nobody on the Hedge breathed, and out along the tree-line the jitter paced and paced.

*Whereas the second note has taken root in the town of Oakhaven, in the Resonant Reach, among the dwellings of the district called the Hollow* — and on, through the whereases — *and whereas the ground must be made clean before the Rite—*

Kaelen went cold.

*—it is ordered that the Pure Note be held over the town of Oakhaven without end, by day and by night, until the Rite; and that the Hollow be given to the Pure Flame at the hour the Rite of Unison is begun, on Hearthday next, that the ground beneath the Rite be sanctified. And it is further ordered that the Cantor of Oakhaven, Lyra Ashgrove, having been chosen by the Consortium of Bores and by the Arbiterium for the honor of the Unison Hold, be kept in the Church's protection until the Rite, that her voice be preserved unto the Light.*

Across the square, three hundred heads turned.

She was there. Of course she was there. She stood at the mouth of Cooper's Row with her shawl around her shoulders and her hands in its folds, with a bruise on her cheekbone that had not been there on Hearthday and a split at the corner of her mouth. He had heard about the meadow from Harrow on Stasisday morning, who had heard it from everyone: the Cantor had gone out through the tannery culvert at the Listening Hour and sung the calling-song at full voice and brought the Carder boy back, and old Tam had walked out after her singing *The Miller's Daughter*, and they had found his stool. Kaelen had gone to her door that afternoon. She had not opened it. He had stood in the lane for an hour, and he had not blamed her.

She stood very straight now under three hundred stares and did not look at anyone. Two Legionnaires detached themselves from the line below the Greenhall steps and crossed the square toward her, clanking softly, and took up positions on either side of her, bells lifted, and stood there.

*In the Church's protection.*

His father did not move. He stood on the Greenhall steps with his hands clasped behind his back and did not look at Cooper's Row, and did not look at his son.

The choir filed into the Chapel of the Pure Note, still singing, and did not stop. It would not stop now until Hearthday. The note went up out of the empty belfry and settled over Oakhaven like a lid.

---

The Inquisitor found him afterward, while the crowd was breaking up.

Kaelen had not moved from the corner of the well-house. The Flambeaux had grounded their staves at the four corners of the square, so that the glass-fire burned in its cages like four small suns, and people were going home along the edges of the square, quickly, heads down, not looking at the fire. He felt the Inquisitor coming before he saw him. He felt it in the arm. The glass knew the censer.

Creak.

"Engineer," said Ignatius Crane.

He stopped a pace away, the censer hanging quiet at his side on its chain, and looked Kaelen up and down with an expression of courteous and absolute interest, the way a scholar looks at a text he has only ever seen quoted.

"Engineer Vance," he said. "The prodigal technician returns. I see you survived the Foundry." His eyes went to the felt sleeve. "More or less."

"You too," said Kaelen. He looked at the white silk glove. "More or less."

The Inquisitor smiled, and lifted the gloved hand a little, as if showing it to a friend. "The Light took what it wished of me and returned the rest. I have never questioned its choice." He tilted his head toward the sleeve. "And you. Dawning glass, from the fingertips nearly to the heart — I can hear it from here. The Light's own blood, set in your bones. Do you know what the Church calls men like you, Engineer?"

"Consecrated."

"Consecrated." Ignatius savored the word. "The High Patriarch would give a great deal to have you kneel in the Chapel of the Organum. A living relic of the Dawning. You would be a sermon in yourself." A pause. "I told him you would never come. I told him that in three hundred days you never once sang the Pure Note on the gantry. I heard you, you know. Every dawn. Humming your little Reach note under your breath, while forty holy daughters held the Light." He said it without heat, as though remarking on the weather. "I have prayed about that humming for seven years."

"Have you."

"I have." The Inquisitor stepped closer. His voice dropped — not into the Narrow; he did not appear to know the Narrow existed — but into something private. "Shall I tell you what I have come to believe, in seven years of prayer? The unison did not fail. It was *broken*. Thirty-nine voices held the Light perfectly, Engineer. Perfectly. And the Light was pleased. And then one voice shouted. A Reach voice. Too loud." He looked into Kaelen's face. "The first dissonance was yours. The Light heard a second note, and it turned its face from us."

*It did not turn its face because I shouted. It let go because they held. Because they held it perfectly. Because it had waited ten thousand years for somebody to hold it, and I built the bore that let them.*

He said none of that. He said:

"It let go because they held."

The Inquisitor went very still.

"It asked a question," Kaelen said. "Just before. You heard it. I saw your face. It asked, and they answered, and it believed them. That is why it let go. Not because anybody shouted." He was surprised at how steady his voice was. "You have been praying about the wrong sound for seven years, Inquisitor."

For a moment — only a moment — something moved behind the statue's face. A crack of some kind. A fault-line running deep under the surface. Kaelen had spent his life listening for fault-lines. He heard this one.

Then it closed.

"Do not presume to teach me the Light, Engineer," said Ignatius Crane softly. He raised the bleached hand between them, and the white silk slid back, and in the pale morning Kaelen could see the shadows of the bones. "I have *held* it."

He let the glove fall back into place.

"I am to officiate the Hold," he said, pleasantly again. "As I did at the Dawning. The Arbiterium asked for me by name. It is a great mercy, you know, this new Sequence. One voice, not forty. Four hours, not three hundred days. The Supreme Arbiter's mathematicians have made it so much kinder." He inclined his head. "I shall see you at the Rite, Engineer. I am told you will be at the wire."

Creak, went the chain as he walked away. Creak.

And under its sleeve, very softly, the arm kept time.

---

"I'm told I'll be at the wire," Kaelen said.

The Factor looked up from her desk.

Her office was a room at the back of the quarry's counting-house: whitewashed, spare, with one window onto the terraces and the headframe, and a lamp with a plain tin shade and no chimney — which he noticed, and which she saw him notice. On the wall behind her hung a framed drawing of the Carrow Bore in section, very fine, signed in the corner in a hand he knew: *Y. Carrow*.

"Sit down, Engineer," said Adela Vosk.

He did not sit.

"The Church," she said, "has asked that the wire be threaded by a Consecrated hand. As at the Dawning — though I understand you did not thread it yourself, then." The green lenses rested on his sleeve. "The Inquisitor was very particular. He wants you at the collar. He says the Light will know the hand."

"And the Hollow."

"Ah." She folded her hands on the desk. "You heard the warrant. I did warn you, on Rootday. The Church has its own reasons for everything, and its reasons for Oakhaven's Hollow are Rite reasons — the ground must be clean — and the Church's warrants rank above my invoices." She said it with the faintest distaste, as though the Church were a supplier who had overcharged her. "However. If the calibration is secured — if the supervising engineer of Stage Nine is a Consecrated hand, and the Inquisitor has what he wants at the collar — then I believe I can persuade him that purification may wait until after the Rite. After the Rite, the Root will be open, and the Consortium will have a great deal of work for the Hollow's hands. Living hands." She let that settle. "Which is to say: sign as supervising engineer, and I will petition to defer the fire. I believe the petition will be granted. The Inquisitor wants you very much."

She took a paper from a drawer, laid it on the desk between them, and turned it to face him. A pen lay beside it.

*STAGE NINE — UNISON HOLD. Supervising Engineer: ____________. Collar & Wire: ____________.*

"Thirty-one households," said the Factor. "Eighty-four souls, by Surgeon-Grafter Rooke's census. Nineteen of them children. Against four hours." She did not say *against one throat*. She did not need to.

Kaelen looked at the paper.

He had spent his youth doing this arithmetic. That was what the Vance Sequence was, underneath the pitch tables: arithmetic about which things you could afford to let break. He looked at the paper, and the empty line, and the number the Factor had said, and he felt the arithmetic do itself in the back of his head, cold and fast and neat, the way it always had, and arrive at its answer.

"When do you need it?" he said.

"By dusk on Driftday." She paused. "Oh — Engineer. A small thing. One of my Legionnaires walked into the slag-pit in the middle watch on Hearthday. They do that occasionally, when an engram degrades. They wander." The green lenses did not leave his face. "You were on the terraces that night, I think. Did you see anything?"

"No," said Kaelen.

"No," the Factor agreed. "It was very dark." She picked up her own pen. "Driftday, Engineer."

---

The two Legionnaires at Lyra's door let him in because of the green glass disc. They lifted their bells a little as he showed it — the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar step — and stood aside.

He thought about that on the doorstep. The Consortium's consultant. The Rite's engineer. The hand that would be at the wire. The door opened for him because of what he had agreed to become.

She was at the table, with a cup of something steaming between her hands, and a clay jar beside it, and a slate, and the basket from the Flume crossing on the settle with her father's seven pipes laid in it. The bruise on her cheekbone was purple going yellow. When she saw him, she did not get up.

He put a finger to his lips, and pointed at the door, and at the bells beyond it. Then he sat down across from her, pulled the slate toward him, and wrote: *THEY KEEP EVERYTHING. THE FACTOR LISTENS TO THEM EVERY MORNING.*

She read it. She looked at the door. Then she took the chalk out of his fingers and wrote underneath: *THEN WE WHISPER, AND WE WRITE.*

"I heard," he said, barely a breath.

"The whole town heard." Her voice was a ruin — worse than Rootday, hoarse and cracked right through, the voice of somebody who has screamed at a funeral. "Tam walked out into the meadow singing, so I could get Pim back through the culvert. He never reached a note above the middle of the stave in his life. He didn't reach that one either."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing.

"It has my voice," she whispered. "The thing at the Hedge. It called Pim out in my voice, Kael. My old voice. It sang him the falling fifth." She looked into the cup. "I didn't tell you something, on Rootday. I should have." She drew a breath, and he watched her decide, and watched her decide not to. "It knows my voice," she said. "That's all. It knows mine, and it knows yours."

"I know," said Kaelen. "It said a name back to me. Massday, at dawn. In my voice." He looked at his hands. "I killed a Legionnaire in the quarry on Hearthday night. After the tavern."

She went still.

He told her, in the lowest whisper he had. Depot Three. The coil in the straw. The reed-pattern grille. The bell he drank, and the core he broke, and the young woman inside it who had been looking for a blue jug, and who had said her own name three times over as if she were afraid of losing it. He took the cracked lilac sphere out of his coat and set it on the table between them. The fog in it was gone. It was only broken glass now.

Lyra looked at it for a long moment. Then she reached out and laid one finger on it, very gently, the way she had touched Pim's roots.

"Tamsin Reed," she whispered.

"Tamsin Reed."

Then he wrote the Factor's offer on the slate, line by line, and turned it around to face her.

---

She read it twice. Then she wiped the slate with her sleeve and wrote two words and turned it back.

*SIGN IT.*

"No," he breathed.

"Eighty-four people, Kael." She said it very evenly, in the ruin of her voice. "Nineteen children. Orla. Nell and Pim. The Merrow girl. Hob Wendle, who can't run on those feet. They'll burn them in their houses on Hearthday while the choir sings its one note, so that nobody hears them scream. I heard the warrant." She laid her hands flat on the table. "Four hours."

"It isn't four hours. I told you what it is."

"I heard you."

"Then you know it isn't—" He stopped. He looked at her across the table — the bruise, the split lip, the torn voice, the terrible calm — and understood what he was looking at, because he had seen it in a shaving-mirror every morning for seven years.

"You told me once," he said, "that a martyr is just a musician who gave up before the coda."

She stared at him.

"We were twelve," he said. "On the stile. I'd broken my arm falling off the chapel roof trying to get a look at the bells, and I said I didn't care if it hurt, because I was the only one who had to have it. And you said that. *A martyr is just a musician who gave up before the coda.* I didn't know what a coda was. I had to go and ask your da."

"I was a very annoying child," Lyra whispered.

"You were right."

She looked away. He watched her jaw work, and watched her not cry, which was a thing he had seen her do since she was eight years old.

"There's another way," he said. He took the slate and wrote as he whispered. "I go down tonight, before the Legion posts a guard on the shaft. The coil in my toolbag. I throw the lock *now*, five days early, while the head is still above the casing. No bore, no Rite. No Rite, no warrant — the fire is for the Rite's ground. They'll have nothing left to burn it for."

"And the Root?"

He did not answer at once.

"You don't know what it will do," Lyra said. "If you break the bore five days early with the Root half opened. You don't know. I can see it on your face." She leaned forward. "And they'll know it was you. They'll know by morning. The Factor already knows you were on the terraces on Hearthday. They'll hang you off the headframe and burn the Hollow anyway, out of spite, and then they'll wire me anyway when the new head comes up from the Organum in the spring."

He said nothing. She was right. He had known she was right before he said it. He had said it anyway, because it was the plan in which only he went into the dark.

"No," Lyra said, and took the chalk. "Listen. This is what we do."

She wrote it out, and whispered it, slowly, in the ruin of her voice, with her other hand resting on the broken core of Tamsin Reed. As she did, he understood that she had been working it out since the warrant was read — standing at the mouth of Cooper's Row, with three hundred people staring at her and two Legionnaires taking up their places at her shoulders.

*1. YOU SIGN.* "You're the supervising engineer. The Consecrated hand. The Inquisitor gets what he wants, the Factor gets her petition, and the fire waits until after the Rite. That buys the Hollow past Hearthday."

*2. THE CARDERS GO ON THE SHIP. AND ANYONE ELSE HE'LL CARRY.* "I'll make him take more than three. I don't care what it costs. There's the purse, and there's me, and I'll sing at his funeral for free."

*3. THE COIL GOES DOWN IN YOUR TOOLBAG.* "With your instruments. On Hearthday. Nobody searches the supervising engineer's toolbag."

*4. THEY BRING ME DOWN TO THE CASING THEMSELVES.* "With the collar. Because I'm the voice."

She looked up at him, and wrote the last line slowly.

*5. YOU'RE THE HAND AT THE WIRE. SO DON'T THREAD IT.*

The hush-lamp whistled. Over the rooftops, in the Chapel, the Pure Note went on and on and on.

"The Inquisitor will be standing over me," Kaelen breathed. "With a censer. He has done this before. He will be watching the needle."

"Then don't let him see it. You've the finest ear for failure in forty years. You wrote me so yourself, when we were eighteen and you were insufferable." Her whisper cracked straight across, and she went on through the crack. "Put the collar on me. Make it look right. Leave the wire out, or thread it wrong, or whatever you have to do so that I can still sing my own note. And when the bore comes to the casing, you seat your coil in its port, and I sing against it. Both of us. From the inside."

"And if I get it wrong?"

"Then you'll have got it wrong," said Lyra, "and I'll have a wire in my throat, and at least it will have been your hand that put it there, and not somebody who doesn't know how to be sorry." She put her hand over his, on the table, over the broken glass. "You don't get to carry this one alone. We said so. Rootday."

He looked at her hand.

There were three things the engineer in the back of his head wanted to say. That the plan had nine points of failure he could count without trying, and probably thirty he could not. That the Inquisitor had heard him hum under his breath on a gantry, through forty voices, for three hundred days, and would not miss a needle. That on the ninth morning at the Dawning he had come down from that gantry and seen blood in a girl's collar and flinched, and gone back up, and never looked again — and that if he set a collar around Lyra Ashgrove's throat in front of the Inquisitor, he did not know whether his hands would be steady, or whether, for the first time in his life, they would not.

He said none of them.

"The Carders go first," he whispered instead. "Before anything. Before I sign. I want to see them on that ship."

"The Carders go first," Lyra agreed.

"And the pirate—"

"Leave the pirate to me."

She wiped the slate clean with her sleeve, slowly, line by line, until there was nothing written on it at all.

---

On Driftday afternoon, from the North Gate, he watched a small figure in a dead man's cut-down shirt climb the mast-ladder of the *Drift-Kestrel* behind a woman built like a ship's hawser, his stiff little hands locked around her neck, and go aboard. A while later a thinner figure followed, climbing slowly, looking down at every rung. Nell Carder did not like heights. She climbed anyway.

He signed the Factor's paper at dusk that day, in the Greenhall, under the pale green seal of the dead Queen.

His father was there. The Inquisitor was there, in red, with the censer hanging at his side. The Factor was there with her lenses, watching his pen. He wrote *K. Vance* on the line beside *Supervising Engineer*, and on the line beside *Collar & Wire* he wrote it again, and his hand was perfectly steady, because that was what he had instead.

Across the table, his father let out a breath that Kaelen had never heard him let out before. It sounded like relief. It sounded like a man who has been holding a door shut against a storm for seven years and has just heard the wind drop.

Aldric looked at his son, and his eyes were wet, and he nodded once, as if to say: *There. There, now. You've come home.*

Kaelen could not look at him.

The Inquisitor lifted the censer and swung it once over the signed paper in blessing, and the chain creaked at the top of its arc.

Creak.

Outside, in the square, the four cages of glass-fire burned white-gold on their staves in the falling dark, and over the rooftops the Pure Note went on without end. Along the tree-line beyond the Hedge, the jitter paced. In the Hollow, eighty-four souls had been given until after the Rite. On Cooper's Row, two Legionnaires stood at a door with their bells lifted to the sky, murmuring, and behind the door a woman with a ruined voice sat at her father's table with a wiped slate in front of her, planning.

Four days.

Creak.

And under the felt of his sleeve, very softly, his arm began to keep time.
