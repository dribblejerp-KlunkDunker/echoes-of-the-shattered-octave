## CHAPTER FOURTEEN
### Entry

> *Form 9: Intake. The subject is to be asked three questions and no more. Name. Age. Trade. Further speech is not to be recorded, as it lengthens the queue.*
>
> — Office of the Catalogue, standing forms

*Whisper-Grove, in the Spectral Woods. Memoryday.*

---

At dusk, the archivist called Sable went down into the Weeping Plaza to read to the dead.

They were not dead. She was careful about that, in the Ledger and in her head, because the distinction had cost seventy-four thousand people everything they had and the least she could do was keep it. *Dead* was a thing with an entry and an end date. These had neither. The Office's word for them was *residual*; the word in the villages was *Hollow*; the word Sable used in the Ledger, because it was the only honest one, was *unnamed*.

The plaza had been the civic heart of Whisper-Grove: a square of white marble the size of four fields, with a colonnade on three sides and the Moot-house on the fourth, and a shallow ornamental basin down the middle of it in which the city had once floated lamps on festival nights. When the Anchor of Memory was drilled in 416, the water table had come up. Now the plaza stood under a foot of still water from end to end, the colonnade rose out of it like a row of teeth, and at dusk the water lit itself: a cold lilac phosphorescence, no brighter than a candle but everywhere at once, so that the whole square glowed from underneath and the mist over it glowed too, and everything that moved in it cast its shadow upward onto the fog.

Sable came down the Moot-house steps into the water with her skirts already sodden from three years of it, and the Bound Ledger came with her, floating at her left elbow at the height of a writing-desk, open, its pages turning themselves.

There were perhaps two hundred of them in the plaza tonight.

They did not gather. They simply ended up in the same place, the way leaves do. A woman in a good wool coat walked from the third pillar to the basin's edge, stopped, turned, and walked back to the third pillar, and had been doing it since the spring. Two men stood facing each other at the corner of the colonnade, and one of them lifted his hand at intervals to make a point in an argument that had finished three years ago, and the other waited for him to finish, forever, politely. A young man crouched at the water's edge washing a plate that had no dirt on it and had not had for a thousand days. Further out, near the drowned fountain, an old man was setting a table.

He did it every evening. He had built the table himself out of a door and two barrels, and every evening he set it for four: four flat stones for plates, four twigs laid across them, and a fifth stone at the head, which Sable had decided long ago was a bread-loaf. Then he stood behind it with his hands folded and waited for the people who were coming.

His entry was twelve thousand two hundred and seven. Halder Wick, fifty-eight, joiner, of the Bell Yard. Taken on the second day, in the first hour, because he had lived near the works and the works had started at the end of his own street.

"Good evening, Master Wick," said Sable.

He did not look up. They never did.

She opened the Ledger to the place she had left it and began.

"Entry forty thousand, one hundred and one. Ivo Renn, forty-one, cooper, of Ashlane. Taken on the fourth day." She read it the way the Veil had taught her to read, flat and clear and unhurried, in the voice of a person reading a will. "He asked whether he would be permitted to keep his hands. He was told that he would."

The water moved gently around her knees. The mist glowed.

"Entry forty thousand, one hundred and two. Bettis Renn, thirty-eight, his wife. Taken on the fourth day. She asked to go second."

Nothing happened. Nothing had ever happened, except four times in three years, and each of those four times was recorded in the back of the Ledger in her own hand under the heading OBSERVED EFFECTS, and each of them had amounted to a Hollow stopping what it was doing for between one and nine seconds.

She read twenty names a night. She had done the arithmetic on the first night, standing in this water with her hands shaking: seventy-four thousand and six hundred and eleven entries, at twenty a night, is ten years and two hundred and twenty days, and then you begin again at the first name, and by then the ones you started with will have been standing in the rain for fourteen years.

She read twenty names a night anyway.

It was not a cure. She had never once believed it was a cure. It was the only thing she had been able to think of that was not simply leaving.

---

When she had finished the twentieth, she closed the Ledger and did the inventory.

This was not in any rule of the Veil. She had invented it herself, in the second month, when she noticed that the word *scriptorium* had begun to arrive a half-second late.

The Bound Ledger holds what is written in it. That is its whole nature and its whole price. It is not paper; the Veil bound the first of them eleven hundred years ago out of something that is not paper, and what it holds, it holds against the world — against fire, against water, against the Catalogue, and against the Office's habit of amending the record. But it holds nothing for free. It is a set of scales. Weight for weight: to keep a thing, the scribe gives up a thing of her own, and the Ledger decides what, and the Ledger is not cruel about it and not kind about it either. It takes what is nearest to hand.

She had written seventy-four thousand six hundred and eleven names into it in eleven days.

So: the inventory. Every night, before sleep, in order, out loud.

"One. My mother's hands, braiding my hair. She did it too tight. She had a ring on the third finger of her left hand, a plain one, and it caught, and she said *sorry, chick* every time and never once loosened her grip."

The mist moved.

"Two. The scriptorium at the Veil-house. Cold stone, tallow, wet wool, and vinegar, because we cleaned the desks with vinegar on the last day of every month. Fourteen desks. Mine was by the second window."

She stopped there for a moment, as she always did, and made herself say the third.

"Three. My name is—"

And she said it, out loud, to a plaza full of people who had none, and heard herself say it, and it was still there.

Three fragments. There had been more. She could not have told anyone how many more, because the nature of the loss is that the shelf does not look empty; it looks like a shelf. She knew about these three only because she had written them down on the flyleaf on the first night, in the scriptorium, before she understood what she had agreed to — a young woman being clever, making a list of the things she would mind losing.

She had put the list at the front of a book that eats lists.

She would think about that, most nights, at about this point in the evening.

---

The Office was three days behind her, and closing.

She had seen the first proctor on Stasisday, from the Moot-house gallery: a grey figure in oilcloth at the edge of the mist, standing still in the way that only Catalogue field staff stand still, watching the plaza for a full hour without once shifting his weight. She had seen two of them on Dawnday, together, walking the Bell Yard.

And on Driftday she had found the twice-taken man.

He was in the Ashlane, sitting against a wall with his legs straight out in front of him, and he was not doing anything. That was how she knew. Hollows always do something: they walk their loop, or wash their plate, or set their table. A Hollow is a person with the top layer lifted off, and what is left underneath keeps working, the way a mill keeps turning when the miller has gone.

This man was doing nothing at all. His eyes were open and wet and they tracked her when she crouched in front of him, and there was nothing behind them, and there would never be anything behind them again, because the Office had pressed him a second time.

They had done it to find out whether she had spoken to him.

She had. On the eleventh of Leaffall, she had sat with him for an hour because he had been walking a loop that took him past her door and she had wanted the company. She had read him four names. He had carried on walking.

Sable had knelt in the road with her hands in her lap for a long time. Then she had opened the Ledger and written his entry, and underneath it, in the margin, she had written: *This was done because I spoke to him.* And then she had drawn a line through the margin note and written it again properly, without the excuse in it: *He was pressed a second time by the Office of the Catalogue on the fourteenth or fifteenth day of Leaffall, 419, in the course of an inquiry into the theft of this book.*

The Veil keeps three rules. Write what happened. Write who it happened to. Do not comfort yourself in the margin.

---

She was climbing the Moot-house steps out of the water when she heard the ship.

She heard it long before she saw it, because Whisper-Grove is the quietest city in the world and has been since the drills, and a working skyship at three hundred feet sounds like a barn being dragged slowly across the sky: the hum of lift-tanks out of tune, the creak of an envelope, the slap of loose canvas, and under all of it a donkey-engine with a bad bearing.

Then it came over the willows from the south-west with the last of the light behind it, and Sable stood on the steps with lilac mist to her knees and looked up.

Brass ribs. Oak planking. A grey envelope patched like a quilt, four lift-tanks under the keel burning cobalt, and a swivel-gun mount on the foredeck with no gun in it. She was flying light and she was flying badly, down by the head, and there were people on her deck — a great many people, far more than that class of hull was ever meant to carry, standing at the rail in a crowd and looking down.

On her bow, under two coats of paint that had been put on to cover the last name and then the one before it, was the ghost of a registry mark.

OK-114.

Sable stopped walking.

She knew that mark the way a clerk knows any mark she has read eleven times. She could have told you the color of the ink the carriage-office used for it. She had signed the consignment notes underneath it: *attested, dry, secure, unopened*, and a date, and the Office's stamp, and then her own initials, because the Registrar-General required initials on carriage as well as intake.

Eleven consignments. The first one twelve crates, the second sixteen, and then twenty-four, and twenty-four, and twenty-four.

The wind off the willows turned and came over the plaza, and it brought down from three hundred feet, faint and overlapping and perfectly familiar, the sound of a hold full of people talking in their sleep.

---

She did not move until the ship had gone over the plaza and away north toward the old sky-dock at the Cloister road, and the sound of it had gone out of the mist, and the Hollows had gone on walking their loops through the glowing water as though nothing whatever had happened, which, for them, was true.

Then she went up to the room she had made for herself in the Moot-house muniment store, and lit her lamp, and set the Bound Ledger on the desk, and opened it to the current page, and took up her pen.

She wrote:

*Entry. Memoryday, the sixteenth of Leaffall, 419. At dusk, a skyship over the Weeping Plaza, flying no colors, riding light, down by the head, carrying an estimated twenty to thirty persons on deck. Registry OK-114, twice painted over. Carriage attested by this hand on eleven occasions between Greenleaf 417 and Ashfall 419: total consignment one thousand three hundred and forty-two cores.*

*The hold is occupied.*

She stopped. She looked at the sentence for a while.

Then she wrote, under it, in the same flat hand:

*Note: the Office's proctors are in the Bell Yard and they will have heard it too. They will reach the sky-dock before dawn.*

*Note: I have spent three years reading twenty names a night to people who cannot hear them, because I could not think of anything else to do.*

*Note: that ship can carry a book.*

And then, because the Veil keeps three rules and the third one is the hardest, she made herself write the last line plainly, without any comfort in the margin at all:

*Note: I am going to go and look at it. This is the first time in three years that I have written the word I.*
