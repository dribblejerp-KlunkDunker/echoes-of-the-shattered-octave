## CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE
### Ten Heartbeats

> *An Office cutter carries listening gear forward of the mast. Running silent will not help you. Your cargo is not silent.*
>
> — Free Captains' handbill, Port Cantabile

*The Cloister road sky-dock. Nullday, mid-morning.*

---

They loaded in eleven minutes with a column of smoke standing over the Malting and every bird in the Spectral Woods going up out of the willows at once.

Pennick did his figures on the ladder, shouting them upward, which was how Vane learned that his ship was now carrying four hundred and thirty-two cores, twenty-one Oakhaven survivors, five crew, one archivist, one press at four hundredweight, one woman who could not stop screaming, and one thing that was walking up the mast-ladder at that moment and putting a hundred and sixty pounds on each rung.

"Captain. *Captain.* The lift's gone — we are eleven hundred pounds over what I told you yesterday and yesterday I was lying to make you feel better—"

"Can she fly?"

"She can *sink slowly*—"

"Then she can fly." Vane leaned over the rail. The Aegis Sentinel was two-thirds of the way up the ladder, climbing hand over hand with a red cloak still folded in its left fist, which meant it was climbing a hundred-foot ladder one-handed. "Water over the side. All of it. And the spare envelope canvas, and the tackle, and that chain."

"We'll have nothing to drink—"

"We'll have nothing to *breathe* if the Office puts us in the Bell Yard. Over the side."

Bettis Renn was screaming in the hold. They had got her down the ladder in a canvas sling with Bettany holding her head, and she had not stopped since the pressing room, and the sound came up out of the hatch and across the sky-dock and into the trees, and every single person on that deck knew exactly what sound carried in these woods and what came to it.

Hetty Bram took it in hand, because somebody had to. She put the nine children in the sail-locker with the door shut and Nell Carder in with them, and then she went down into the hold and sat on the floor by the sling and held the woman's hand and talked to her, steadily, about absolutely nothing, in the Narrow, for the next two hours.

The *Drift-Kestrel* came off the Cloister road at eleven minutes past the hour with her envelope straining and her tanks singing sour, and she went up like a wet sack.

---

The cutter came out of the south before they had six hundred feet.

Vane knew the class. Everyone on the Rust Coast knew the class: an Office courier-cutter, forty feet, lead-grey, no envelope at all but four hard little drift-tanks slung in a cage amidships, which made her brutally fast and meant she could not carry anything heavier than paper and proctors. A long gun forward on a swivel. A grapnel-thrower aft. And on the bow, where a figurehead would be on an honest ship, a brass horn the size of a wash-tub.

"What's the trumpet for?" Tobiah said.

"It's not a trumpet," said Vane.

It was a listening horn. A sounding-cone with a proctor's ear at the small end of it, the same kit the Office used to walk a street in a dead town and find the one house with somebody breathing in it. In clear air at a thousand yards, a man on that horn could hear a cough on a deck.

And the *Kestrel*'s hold had four hundred and thirty-two people murmuring in it, and a woman screaming.

*They don't need to see us at all*, Vane thought, with great clarity. *We're a bell. We've been a bell for two years and I've been the one ringing it.*

"Mags." He did not look away from the grey shape coming up out of the mist. "Down, and tell Hetty: everything they can stuff into the straw, round every crate. Blankets, coats, the canvas, the sail-locker spares. Pack it like glass for a long haul."

She read his lips and was gone down the hatch.

"Pennick, how low can you put her?"

Pennick looked at the canopy going past underneath — silver willow-tops, a hundred feet of them, with the drowned marble of Whisper-Grove showing through in patches.

"Captain, we're already low."

"Lower."

---

What followed was the worst twenty minutes of Vane's professional life, which was a field with serious competition in it.

He took the *Drift-Kestrel* down into the canopy of the Spectral Woods at forty feet with eleven hundred pounds too much aboard, and flew her up the line of the Cloister road because a road is the only straight thing in a forest, and the willow-tops came past the rail close enough that Tobiah, holding the forward shrouds, got a faceful of leaves twice.

The cutter went over the top of them at three hundred feet and lost them in the mist and came back.

It came back because of the horn. It found them the way you find a hive: by listening until the sound got louder. It came down the road behind them at twice their speed and the long gun on its bow went off, and the first shot went through the envelope's skirt and out the other side, which does nothing to a skyship, and the second took four feet of the port rail off and sent a splinter the length of a forearm through the meat of Pennick's thigh.

Then it was over them, and the grapnel came down.

---

Vane had been boarded three times in twenty-five years. Twice he had let them. Once, off Cape Dissonance in a crosswind, he had cut the hook and run, and been very lucky, and promised himself never again.

The hook came through the envelope's rigging and bit, and the cutter above began to haul, and the *Kestrel* came up by the nose and slewed sideways out of the road-line into the willows with branches raking down the starboard side like fingers on a drum.

Four proctors came down the grapnel-line.

They came the way they had come through the plaza — grey oilcloth, silver mesh, felted rods, no shouting at all — and they came down a rope at forty feet over a moving ship, which told Vane something he would have preferred not to know about the Office's training.

The first one hit the deck in a crouch, perfectly balanced.

The Aegis Sentinel picked him up off it and threw him over the side.

It was not a fight. There was no exchange. Nine feet of Office ironwork took an Office proctor by the arm and the belt and threw him out over the rail into four hundred feet of nothing without breaking stride, and the man made no sound going down, because he had been trained not to.

The second one it caught mid-drop and put through the deckhouse wall.

The third and fourth had the sense to go for the rope rather than the deck, and one of them got his rod into the back of the Aegis's knee-strut hard enough to bend something, and the machine turned round with the deliberation of a man noticing weather and caught him by the mesh of his mask.

*DEFEND THE SINGERS*, said the stencil on its chest, four feet from the proctor's face.

Vane looked away. He had the wheel. That was his excuse, and he kept it.

---

"The line!" Tobiah was at the shrouds with an axe he had no idea how to use. "Captain, the hook — if we cut it we drop—"

"Don't cut it."

"*What?*"

"*Don't cut it!*" Vane put the wheel hard over. "Mags! Starboard! Take her *starboard* — into the trees — I want that cutter's bow where we are now in four seconds—"

Mags could not hear him and did not need to. She had been watching his hands on the wheel for nine years.

The *Drift-Kestrel*, overloaded, holed, with a grapnel in her rigging and a forty-foot Office cutter tethered above her, put her nose down and went sideways into the crown of a two-hundred-year-old silver willow.

The *Kestrel* weighed a great deal. That was the whole of Vane's plan and the whole of his argument with Pennick for the previous nine days: she was fat, she was slow, she was carrying eleven hundred pounds too much, and a light fast courier-cutter tied to her by forty feet of rope was a terrier tied to a cart-horse.

The willow took the cutter's bow.

Vane heard it happen rather than saw it: a long tearing crunch of wet wood and brass, the horn going over the side, the cutter's drift-cage shrieking as it tried to climb with its nose full of tree, and then the grapnel-line parting somewhere above with a crack like a whip.

The cutter went into the canopy forty yards to starboard and kept going down through it, shedding pieces, and did not come up.

The *Kestrel*, relieved of it, rose about eleven feet.

"That's not a victory," Pennick said from the deck, grey-faced, with a hand clamped around a splinter in his leg. "Captain, I want to be clear, that was not a victory, that was two ships falling and ours is falling slower—"

---

And then the boy came.

Afterward, nobody on that deck agreed about how long it lasted. Tobiah said four seconds. Sable, who counted things for a living, said closer to two.

There was a sound like a sheet of tin being struck — not loud, but *wrong*, arriving from the wrong direction — and a smell of hot metal and summer storms, and a line of blue-white light came up the Cloister road underneath them, going the other way, at a speed nothing in the world goes.

It stopped on the foredeck.

He was perhaps seventeen. That was the thing Vane kept. He was a boy in a Synod courier's leather tabard, and he was made of light the colour of a lightning-strike afterimage, and he was not standing still — he could not stand still; every part of him was moving, vibrating, his edges smearing and reforming so that looking at him was like looking at a struck bell — and in his right hand, clamped so hard the knuckles of it had gone white-blue, was a sealed packet of paper with a black ribbon and the Arbiterium's scales in wax.

"—whereistheroad," he said. "TheOrganumroad. TheTramway. Whichway— *which way* —"

Every lamp on the deck went out. The lift-tanks shrieked up an octave.

"East," said Vane, because it was the only word he had time for.

"EastEastEast — thankyou — thank you—" The boy's head turned, and for a fraction of a second the smearing stopped and there was a face in there, a young, frightened, ordinary face, and his eyes went across the deck: the children at the sail-locker door, the woman screaming in the hold, the press under canvas, Sable. "*Don't stop,*" he said. "Whateveryoudo. Ifyoustop—"

And he was gone, off the bow, down the road, a blue-white line going east through the willows at a speed that left a crack of thunder behind it and a long scorched stripe along four planks of Vane's foredeck in the shape of two running feet.

The lamps came back. The tanks came down an octave and stayed sour.

"What," said Pennick, carefully, "was that."

"That was a courier," said Sable. She had come up out of the hatch at the noise and was standing at the rail looking east with the Ledger hanging open beside her. "Arbiterium seal, black ribbon. That is a standing writ, which means it must be delivered into a named hand and read aloud and witnessed." She paused. "He has been running since the Valley of Velocity went, which was five years ago, and he has not delivered it."

"How do you know that?"

"Because the Office has a file on him," said Sable, "and the file is one line long, and it says: *courier Quill, J., in transit.*"

---

They put her down in the deep woods nine miles north-east, because they had no choice.

The second shot from the cutter's long gun had not missed. It had gone in through the skirt and clipped the number-three lift-tank, and drift-glass does not explode; it *weeps*, very slowly, a cobalt vapour that takes the lift with it, and by the fourth mile the *Kestrel* was flying nose-high and crabbing and Pennick had stopped arguing, which frightened Vane more than the gun had.

They came down in a clearing of silver willows with the keel in the moss and the envelope sagging over them like a tent, four hundred yards from a long black building that had once had a roof.

Sable looked at it for a while in the last of the light.

"The Mausoleum of Chords," she said. "The old Veil-house. Eleven hundred years of records." A pause. "I learned to rule a page in there."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Everything here is dangerous, Captain. That one is only sad."

They got Pennick's leg bound and Bettis dosed and the children fed, and Mags went up the envelope with a needle in her teeth and one working arm, and Vane walked his ruined deck in the dark: four feet of rail gone, two scorched footprints, a hole in the deckhouse the size and shape of a man, a cracked knee-strut on a nine-foot automaton that was standing at the bow facing the way they had come, and six feet of cut rope still hanging from the winch drum, which nobody had coiled in five days.

He stood at the stern for a long time looking back toward the Cloister road.

"Twenty-one," he said, to the dark. He had got into the habit of saying it out loud, lately. "And four hundred and thirty-two. And one screaming."

In the hold beneath him, patiently, four hundred and thirty-two people went on talking in their sleep.
