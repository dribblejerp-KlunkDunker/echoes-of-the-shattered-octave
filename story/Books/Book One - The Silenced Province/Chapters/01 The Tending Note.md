## CHAPTER ONE
### The Tending Note

> *Voice at the hedge, voice at the door,*
> *answer it once and you'll answer no more.*
> *Too loud brings the hounds, too still brings the Hush,*
> *so murmur, my lamb, in the Narrow. Hush.*
>
> — Oakhaven skipping rhyme

*Oakhaven, the Resonant Reach. Autumn, 419 SR. Seven years after the Fracture.*

---

In Oakhaven, children were born into a whisper.

Lyra Ashgrove knelt at the foot of Ruth Madder's bed with a clay jar pressed to the great tight curve of Ruth's belly and her own lips pressed to the jar's neck, and she hummed.

It was a trick older than the Hedge, older than the Synod, older than the Fracture: a Cantor's trick from the orchard days, for when you wanted a sound to go *into* a body instead of into the air. The jar caught the note and gave it to the skin. Hummed into the open room, the tending note would have been a sound. Hummed into the jar, it was a warmth, a pressure, a hand laid flat where no hand could reach. One hundred and seventy-four beats to the second. The Root note. The note the whole Reach had been tuned to since before anyone thought to count, and the first note her father had taught her, before he taught her to walk.

*Low*, she told the baby through the clay. *Low and slow. Come on, now. Come down.*

The room was small and hot and stuffed with wool. Someone, Coll Madder probably, in the week before, when there had still been time to do something useful with his hands, had packed every crack in the shutters with fleece and hung felt over the door, and the hush-lamp on the chest by the bed whistled its thin reed-whistle in the corner, never loud, never stopping, so the room would not fall silent even if everyone in it did. Old Bettany crouched at Ruth's head with the bite-strap, a strip of boiled leather gone dark with other women's teeth, and whenever the pains came she put it between Ruth's jaws and put her own mouth to Ruth's ear and murmured, *in the Narrow, love, in the Narrow, you can do it in the Narrow*, over and over, like a charm.

Ruth could do it in the Narrow. Lyra had known a great many women who could not.

Outside the door, Coll paced. Lyra could hear his boots and then not hear them, and she knew he was stopping every few steps to lift his hand, palm flat, the way everyone in Oakhaven measured sound now (*a hand's width of voice, no more*), and listening to his wife through the felt to see whether she was keeping under it. There was nothing else he could do. There was nothing else anyone could do, most days, except listen and measure.

The pains came faster. Ruth bit through the strap's soft edge. Bettany said, "Here she comes," in the flat murmur of a woman who had said it three thousand times, and Lyra took her mouth from the jar and set the jar down and put out her hands.

The baby came out blue and slick and perfect, and made no sound.

Nobody breathed.

It was a strange thing, Lyra thought, not for the first time, that the most frightening moment in an Oakhaven birth was now the moment of silence. Before the Fracture, a mother had waited for the first cry as a mercy. Now she waited for it as a sentence. Too loud, and something in the forest beyond the Hedge might lift its head. Too quiet, and — well. Everyone knew about the still ones. The babies who came out without a sound and lay without a sound and were found in the morning grey and cool and *thin*, as if someone had been rubbing them out through the night like chalk from a slate. The Grafters called it infant pallor. The old women called it what it was.

*Too still brings the Hush.*

Lyra lifted the girl — it was a girl — and bent over her, and put her lips to the soft top of the skull, where the bones had not yet closed, and hummed the Root note straight into her.

*There you are. There you are. Come here to me.*

One breath. Two. On the third the baby's face creased, and her small mouth worked, and she made a sound no louder than a kettle beginning to think about boiling.

A perfect sound. A sound exactly the width of a hand.

Ruth Madder wept without making any noise at all, which was a thing Oakhaven women had learned to do, and Bettany took the baby and swaddled her with the swift disrespect of long practice and said, "There. A good Narrow cry. She'll live." Lyra sat back on her heels on the rush-matting and discovered that she was shaking.

Her voice felt scraped. It always did, after. *Every mending borrows*, her father used to say. A Cantor's voice was her body, and whatever she gave out through it came out of the same account.

"You'll stay for tea," Bettany murmured. It was not a question.

"I can't." Lyra got her feet under her. "I promised Nell Carder I'd look at Pim before the Listening Hour."

Bettany's mouth thinned. She had the look of every old woman in Oakhaven who had buried more children in seven years than in the forty before them. "How are his hands?"

"Better," Lyra lied, and went out.

---

Outside, the afternoon was going long and golden, which in Oakhaven meant it was going pale.

Everyone over thirty said the same thing, if you got them talking late enough: that colors had been brighter once. That the leaves of the iron-oaks had turned red in the autumns of their childhood, a *red* red, a red like a shout, and now they turned a kind of rust-pink, like a stain that has been scrubbed. That the sun at noon had once been a thing you could not look at, and now, on a clear day, you could look at it for a long time if you were stubborn. The Church said it was the Cascade, that all things had gone a quarter-tone flat since the Spire of Dawning broke and would come right again with prayer. People nodded, and prayed, and privately checked the reds every autumn.

Lyra walked up Cooper's Row toward the Hollow with her shawl pulled close and the tiredness humming in her ribs.

The iron-oaks were ringing.

It was a small sound: the wind moving through the high branches and the branches knocking together, and instead of the old dry creak of wood on wood, a low, sweet *chime*, like a spoon touched to a bronze bowl. It had started not long after the Consortium's bore went into the quarry and began drawing on the Root. Now the whole town rang faintly whenever the wind got up, and everyone who heard it glanced toward the Hedge, because hounds loved a chime.

The Synod had forbidden anyone to fell the oaks. They were Consortium property now, like the quarry, like the Root under the quarry, like the glass that came out of it.

On the corner by the well, a sapling had come up overnight through the cobbles, a young oak, knee-high, its roots heaving three stones out of the road like loose teeth. That made the third this week. Someone had already been at it with a hand-saw and given up; the stump was sprouting again from the cut, fine white shoots curling out of the wood like the fingers of something waking. Lyra stepped around it without slowing.

That was how you lived in Oakhaven now. You stepped around things.

You stepped around the bakery, where the loaves had started rising past their tins and splitting the ovens, so that the baker's wife punched the dough down every hour through the night and wept with exhaustion into the flour. You stepped around your own fingernails, which grew a quarter-inch between dusk and dawn, so that every household kept a knife by the washbasin. You stepped around the churchyard, where the graves had begun to heave in the spring. Not opening; only *swelling*, the turf rising in long soft mounds over the dead as if they were growing down there. The sexton had started salting every new grave by the barrow-load, and binding the jaws of the dead with linen, and setting a flat stone on each tongue, so that no corpse in Oakhaven could ring.

Lyra had never heard a corpse ring. She knew two people who had. Neither of them would talk about it.

To the north, beyond the roofs, the quarry glowed.

It glowed day and night now, green-gold, the color of new leaves with a lamp behind them. The color of Root-glass. Under Lyra's boots, under the cobbles, under the whole of Oakhaven, she could feel the bore: not a sound so much as an ache in the teeth, a slow deep grinding that never stopped. *The Root being milked*, people called it, and then looked sorry they had said anything. Once a day the tanker-wagons came up the quarry road with their loads of green glass sloshing behind iron grilles and went east toward the Organum, and on either side of every wagon walked a Carillon Legionnaire.

Lyra did not look at the Legionnaires. Nobody did.

They wore bells for helmets, full bronze bells closed at the face, with a grille where a mouth would be, and their armor was the grey of old pewter, and they did not speak. If you stood near one, which you did not, you could hear something inside the bell. A murmur. Like someone talking in their sleep in a room down the hall.

On the wall of the well-house, a new decree had gone up over the old ones, the paste still wet. BY ROYAL ASSENT, it began, as they all did, above a seal of pale green glass pressed with a queen's profile. *Maelis, by the Grace of the Pure Note, Queen of the Reach.* The Synod's decrees always came with her seal and her signature. Queen Maelis had been dead for a hundred and forty years.

Her signature was very steady. People said that was because her hand no longer shook. People said a great many things, very quietly.

---

The Hollow was the lanes behind the tannery, where the ground dipped toward the Flume and the damp came up through the floorboards. It had been a poor district before the Fracture. Now it was where the town kept its burl-sick.

Nobody had decided that. It had happened the way water finds the lowest place. The first ones had gone there because the tannery reek covered the smell of green sap. The rest had gone because the first ones were there, and because the Grafters came to the Hollow on the second Rootday of every month, and it was easier, people said, to have everyone in one place.

Easier for whom, nobody said.

Nell Carder's door was painted blue, the paint flaking, and a hush-lamp whistled in its window. Nell opened it before Lyra could knock. She was a small woman with a big jaw and eyes like bruises, and she had been widowed two winters ago, when her husband Joss saw a pack of hounds coming over the east field toward the children's lane and did the thing you never do.

He shouted.

He shouted *hounds!* and *run!*, and the children ran, and every hound in the pack turned away from the lane and toward the loudest voice in the Reach. They found his boots in the morning. Only the boots.

"He's worse," Nell said. "Since yesterday. I think. I don't know. You look."

Pim was sitting on the hearth-rug, seven years old, sturdy and freckled, in his dead father's shirt cut down to fit, with his hands resting palm-up on his knees the way Lyra had taught him. So they wouldn't touch anything.

So they wouldn't take root.

Lyra knelt in front of him. "Hello, Pim."

"'Lo, Lyra." In the Narrow, perfectly. Oakhaven children learned to murmur before they learned to read. "They're itchy."

"I know, love. Let's see."

She took his hands in hers, carefully, by the wrists, and turned them to the lamp.

At the tip of every finger, just under the nail, the skin had split in a fine white seam, and out of every seam grew a tuft of roots. Root-hairs, pale as thread and finer than hair, a thumb's length now, longer than yesterday — Nell was right — and they were moving. Very slowly, and not with any breeze. They were turning, the way roots turn in the dark, reaching toward whatever was nearest and dampest. Toward Lyra's wrists. Toward the warmth of her.

"Itchy," Pim whispered again, and his lip shook.

"I know," Lyra said. "I'm going to sing to them. Remember? You keep very still for me, and you think about something good, and you let me do the work."

"Can I think about the harvest fair?"

"You can think about anything you like."

She bent over his hands and began.

Not the Root note this time, or not only that. The Root note was for bringing things *on*: births and seedlings and wounds that needed closing, and the last thing Pim's hands needed was bringing on. She had spent a week trying to work out what they did need, lying awake over her father's old notebooks, and what she had come to was something he had written in a margin twenty years ago, beside a pencil sketch of a split apple branch. *Growth can be sung back, a little. The way a string can be let down a half-step before it complains. Try the falling fifth.*

So she sang the Root note, and then she let it fall, down the whole distance of a fifth, slow as a leaf coming off a branch, and then climbed back up and let it fall again. In the Narrow. Into the hollow of her own cupped hands, so the sound would go into his.

*Down*, she told the roots. *Go down. Go back where you came from. Go back to being a boy.*

For a long moment nothing happened.

Then the root-hairs on Pim's left forefinger curled, the way a snail's horns curl when you touch them, and drew back. Not far. A fingernail's width. The seam at the tip of his finger closed over them a little, the way lips close. Then the next finger. Then the next.

It was not a cure. She knew it was not a cure. It was a half-step. A string let down a little way, before it complained.

When she stopped, the roots were half the length they had been, and Pim was grinning, and Nell had both hands over her mouth, and Lyra's throat felt as though she had swallowed a handful of gravel.

"It'll hold a day or two," she said. "Keep his hands dry. Don't let him sleep near the wall. I'll come back on Hearthday."

"The Grafters come on Rootday," Nell said.

Lyra didn't answer.

"They took the Merrow girl's hands," Nell said. "Last week. Both of them. Here." She touched her own forearm, just below the elbow. "They said the roots had gone too deep. They said it was a kindness. She's eleven, Lyra. She's eleven, and she's got no *hands*." Her voice climbed and caught and came back down into the Narrow by force, the way you drag a dog back by its collar. "And they're saying — at the well, they're saying the Church is sending Flambeaux up from the capital. To *purify* the Hollow. Is that true?"

"I don't know."

"You'd tell me. If you knew."

"I'd tell you." That, at least, was true.

"He's all I've got left of Joss." Nell took Lyra's scraped, tired hands in both of hers. "Whatever it costs. Please."

"I'll come back on Hearthday," Lyra said, because it was the only promise she had that she could keep, and she kissed Pim on the top of his head and went out into the lane before either of them could see her face.

Halfway up the lane she felt something in her right palm, like the prick of a splinter.

She stopped under a hush-lamp and opened her hand. Out of the crease of her palm, where the life-line crossed the heart-line, grew a single root-hair. White. A thumbnail long. Turning, very slowly, toward the warmth of the lamp.

She took it between finger and thumb and pulled. It came with a small wet tug, like a hair, and a bead of blood welled up after it, and the blood was very slightly green.

*Every mending borrows.*

She wiped her palm on her skirt and walked on.

---

The Listening Hour came down on Oakhaven the way it always did: with lamplight, and the sound of people closing doors softly.

There were no bells to mark it. There had been once. The Chapel of the Pure Note had had a fine peal of six, and the town hall a great clock-bell, and the orchard-cantors had hung little wind-bells in the reed-rows. But the Synod had requisitioned every bell in the Reach in the first year of the quarry. The bore's teeth needed bronze. Nobody had argued much. By then everyone knew bells were how the hounds found you.

So instead, at dusk, the lamplighters came, walking the lanes with their poles and their tapers, lighting the hush-lamps one by one. Each lamp began its thin reed-whistle as it caught, until the whole town was full of a soft high breathing, like a field of crickets that had learned to keep time. And the night-hummers went out with their stools and their shawls, old men and old women mostly, to sit at every crossing through the dark and hum under their breath, so that no street in Oakhaven would ever fall wholly silent.

It was the most comforting sound in the world. It was also the sound of a town that knew exactly how close the edge was.

Lyra took the Hedge path home.

It was the long way, and she should not have, and she knew why she did.

The Hedge ran around Oakhaven in a great uneven ring, three men deep: blackthorn and bramble and hawthorn, planted two summers ago and grown, like everything in the Reach grew now, far faster than it had any right to. The Consortium had paid for the planting, which was the only generous thing the Consortium had ever done, and the town had done the rest. They had packed wool into it by the cartload, hung felt and sacking from its branches, stuffed its roots with straw, until it was less a hedge than a wall of wadding. Its purpose was very simple. Every small sound Oakhaven made, every dropped pan and coughing child and complaining hinge, went into the Hedge and did not come out the other side.

And anything on the other side had to listen very hard to hear what was in.

The Hedge was the second most important thing in Oakhaven. The first was not saying the wrong thing near it.

Old Tam was on his stool at the Weavers' Cross with a blanket over his knees, humming "The Miller's Daughter" through his nose and missing the high notes. He nodded to Lyra as she passed, and she nodded back.

She was ten steps past him when the voice came through the Hedge.

"*Lyra-lark.*"

She stopped walking.

It was not loud. It was never loud; it knew better. It came from the dark on the far side of the thorn, low and warm and a little hoarse, the way it had always gone hoarse at the end of a long day's singing in the orchards. Her father's voice. Tobin Ashgrove's voice, that had sung her to sleep and taught her the Root note and called her home across the reed-rows at dusk. *Lyra-lark. Lark, come in, it's getting dark.*

And after it, softly, humming: the first bars of "Apples in the Rain." The old orchard working-song. He had hummed it every autumn of her life, off-key on the fourth bar. Always the fourth bar.

Off-key on the fourth bar.

Behind her, Tam's humming stopped.

She heard him get up from his stool. She heard the tiny hiss of breath through his teeth as he made the ward-sign, two fingers to the lips and then to the ear. *Mouth shut, ears open.* She did not turn around.

"*Lark*," said her father's voice from the dark beyond the Hedge, very gently. "*I'm cold.*"

Lyra began to walk again.

She walked at exactly the pace she had been walking before. She did not hurry and she did not slow. She kept her eyes on the path and her hands in her shawl and her mouth closed, and she walked away from her father's voice as if it were no more than the wind in the thorn, the way every child in Oakhaven was taught to, the way the rhyme said. *Answer it once and you'll answer no more.* She felt Tam watching her all the way to the corner. She knew what he would tell his wife tonight — *the Ashgrove girl, walked right past it, cool as milk, didn't so much as turn her head, and it her own da* — and she knew the tone he would use, which was the tone people used about Cantors. Admiring. A little afraid.

Behind her, off-key on the fourth bar, the humming followed her along the Hedge for a while, and then stopped.

---

Her father had been taken two years ago, in the spring, at the Pellow farm, out past where the Hedge runs now.

The Pellows' youngest had gone down with a marsh-fever, and the fever had gone the wrong way. Not burning but *cooling*, the child growing quieter and quieter in her cot, paler, stiller, slipping toward the kind of silence that does not come back. Her father had sung to her all night in the Narrow, and she had kept slipping. Somewhere toward dawn, Mistress Pellow said afterward, Tobin Ashgrove had looked at the child and then at the window and said, very calmly, *Well. There's nothing for it*, and had opened his mouth and sung.

Full voice. The Root note, and then the old calling-song the Cantors used for bringing lost lambs home, loud enough to go through walls, loud enough to go through the fever and find the child wherever she had wandered and drag her back by the hand.

It worked. The Pellow girl opened her eyes and began to cry: a good loud outraged cry, the first loud sound in that house in a year.

And every hound between the quarry and the Flume heard him.

He had known they would. That was the part Lyra could never get past. He had known, and he had gone out the kitchen door so that they would find him in the yard and not in the house, and he had kept singing all the way across the yard so that they would follow the song and not the child. They found his lyre in the morning, by the stile. Nothing else.

A week later, the voice began. At the edge of the fields, at first. And at the Hedge, once there was a Hedge.

*Lyra-lark.*

---

Where the Hedge path forked, one track went on toward Cooper's Row and home, and the other went out through a gap in the thorn with a gate across it, a real gate, oak and iron, always locked, and on through the dark toward the charcoal clearing.

There was a light out there. There always was.

Lyra stopped at the fork, as she did most nights, and looked at it: a single lamp in the window of a burner's hut half a mile off among the stumps, the only lit window in the world outside the Hedge. And if the wind was right, and the town was quiet enough, you could hear the other thing, too. A high, thin, endless tone, like a wet finger drawn around the rim of a glass and never lifted.

Kaelen's arm.

They had been children together, she and Kaelen Vance: the Governor's boy and the orchard-cantor's girl, which had made them an odd pair in the eyes of everyone but themselves. He taught her to climb the iron-oaks. She taught him the Root note, which he was hopeless at, and which he practiced anyway, stubbornly, for years, until one summer he was not hopeless anymore. At seventeen he went to the Organum, to the Synod's great Conservatory of Bores, on a scholarship the whole town had been proud of. He wrote to her for a while. Then less. Then not at all.

Seven years ago he came home with a Synod discharge and no letter of explanation, and his left arm turned to glass from the fingertips to the shoulder. Glass the color of a winter sky, pale and blue and clear, with the shadows of his own bones visible inside it when the light was right. He did not go to his father's house, or his father did not let him in; the town told it both ways. He went out to the burner's hut in the clearing, before there was a Hedge to go out past, and he had lived there ever since, alone.

The hounds did not touch him. Everyone knew that. They came down to the clearing sometimes; on bad nights you could see their jitter in the air among the stumps, like heat shimmering off a summer road. But they would not go near the hut. The Grafters said it was the glass. *Spire-blood*, the old women said, and made the ward-sign.

People said he had done something at the Spire of Dawning, the year it broke. People said that was why the sky had gone pale. People said a great many things.

He had told her once, the only time she asked. They were sitting on the stile outside his hut, that first winter he was back, and she asked him straight out, *What happened, Kael?* He looked at his arm for a long time and said, *I was supposed to hold something together. And I didn't.*

She had not asked again.

She did not go to him tonight. She told herself it was because she was tired, and because her voice was scraped raw, and because it was the Listening Hour and nobody walked outside the Hedge in the Listening Hour. All of which was true.

She took the other path home.

---

Her father's house on Cooper's Row was two rooms and a loft, with a lean-to kitchen and a hush-lamp in every window. She had lived in it alone for two years and had not moved a single thing.

His chair still stood by the hearth, the cushion still dented. His books were still on the shelf over the settle, the Cantor's notebooks, twenty years of them, full of intervals and orchard-lore and small pencil sketches of trees. And on the wall by the door, on their pegs, hung his wind-reed pipes, a set of seven, cut from the orchard reeds when she was small.

The orchards themselves were gone. The wind-reeds had been the Reach's glory once: whole valleys of them, planted in rows by the Cantors over three hundred years and tuned by pruning, so that when the wind came down off the hills the orchards *sang*, great slow chords you could hear from Greenroot. On the day the Spire of Dawning broke, every reed in the Reach turned in an hour to something like spun sugar, brittle and glassy and clear, and the next wind blew them into glittering dust. Lyra had been standing in the rows when it happened. She watched her father open his mouth to call her name and heard nothing come out but a kind of hiss, like static. It lasted a moment. Then his voice came back, and he was calling her, and the orchard was dust.

These seven pipes were the last reeds in the Reach that had not turned. She oiled them every month, because he always had. They had not been played in two years.

She made tea she didn't drink. She sat in her own chair, not his, and watched the hush-lamp and listened to it whistle. She went up to the loft and lay down in her clothes and closed her eyes and did not sleep, and at some point she heard the night-hummers change at the crossings, the soft shuffle of stools, the murmur of *all's well, all's quiet, keep to the Narrow*, and knew that it was the dead hour.

She got up. She put on her shawl. She went downstairs and out the back door, soft as a thief, into her own town.

There was a stretch of the Hedge behind the old cooperage where the thorn was thinner. A wet spring had rotted some of the wadding, and nobody had got round to replacing it. There was no crossing near it and no night-hummer. There was a flat stone at its foot, worn smooth, where a person could sit with her back to the thorn.

For two years she had walked past the voice, the way the rhyme said. Then, one night at the end of summer, after she had sung the Tanner twins into the ground side by side, she had come out to the cooperage wall and sat down on this stone, and when the voice spoke, she had answered.

Lyra sat on the stone.

She pulled her shawl tight and drew up her knees and listened to the dark on the other side of the Hedge. The oaks chimed once, far off, in some small wind. Somewhere in the town, very faintly, a hush-lamp whistled. Under everything, in her teeth, the bore ground on and on at the Root.

She did not have to wait long. She never did.

"*There's my lark.*"

So close. Just the other side of the thorn, a hand's width away, a hand's width of voice, the way he would have wanted. Warm, and a little hoarse. She shut her eyes.

"Hello, Da," Lyra whispered.

"*You're out late.*"

"I couldn't sleep."

"*You never could, after a birth. Your mother was the same.*" A soft sound, like a laugh through the nose. "*Boy or girl?*"

"Girl. Ruth Madder's. She came out quiet, but I brought her round."

"*Course you did.*" There was pride in it. Real pride; she could hear it. "*Did you remember to oil the reed-pipes? They crack in the dry, this time of year.*"

"I oiled them on Memoryday."

"*Good girl.*" A pause. Something in the dark shifted its weight; she heard the thorn creak, very slightly, as if something had leaned against it from the other side. "*And the Carder boy? His hands? You said you'd try the falling fifth.*"

Lyra opened her eyes.

She looked at the dark, and at the thorn, and at her own hands in her lap, where a bead of green-tinged blood had dried in the crease of her right palm.

She had told it that. Last night. She had sat on this stone and told it about Pim Carder's hands, and the notebooks, and the note in the margin, and the falling fifth. Because it had asked about her day. Because it always asked about her day. Because her father had always been the one she told everything to, every patient and every fear and every small stupid thing, and he was dead, and his voice was on the other side of the Hedge, and it asked.

Her father had never heard of Pim Carder's hands. Her father had been dead for two years.

This was the forty-first night.

"It worked," she whispered. "A little. The roots went back a half-step."

"*That's my girl*," said her father's voice, warm as a hand laid on her hair, from a place in the dark where there was nothing warm at all. "*Tell me everything.*"

And because she had never in her life been able to refuse him, Lyra did.
