## CHAPTER TWELVE
### Grab the Line

> *The line is not a promise. The line is a rope.*
>
> — Rust Coast saying

*Above Oakhaven. Hearthday afternoon.*

---

The mast went first.

Vane was on deck when it started, watching the quarry fill up like a basin under a pump, and the first he knew of it was Mags hammering the rail with the flat of her hand: three strikes, which meant *look at me now*, and then one finger pointing straight down.

The Consortium's mooring mast was a hundred and twenty feet of riveted iron on four splayed legs, sunk into the bedrock of the quarry rim. There was a young oak in the middle of it. It had not been there twenty minutes ago. It had come up between the legs, and it was still coming, and as Vane watched, the iron on the north leg went from straight to not-straight with a sound like a bell being hit with a hammer, over and over, as the rivets let go one at a time.

*Cut,* he signed. *Cut cut cut—*

Mags already had the axe.

The mooring cable was two inches of wound steel, and she put four blows into it and then the mast leaned and the cable did the rest, parting with a crack that went across the whole quarry, and the loose end came whipping back and took a four-foot splinter off the *Drift-Kestrel*'s starboard rail an arm's length from Pennick's head.

And then they were free, and rising, and turning slowly, and the town of Oakhaven came out from behind the quarry rim below them like something being uncovered.

"Oh," said Pennick.

He had come up the ladder with his tuning-hammer still in his fist and his mouth open, and he stood at the rail and looked down at it and said *oh*, in a small voice, and did not say anything else for a long time.

From a thousand feet, you could see the shape of it. The Greening was not a fire and did not move like one. It moved like a tide coming up a beach: a green edge, advancing, and behind the edge, everything changed. It had the whole north of the town. It had the quarry road and the common and the churchyard. As Vane watched, it took the roof of a long building near the square — the roof did not burn or fall, it simply came apart into green — and something inside it that must have been a beam went up thirty feet in about four seconds, putting out leaves.

Nell Carder had come up out of the hold. Nobody had stopped her. She stood at the rail with her son's hand in hers and looked down at the Hollow, and did not make a sound, because she was from Oakhaven, and that was what they had all been taught.

"That's the tannery lane," said Pim.

"Get below," said Vane.

"That's our—"

"*Below*, boy."

---

"Eighteen," said Pennick.

They were at the rail with the slate between them and the town going under them and the wind pushing the *Kestrel* south-east, away, toward clean country, which was the direction Vane had spent twenty-five years going.

"Eighteen with the crates," Pennick said again. His voice had gone up half an octave and was staying there. "I told you. That's water down to a day and no ballast and the gun over the side, and that was *this morning*, Captain, and since this morning we've had —" he waved the slate at the rigging — "wet canvas, and I've no idea what the envelope's picked up out of that air, and the tanks are singing sour again because everything for ten miles is *growing*—"

"How many are down there?"

"What?"

"In the town. How many people."

Pennick stared at him. "I don't — four hundred? Four hundred and something. It's a town."

Vane looked down at the green coming up Cooper's Row.

He thought about the sensible thing, which was south-east, and about Oona Salt's four hundred and eighty sovereigns, and about a woman with violet eyes asking politely in a doorway on the Salt Stair, and about six-year-old Annet Sowerby in the third crate asking whether anyone was there.

"Take her down over the north gate," he said. "Mags — line and winch. Knots at four-foot. And get the boy's mother away from that rail."

---

They found the town in the only clear ground left: a lozenge of trampled mud inside the gate, sixty feet across, shrinking.

Vane brought the *Kestrel* down to forty feet and held her there with the envelope taking a beating from the updraught off all that new wood, and Mags put the line over, and four hundred people looked up at it.

They did not rush it. He would think about that for the rest of his life, too. Somebody down there — a big woman with her sleeves rolled, standing on a mounting-block by the gatepost — was pushing people into a queue with her forearms, and an old woman in an apron was going down the line pulling children out and moving them forward, and a man in shirtsleeves with a surgeon's bag was doing triage on the ground with two lamps and no table.

They took the children first. Four at a time on the knotted line, the biggest child with the smallest one tied against her chest with a shawl, and Mags on the winch drum taking them up hand over hand with her jaw set, and Vane counting them onto the deck.

Four. Eight. Eleven.

"Captain—" said Pennick.

"I can count."

Thirteen. Fifteen.

The *Kestrel* was settling. He could feel it in the wheel and see it in the way the horizon crept up the shrouds: she was a hundred feet lower than she had been ten minutes ago, and the iron-oaks on the common were not a hundred feet lower than they had been ten minutes ago. They were higher.

That was the thing nobody had said out loud yet. The trees were coming up at them.

---

At eighteen, the hounds came into the canopy.

He heard them before he saw them, because that was how it always was: from the new green of the oaks below and to starboard, a woman's voice, singing something sweet and patient about lambs and dusk, and then a man's cracked tenor going up and up for a high note and missing it, and then, very clearly, a child laughing.

"*Up*," Vane said. "Mags — *up*, get them—"

The jitter came out of the crown of the nearest oak and dropped onto the line.

There were four on the rope: a young man near the top, then two women, then an old man at the bottom with his boots hooked in a knot. The jitter went down the line the way a spider goes down silk, and it did not stop at the old man. It went through him.

Vane saw it happen from thirty feet away in clean afternoon light. The man's shape went soft at the edges, and then grainy, like a charcoal drawing with a thumb dragged across it, and the grain came apart, and the screaming — he was screaming, and everyone below was screaming — pitched up and thinned into static in the space of a breath and a half.

His hands stayed on the rope.

Just the hands, gripping the knot, for perhaps a second, before they came off and fell.

Then the thing went up the line after the women.

Kaelen Vance came over the port rail at that moment with his left sleeve gone and his arm blazing like a lamp with a white-hot wick, and put himself between the winch and the line, and when the jitter came over the rail he took it out of the air with his glass fist and it burst into a spray of bright falling notes and a laugh that Vane recognized, and which he was going to hear again, he knew, for years, at three in the morning.

"*Get them up!*" Kaelen shouted.

They got the three up. The old man on the deck-boards was a pair of boots and a knitted cap and nothing else, and one of the women who came over the rail after him had his blood on her, though there had been no blood, and Vane understood that she had bitten through her own lip getting up the rope.

Twenty-one.

"Captain." Pennick had hold of his arm with both hands. He was crying and did not seem to know it. "Captain, we are at twenty-one souls and the crates and we are *going down*. Not slow. Look at the trees. Look at the *trees*, Captain—"

---

Lyra Ashgrove came up with the last four before the last.

She came over the rail like a dropped coat, and the big landlady and the old midwife got her under the arms, and she could not stand, and there was blood down the front of a white shift that had been white that morning and a hole under her jaw where a copper wire had been. She got a hand into Kaelen's collar and pulled his ear down to her mouth, and Vane watched her try to say something, and nothing come out at all. Not a rasp. Nothing.

Kaelen said, "I know. I know. They're coming."

And Mags put the line down for the last load.

Five went onto it.

Vane, leaning over the rail with the wind of the envelope beating up into his face, saw them from above: a girl of eleven with bandaged stumps for hands, roped to the knot under her arms by two men; an old woman in a dye-works apron — the same one who had been pulling children out of the queue, who had, somebody told him later, taken over the night-hummer's stool at the Flume crossing when the old man died; a young man in Consortium drab with a roll of charts stuffed down his shirt; a woman who was screaming somebody's name up at the ship.

And at the bottom, last, with his boots on the lowest knot and one arm through it and the other hand still on the shoulder of the man he had just pushed up ahead of himself, was a big grey-headed old man in a Governor's chain of bronze oak-leaves.

He had loaded his town onto that rope. He had been doing it for twenty minutes, Vane realized: every load, the same figure at the foot of the line, taking people by the elbows and putting them on and sending them up, and stepping back for the next one.

"*Take her up*," Vane said.

Mags wound. The line came up. And the *Drift-Kestrel*, sixty feet lower than she had been at the start of it, with twenty-one souls and twelve crates and four hundred gallons of nothing left to throw, did not go with it.

She went sideways.

---

It happened the way it always happens: slowly, and then in about ten heartbeats.

The wind came round off the burning of something in the south and pushed her, and she had no ballast and no grip and she slid, and the line and the five on it swung out and down, and the crown of an iron-oak that had been a sapling at breakfast came up through the underside of that arc like a hand closing.

The rope went into the branches. Then the rope snagged.

Everything after that was arithmetic. Vane had never in his life been good at arithmetic and he did it in about a second and a half. A wooden ship at forty feet with no lift, anchored by her winch drum to a tree that was growing, and the drum bolted through the deck-beams, and the deck-beams holding the hull together.

Pennick was howling. "The drum — Captain, the drum's coming out of the *deck*—"

*One.*

Twenty-one people on the deck. Nine of them children. A boy in the hold with his ear against a crate.

*Two.*

The old woman on the line had got her feet onto the branches and was trying to get the girl with no hands off the rope and into the tree, which would have been clever if the tree had been a tree and not the thing that was eating the town.

*Three.*

Mags, at the winch, with the axe already in her fist because she had been at sea for thirty years and knew exactly what came next, and she was looking at him, and she was not going to swing it without an order, because she never had.

*Four.*

The deck-beam under the drum went off like a musket.

*Five.*

Vane took the axe out of Mags's hands, because if it was going to be anyone it was going to be him.

*Six.*

And Kaelen Vance came up beside him at the rail and looked down the line at his father.

*Seven.*

Aldric Vance looked up.

It was forty feet. Vane could see his face perfectly well. He was a big old man with a jaw like a gate-post and grey hair combed long over one temple, and he looked up past four people at a ship that was coming down onto a tree, and he understood the arithmetic faster than Vane had, because he had been a Governor for thirty years and it is all any of them ever do.

*Eight.*

He let go of the rope with one hand, and raised it, palm out, the way a man waves a cart on past a hole in the road.

*Nine.*

"Do it," said Kaelen.

*Ten.*

Vane cut the line.

---

She came up like a cork.

That was the obscene part. The instant the rope went, the *Drift-Kestrel* shed three hundred pounds and the tree's grip together, and she leapt — bodily leapt — a hundred and fifty feet into the green afternoon, and everyone on deck went down on their knees with the surge of it.

Vane kept his feet, because he had the rail.

So he was the only one who saw it, except Kaelen, who did not look away either: five people falling forty feet into a canopy that had not existed that morning, and the canopy closing over them, and a sound that the wind took most of.

Then there was nothing down there but leaves.

---

They lifted out of it into cold clean air at eight hundred feet, and the town went small below them, and the green kept coming.

From up here you could see the whole shape of the Reach: Oakhaven going under, and the Greening spreading out from the quarry in a rough circle four miles across, and the spearhead of it already reaching down the Greenroot road toward the south, following the ditches, the way water follows a furrow.

And in the trampled mud by the North Gate, three hundred and eighty people stood in the last clear ground in the world and watched the only ship go away.

Some of them were waving. That was the thing Vane could not put down afterward. Not shaking fists. *Waving* — the way you wave a cart on past a hole in the road.

He stood at the rail with the axe still in his hand until Mags came and took it off him, gently, the way you take a cup off a drunk.

Pim Carder was at the hatch. He had come up in the middle of it, and nobody had sent him down, and he had seen all of it, and he was looking at Vane now with his mouth a little open and his hands held out stiff in front of him.

Vane opened his mouth to say something to him.

He found that his body, which had laughed in a brothel in the Screaming Year with a girl dying in the next room, and laughed in the Boar & Dulcimer with a hound's mouth coming at his boots, and laughed at his own reflection every bad morning of his adult life, was not going to do it this time. Nothing came up at all.

He shut his mouth and went aft and stood at the wheel and set a course north-east, into the wind, away from the coast, toward the Spectral Woods.

There was six feet of rope hanging from the winch drum, cut clean, swinging.

Nobody coiled it. It hung there for six days.
