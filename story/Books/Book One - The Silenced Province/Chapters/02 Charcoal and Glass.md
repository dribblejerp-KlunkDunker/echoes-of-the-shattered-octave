## CHAPTER TWO
### Charcoal and Glass

> *Every structure has a note at which it wishes to fail. The engineer's task is to find that note before the structure does, and to hold the bore a hair's breadth beneath it.*
>
> — K. Vance, *On the Calibration of Resonant Bores*, Conservatory of Bores, 409 SR

*The charcoal clearing, outside the Hedge. Rootday.*

---

Kaelen Vance slept with his arm in a trough of sand.

He had built the trough the first winter, out of four planks and a tarred bottom, and set it on trestles beside the cot so that he could lie on his right side with his left arm buried to the shoulder. Sand was the best thing he had found. Wool muffled the glass, but it muffled it the way a hand over a mouth muffles a scream: you could still feel the scream underneath. Sand took the sound and spread it through ten thousand grains until each grain carried almost nothing, and on a good night, almost nothing was what he heard.

This had not been a good night.

He lay in the grey before dawn with his eyes open and listened to the arm listening.

It heard everything. That was what the glass did. It heard the clamps ticking as they cooled, and a fox moving in the bracken at the edge of the clearing, and, half a mile off through the thorn and wadding of the Hedge, the whole field of Oakhaven's hush-lamps whistling their thin reed-whistles, like crickets in a jar. It heard the bore at the quarry, a mile north and five hundred feet down, grinding at the Root, a sound he felt in the glass the way you feel a cart go by in the boards of a floor.

And under the bore, it heard the thing he did not name.

It was not a sound, exactly. It was the shape of one: something vast, and slow, and very far down, drawing in and letting out. Like breathing heard through a wall. Like someone in the next room who has been ill for a very long time. It had been there, faintly, for as long as the bore had been in the quarry, and in the last month it had grown. Not louder. *Nearer*, the way a sound grows nearer when you stop pretending you can't hear it.

He had heard something like it once before.

He did not think about where.

He drew the arm up out of the sand in a long hiss of grains running off glass, and sat up, and the world came in through it bright and sharp and much too loud. He waited for it to settle. It never did, quite. Then he reached for the tailor's tape on the shelf above the cot and measured.

The glass began at his fingertips and ran unbroken to the shoulder, and at the shoulder it did not stop cleanly. It gave way in a ragged frost-line, where clear glass clouded to a milky sheen and the sheen faded into skin. He called it the tideline. Every morning he measured from the point of his shoulder to the tideline, along the line of the collarbone, the way other men checked the weather. This morning it was two fingers and a half. At midsummer it had been one.

He wrote the number on the wall with a nub of charcoal, under all the other numbers, and did not look at the column as a whole. He knew what a column like that did. He had spent his youth drawing them.

"Morning," he said to the hut.

He had got into the habit, living alone, of saying things out loud. Nobody heard. That was the point.

---

There were four clamps in the clearing, and he walked them in the half-light the way a shepherd walks a fold.

A clamp was only a heap of cordwood built into a careful dome, packed round with turf and earth and lit from the center, so that it burned for days without ever catching fire. That was the whole art: to keep a thing burning without letting it burn. Too much air and it flared, and you had a heap of ash and a lost week. Too little and it died. You managed it by listening. A good clamp breathed, a low steady hiss through the vent-holes. A clamp about to flare went quiet first, and then popped — a small hollow crack, deep in the stack, like a knuckle.

He was very good at it. He had been told at the Conservatory that he had the finest ear for failure of any student in forty years. It had been meant as praise.

The second clamp was running hot. He knelt and packed turf over two of its vents with his right hand and listened until the breathing slowed. The glass hand he kept against his chest. It was no good for fine work anymore. It could still hold, and it could still hit, but it had no feeling except the one, and that one never switched off.

Halfway to the woodpile, he stopped.

There was a patch of ground beside the stacked cordwood, a rough oval a little longer than a big dog, where the dew had not formed. The grass all around it was grey with wet. Inside it the grass was dry, and flattened, and when he dropped a pebble into the middle of it, the pebble struck the earth and made no sound at all.

A hound's bed.

They came down into the clearing some nights. Everyone knew that. They did not come near the hut. They lay out among the stumps at the clearing's edge, where the forest began, and in the mornings their beds were there: dead oval patches where no bird would land and no sound would carry for a day, until whatever they had left behind wore off. He had never found one closer than the stumps.

This one was twenty paces from his door.

He stood and looked at it for a while. Then he looked at the Hedge, a long dark wall of thorn a quarter mile off, and the lamplit town behind it.

"You're getting bold," he said, to no one, and went to fetch the barrow.

---

The Legionnaire was waiting at the Hedge gate when he brought the week's first barrow of charcoal down the track.

Old Harrow the gate-warden stood beside it with his keys in his fist and the look of a man who had been asked to stand next to a lit furnace. The Legionnaire did not look at anything. It stood very straight in its pewter-grey plate, hands at its sides, its bell-helm tilted slightly toward the sky, as if it were listening for something only it could hear. Up close, the bell was beautifully made. Kaelen had not stood this near to one since the Organum. Someone had cast it with care: the bronze was thin, the lip finely turned, the grille at the mouth worked into a pattern of interlaced reeds.

"Got a letter for you," Harrow muttered, and would come no nearer to hand it over, so the Legionnaire did.

It lifted its arm with a small sound of oiled joints, a folded sheet between its gauntleted fingers. Green wax. The Oakhaven oak.

*The Governor requires the attendance of K. Vance at the Greenhall. Immediately.*

His father's hand. His father had signed his first letters to the Conservatory *your loving father, A.*, and then, after the second year, only *A.*, and then he had stopped writing altogether, and Kaelen had not noticed for a month, which he had never forgiven himself for either.

*Requires.* And sent with a Synod soldier, as though he might need to be carried.

"I'll need to wash," Kaelen said.

The Legionnaire did not answer. Somewhere inside the bell, very softly, someone was talking.

---

He washed at the rain-barrel, and put on the one coat he owned that had never carried charcoal, and last of all he drew the felt sleeve up over the glass arm and laced it tight at the shoulder.

The town had asked him to wear it, that first winter. Not for the look of the arm. For the sound. On still days, in the lanes, the ringing got into people's teeth, and the children cried, and the old women said it was drawing the hounds, which it was not. He had worn the sleeve ever since. It did not stop the ringing. It turned it down to something you could pretend was a noise in your own ears.

They walked into Oakhaven together, he and the Legionnaire, down the track from the North Gate and along Cooper's Row toward the square.

Oakhaven watched him go by the way it always watched him: sideways. The baker's wife, floured to the elbow, stopped in her doorway with a tray of loaves on her hip and stared until her husband pulled her inside. Two girls at the well made the ward-sign, fingers to lips and fingers to ear, and then, because he was walking beside a Synod soldier, made it again, faster. Nobody spoke. Nobody ever spoke to him in the lanes. They bought his charcoal through Harrow and left the coin on the gatepost, and on Chapel-days, when the chapel-reader's sermons came round to the Consecrated and the blessing of the glass, some of them looked toward the North Gate with a kind of nervous piety. Most days they simply stepped around him, the way they stepped around the saplings in the road.

The Legionnaire walked on his left. Its bell was level with his shoulder, and his shoulder was glass, and so he heard what was inside the bell much more clearly than he wanted to.

A woman's voice. Young. Patient, the way you are patient with a child who is not listening.

*—the blue jug, Mam. Not the white. The blue one. The white one's cracked, it leaks all over the table—*

A pause, as though someone had answered.

*—the blue jug, Mam. Not the white—*

He had known. Of course he had known. At the Conservatory the Catalogue's cores had been *resonant engrams* and *harmonic templates*, a line in a supply ledger, and the engineers of the Consortium concerned themselves with the Catalogue's methods no more than a butcher concerns himself with the farmer's. He had been very good at not concerning himself. He had stood in the same yard as Carillon Legionnaires a hundred times and never once put his head close enough to a bell to hear what it was saying.

*—the blue one, Mam—*

He changed sides and walked on the Legionnaire's right, and under the felt his arm rang a little higher, the way it did when it recognized something.

---

The square was the same as ever — the well-house with its layers of decrees and the dead queen's glass seal, the Chapel of the Pure Note with its empty belfry, the Greenhall's long timbered front — except that above the roofs to the north, where the Consortium's mooring mast stood over the quarry like a gallows, a skyship was riding at anchor.

Kaelen stopped to look at it before he could stop himself. It was small for a skyship, sixty feet perhaps, brass-ribbed and oak-planked, with a patched grey envelope and four lift-tanks slung beneath her keel glowing the faint cobalt of drift-glass. Her name had been painted over on the bow, and then painted over again. She flew no colors. Consortium freighters flew green and black, and the Synod's cutters flew iron-grey, and a ship that flew nothing at all from a Consortium mast was either a Consortium secret or a smuggler with a Consortium contract. In Kaelen's experience those came to the same thing.

A smuggler would take passengers. A smuggler would not ask why.

He filed the thought where he filed everything, and followed the Legionnaire into the Greenhall.

---

The orchard-cantors had built the Greenhall three hundred years ago as a guildhall for the reed-growers of the Reach, and every beam in it was carved: reeds and apple boughs and small singing birds, climbing the posts and running the length of the tie-beams, polished dark by three centuries of lamp smoke. When Kaelen was a boy, his father had held the Governor's audiences here with the carvings in full view. There had been a Cantor's chair at his right hand, and on court days Tobin Ashgrove had sat in it and hummed under his breath whenever the petitions grew tedious.

The carvings were hung over now with banners: the Synod's iron-grey and bronze, the Consortium's green and black with its drill-and-wheel, and behind the Governor's chair, where the Cantor's chair used to stand, the pale green glass of the Queen's seal, a yard across, set into a frame of oak.

His father sat beneath it.

Aldric Vance had been a big man, handsome in a red-faced, shouting way — a man who filled doorways and argued with weather. He was still big. But the size had gone soft and grey at the edges, like a loaf left out, and the shouting had gone out of him altogether. He wore the Governor's chain of bronze oak-leaves over a coat that had fit him ten years ago. His hair, which he had always kept short, he now wore long on the left side, combed down over the temple.

"Kaelen," his father said.

"Governor."

"Sit."

He did not sit. His father's jaw moved once and did not move again.

There was a woman at the end of the table. She rose as he came in, which nobody in Oakhaven had done for him in seven years, and inclined her head.

"Engineer Vance," she said.

Nobody had called him that in seven years either.

She was perhaps forty-five, tall and spare and very neat, in black with green piping at the collar and cuffs: Consortium colors, worn by someone who had never needed telling to wear them. Her grey hair was cropped close to the skull. On her nose sat a pair of round spectacles whose lenses were not glass but Aether-Glass, pale green and faintly luminous, and through them her eyes looked slightly too large and slightly too bright.

Resonance lenses. He had helped to calibrate the first pair.

"Adela Vosk," she said. "Resident Factor for the Oakhaven works. Thank you for coming."

"I was required."

"Yes. I'm sorry about that. The Governor felt you might not come for a request." Her gaze went to his left arm, to the felt sleeve, and stayed there, and he knew what she was seeing. Through those lenses, pitch was color, and the arm would be burning like a lamp. "Dawning glass," she said softly. "Third stage. I had heard, of course. But to see it." She seemed genuinely moved. "May I—"

"No."

"Of course." She sat. After a moment, so did he — not because anyone had asked him again, but because she had spread a sheaf of drawings across the table, and he had seen the title block on the top sheet, and his knees had made the decision for him.

---

It was a bore plan: the Oakhaven works, drawn in the Consortium's standard hand. The quarry in section; the shaft; the bore-head descending through strata of stone and clay and a great twisting mass drawn in green ink and labelled simply ROOT. At the bottom of the ROOT, a tight dark shape: the casing. Numbers everywhere, in neat columns. Pitch tables. Torque curves. The resonance envelope of the bore-head, plotted against depth.

And in the title block, in the lower right-hand corner, in a draughtsman's capitals:

**CALIBRATION: VANCE SEQUENCE, REV. 7 (OAKHAVEN ROOT)**

He had written the Vance Sequence at twenty-four, in six weeks, in a rented room over a chandler's shop in the Organum's Brass Quarter, living on bread and cheap wine and the certainty that he was the cleverest person in the city. It was a method for walking a bore down through a resonating structure by holding the bit always a hair's breadth beneath the structure's failure-pitch: listening for the note at which the thing wished to break, and staying just under it, all the way down. It had made his name. It had won him his badge and his rank and his patron. It had been used on every major bore the Consortium had sunk since 409.

He had written three revisions of it. The third had been used at the Dawning Spire.

This was the seventh.

"Nine days," Factor Vosk was saying. "At the present rate of descent, the bore-head reaches the casing in nine days — Hearthday next. And we have been experiencing what my engineers insist on calling harmonic slippage." A thin smile. "The Root does not hold still the way stone does. It moves. We have shattered three bore-heads this month. We have lost two shift-crews." She said it the way she might have said *two wagons*. "The Organum has sent us the revised Sequence you see here, and my people can implement it. What they cannot do is *hear* it. There is only one man in the Reach who has walked a bore through a living casing, Engineer Vance."

"I didn't walk it through," Kaelen said. "It went through me."

The Factor inclined her head, as if conceding a clever point in a debate.

He turned the pages. He made himself read them properly, as an engineer, because the only alternative was to read them as something else. Revisions four through six were refinements, and good ones. Whoever had made them understood the mathematics better than he had at twenty-four; he could see a dozen places where they had caught his errors and quietly mended them. The hand was not a Consortium hand. It was small and exact and very beautiful, and in the margin beside each revision, where a draughtsman would put his initials, there was only a mark: a tiny pair of scales.

The Arbiterium's sigil.

He had never known the Arbiterium to care about bore-work. The Arbiterium cared about law, and taxes, and its Edicts. It left the drills to the Consortium the way a lord leaves the drains to his steward.

He turned to the seventh revision, and his arm rang.

Not the usual ringing. A sharp, high, rising note, straight up through the felt, so sudden and loud that the glass chimney of the lamp at his elbow sang back in sympathy and his father flinched in his chair.

The seventh revision added a stage. It sat at the foot of the pitch tables, at the moment the bore-head met the casing, in the same small beautiful hand:

**STAGE 9 — UNISON HOLD**
*Pitch: 174. Duration: 4 hrs, or until casing yields.*
*Source: Cantor, local (1).*
*Collar: standard. Wire: standard.*

He knew those words. He had seen them once before, on a procurement order pinned to a board in the Siphon Foundry at the foot of the Dawning Spire, in a column headed SKY CHOIR (40). He had signed for the tuning collars himself. It had been one line among two hundred, and he had not read it.

*Collar: standard. Wire: standard.*

There was one Cantor left in the Reach.

"Is something wrong, Engineer?" said Factor Vosk.

"No," said Kaelen, and his voice came out perfectly level, because he had spent seven years learning how to make it do that. "The lamp. The glass resonates with certain chimneys. I should have warned you."

She looked at him through the green lenses a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she smiled again.

---

"Let me be plain," she said. "The Consortium would like you to consult on the final calibration and to supervise the hold. In return, we offer three things."

She counted them off on long fingers.

"First, your arm. Our physicians in the Organum have had real success in arresting third-stage calcification."

That was a lie. There was no arresting the third stage. There were people who died slowly and people who died quickly, and the Consortium's physicians had studied a great many of both. He had read their reports. He let the lie lie on the table between them and did not touch it.

"Second, your badge. Four-oh-eight-A, reinstated, with seniority. The Chancellor-Magnate asked me to tell you herself that the door has never been closed." She let that settle. Ysolde Carrow, who had found him in a chandler's attic and taken him to dinner and told him he would change the world. *The door has never been closed.* "And third," the Factor went on, more gently, "your Hollow."

"It isn't mine."

"Nevertheless. The Church has taken an interest in Oakhaven's burl-sick. An Inquisitor is on the road from the capital with a warrant of purification; I'm told he is four days out." She folded her hands. "The Consortium would much prefer Oakhaven's workforce unburned. The dead don't dig. If the calibration is secured, I believe I could make a persuasive case to the Church that the Hollow is a Consortium matter, and that the Consortium has it well in hand."

"And if it isn't secured?"

"Then I expect I shall be very busy with other things for the next nine days," said Factor Vosk, "and quite unable to spare a thought for the Inquisitor's warrant."

"It's a chance, Kaelen," said his father.

It was the first thing Aldric had said since *sit*. He said it low and rough, looking at the table, and there was something in it Kaelen had not heard in his father's voice since he was a boy, and it took him a moment to name it. Hope. His father thought he was helping. His father had written to the Consortium, and sent a Synod soldier to fetch his son across the town in front of everyone, because it was the only way he knew to open a door.

"And the Cantor?" said Kaelen.

"Will be very well compensated," said the Factor. "Four hours. The collar is removed afterward."

The collar was removed afterward. The wire was not. The wire had been threaded through the vocal folds on a silver needle and knotted into the tuning collar on either side, and when the Rite ended — for those it had not ended — the Tuning Men had cut the knots and drawn the wire back out through the folds, and it had not come out. It had torn. He had heard it described. He had not been there. He had been in an infirmary on the Umbral Tiers of Sol-Luminary with his arm in a tub of ice-water, screaming at a note he could not make stop.

"I'll need to see the bore," Kaelen said.

The Factor smiled a real smile. She reached into her coat and laid two things on the table between them: a small purse, heavy with Consortium scrip, and a disc of green Root-glass the width of his palm, stamped with the drill-and-wheel.

"Show that at the quarry gate," she said. "Any hour, day or night. The purse is an advance against your fee. For your trouble this morning."

He took them both. It was the easiest thing he had done in seven years, and he hated how easy it was.

---

When the Factor had gone, taking her lenses and her drawings and her Legionnaire with her, his father rose heavily, went to the sideboard, poured two cups of apple-brandy, came back, set one in front of Kaelen, and sat.

Kaelen did not touch it.

"Thank you," said Aldric.

"Don't."

His father drank. He drank the way he had always done everything, like a man with a job to get through. Then he set the cup down and looked at his son properly for the first time.

"You'll do it, then."

"Who's the Cantor, Da?"

A long silence.

"You know who," his father said.

"Say it."

"Lyra Ashgrove." Aldric's hands lay flat on the table. "She'll be paid. Paid enough to leave the Reach if she wants — go south, go somewhere the sky's still blue. It's four hours. The Factor gave me her word."

"Do you know what the wire is?"

"Then tell me." Aldric leaned forward. "You're the engineer. You were at the Organum ten years. You were there, when the Spire went. Tell me what the wire does, Kaelen, and I'll go to the Factor myself and tear up the paper in front of her. Tell me."

He opened his mouth.

And the explanation was the Foundry. It was forty girls on the risers in white shifts, and the one on the top tier bleeding into her collar on the ninth morning, and the censer-chain creaking, and the question coming up out of the stone like a hand out of deep water, and his own fork falling from his own fingers, and three hundred days of looking away. It was a procurement order he had signed without reading. It was his name in a title block.

He closed his mouth.

His father watched him do it. Something in Aldric's face, which had been open for the length of a breath, closed too.

"No," his father said quietly. "I didn't think you would."

"You sold them," said Kaelen.

"I fed them."

Aldric did not raise his voice. He had not raised it in years. But his big hands had closed into fists on the table, and his voice had the old iron in it, the Governor's voice, the voice that argued with weather.

"You were in the Organum the winter the Spire broke. You were in a hospital bed with the best physicians the Consortium could buy. I was here. The orchards went in an hour — you've seen the dust. The wheat came up wrong. By midwinter we'd eaten the seed-corn. By Candlefast we were boiling harness leather. I buried the Wetherby twins, and the Hale girl, and the miller's three, and eleven more that winter, Kaelen — *eleven* — in ground so hard the sexton had to burn fires on it to get the spade in. And in the spring the Consortium came up the Greenroot road with forty wagons of grain and a paper." He sat back. "And I signed it. And I'd sign it again tomorrow. Don't you look at me like that. Don't you *dare*, with that on your arm. You drew the pictures, boy. I only sold them the ground."

He was right. That was the thing. He was right about all of it except the part he did not know.

"Da," Kaelen said. "Your hair."

His father's hand went to his left temple before he could stop it — the reflex of a man who had been hiding something so long he had stopped noticing he was doing it. The long grey hair had slipped. Beneath it, above the ear, was a square of clean linen, and beneath the linen's edge, pale as a grub, a root-tip. A thumbnail long. Turning very slowly toward the lamp.

Aldric smoothed his hair back over it.

"The Grafters are discreet," he said, "with the Governor."

"How often?"

A pause. "Twice a month, now."

The Governor who had sold the Root, putting out roots. Kaelen looked at his father and could not think of one thing to say, and his father looked back, and neither of them said it.

"You never came to the door," Aldric said at last. "When you came home. You stood at the gate."

"You never came to the gate," said Kaelen. "You stood on the step."

Both of which were true. The town told it both ways because it had happened both ways: a young man with his arm in a sling of glass standing at the gate of the Governor's house in the snow, and an old man standing on the step with the door open behind him and the warm light spilling out around him, and the two of them looking at each other across the yard for as long as it took the snow to cover the young man's shoulders. Then the young man turning and walking away up the North Gate road, and the old man going back inside and closing the door.

Kaelen stood up.

"Your room's as you left it," his father said, to the table. "I had them air it every spring."

Kaelen stood with his hand on the back of the chair and his arm ringing, and there was nothing in him anywhere that knew how to answer that.

He went out.

---

The square was full of pale noon light. The skyship rode at the mast above the roofs, turning slowly on its line. Under his boots the bore ground on at the Root, and under the bore, very far down, the vast slow breathing that was not a sound went in, and out, and in.

He stood by the well-house with the Consortium's purse in one pocket and the Consortium's glass in the other, and made himself think about it like an engineer. That was what he was for.

Nine days. A bore he had designed, walking down to a casing he could hear, on a Sequence someone in the Arbiterium had improved into something he did not yet understand and did not like. A unison hold on a single local source, which could only be one throat. An Inquisitor four days out with a warrant for the Hollow. A smuggler at the mast who would not ask questions.

Every structure had a note at which it wished to fail. The bore was a structure. He had written the book on finding that note.

Nine days to break a machine he had designed. Nine days to get Lyra Ashgrove out of the Reach without telling her how he knew what the wire would do to her.

He was fairly sure he could manage the first.
