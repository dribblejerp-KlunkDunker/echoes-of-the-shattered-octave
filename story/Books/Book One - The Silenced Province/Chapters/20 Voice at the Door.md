## CHAPTER TWENTY
### Voice at the Door

> *It cannot read. It cannot guess. It knows nothing about you that was not said out loud within its hearing, which is why the old women say: do your weeping on paper.*
>
> — *A Plain Account of the Cacophony*, Null-Watch Press

*The Cloister of the Lost Choir. Nullday morning.*

---

They left her at the Cloister because she had no voice, and she understood the arithmetic, and she hated it.

"Somebody has to hold the fallback," Vane had said, in the orchard, not looking at her. "If it goes wrong at the Malting we come back up this road at a dead run with the Office behind us, and I want a door that opens and somebody behind it who knows what to do with a wounded man."

Which was true, and was not the reason, and everyone standing in the wet grass knew it. The reason was that a raid needs people who can shout.

So Lyra Ashgrove sat in a children's choir-room two hundred yards from the harvest-house with a slate on her knees and a nine-foot Office automaton standing against the far wall, and listened to the shift-bell ring the seventh hour, and waited.

After a while, because waiting was unbearable, she got up and started taking the cloaks off the pegs.

---

They came off their hooks stiff as board, furred green at the shoulders, and the damp had got into the wool so deeply that they weighed twice what they should. She laid each one on the end of the risers and did what her mother had done with the winter things every autumn: turned back the collar, looked for the tape.

Reach mothers sewed names into children's clothes. Everybody did. A cloak is a month's work and a cloak is handed down, and in a town where thirty children leave the same schoolhouse at the same hour, a name-tape stitched inside the collar is the difference between your coat and somebody else's.

Whisper-Grove mothers had done it too.

*M. ORRY*, in cross-stitch, red on undyed linen.

*BEN DRAY* — the choir-mistress's own, then.

*A. SOWERBY*, in a hand that had run out of room and put the Y up the side.

Lyra sat down on the bottom riser with that one in her lap for a while.

Then she went on, and wrote them on the slate as she went, in chalk, very small, so they would all fit: Orry. Dray. Sowerby. Halloran. Two Veskes, probably sisters. A Marchant. A Tipp. Nine, and then a tenth with the tape gone and only the holes where it had been.

Ten cloaks. Eleven pegs.

She looked up.

The Aegis stood against the west wall where it had put itself, head bowed under the barrel ceiling, perfectly still except for the small uneven *tick* somewhere in its chest. The red cloak hung over the fingers of its left hand, exactly where it had been since the orchard wall.

Lyra crossed the room and stood in front of it and held out her hand.

Nothing happened for long enough that she began to feel foolish. Then the lilac point of light behind the grille came round and steadied on her face, and the great hand closed.

Not fast. Not a snatch. The fingers folded in over the red wool with the slow deliberation of a thing being put away in a pocket, and the arm came in against the bronze chest and stayed there.

"Please," said Lyra, with no sound at all.

The Aegis Sentinel looked down at her and did not give it up.

She stood there a moment longer. Then she touched the back of its hand — the bronze was cold, and there was a dent in it where Kaelen's fist had landed — and went back to the risers and wrote on the slate: *RED CLOAK — NAME UNDER HIS HAND. HE WON'T.*

Outside, the fog had thickened until the orchard wall was a suggestion.

And the voice said, from the porch, "Lark."

---

She did not move.

Two years of practice held her: you do not turn, you do not answer, you walk on at exactly the pace you were walking. She sat on a children's riser with a slate on her knees and a dead girl's cloak across her lap and did not turn around.

"*I know you're in there, lark. I can hear you breathing. You always did breathe through your mouth when you were frightened; your mother used to say you sounded like a kettle.*"

Her father's voice. Warm, a little hoarse, patient. Two hundred yards away the shift-bell was still ringing at the Malting, and under it, faintly, people shouting.

"*Your friend in the hat was very kind to me,*" it said, in Tam's cracked tenor, companionably. "*Walked right out into the meadow for you, he did. Didn't make the high note.*"

Then: "*Lyra.*"

And that one was Kaelen.

Not an approximation. Not the way it had taken Davin Hale's voice in the tavern, a half-beat behind and a quarter-tone flat. It had had five days with him. It had stood in the clearing outside a charcoal-burner's hut for two years listening to a man who talked to an empty room because nobody heard, and it had learned him the way it had learned her: completely, from the inside, with the rasp at the bottom and the Reach in the vowels and the particular flat way he said a thing he had decided not to feel anything about.

"*Lyra, I need to tell you something and I'm not going to be able to say it to your face.*"

She was on her feet.

---

The porch door stood open on a wall of fog. There was nothing in the doorway. The jitter was out past it somewhere, in the white, shifting, and the Aegis had come off the west wall and was moving — slowly, one piston at a time — to put itself between the risers and the door.

"*I calibrated the bore at the Dawning Spire,*" said Kaelen's voice.

Lyra's hands came up over her mouth.

"*The Carrow Bore. Revision three of the Sequence was mine, and the pitch tables on the gantry were in my hand, and I walked that bit down through three hundred days of a living casing a hair under the failure-pitch, which was the finest piece of work anyone in the Consortium had done in forty years, and I was proud of it. I was proud of it until about nine o'clock in the morning on the three hundredth day.*"

The fog moved.

"*I signed for the collars. Line ninety-four. Forty collars, forty spools of wire, forty needles, between the lamp oil and the scaffold clips, and I didn't read it, and I have told myself for seven years that nobody reads line ninety-four, and that is true, and it does not help.*"

She had her back against the riser. The slate was on the floor somewhere. She had not heard it fall.

"*On the ninth morning I came down the gantry to check the resonance at the risers and the girl on the top tier was bleeding into her collar. Nineteen, maybe twenty. The blood had gone down the front of her shift and dried and gone down again over the top of it, so it had been days. And she looked at me.*" A pause of exactly the length Kaelen would have left. "*And I went back up the gantry. That's the whole of it. Three seconds, and I went back up, and I did not look at the risers again for two hundred and ninety-one days.*"

Lyra made a sound.

It was not a word and it was barely a noise — a torn scrape of air out of a throat that had nothing in it — but it was a sound, and in the fog beyond the door, the thing stopped talking at once and *listened*, with an attention she could feel on her skin like a hand.

She clamped both palms over her own mouth and held them there.

After a moment, patiently, it went on.

"*At the end it asked a question. The Spire. I don't expect anyone to understand that who wasn't in the room — there were no words, it doesn't have words — but it asked, and I knew what it was asking, and I had about six seconds, and I used three of them being sure.*" Kaelen's voice did not change at all. "*Three seconds. I dropped the fork and I shouted stop the bore and the Inquisitor said hold the note and they held it, and it let go, and eleven thousand and forty people died at Sol-Luminary, and thirty-nine of them were girls I had walked past every morning for ten months.*"

"*That's what happened to the sky, lark. That's why it's grey. That's me.*"

---

She got the slate up off the floor.

She was shaking so hard the chalk broke on the first stroke, and she wrote it anyway, in letters six inches high, and carried it to the door and held it up into the fog with both hands.

**IS IT TRUE?**

The fog said nothing.

She held it higher. She shoved it out past the door-frame into the white, into the air where the voice was, and stood there with her arms straight and her heart going like a bird in a box.

"*Lark,*" it said, gently, "*I can't read.*"

The slate came down.

"*I only know what people say out loud. That's all there's ever been. Your da said your name out loud in a yard in the spring; the old fellow sang in a meadow; your engineer talks to an empty room every night of the world and has done since the year the sky went grey, and you —*" and here the voice slid, without any seam at all, into Lyra's own, the whole one, the warm one, the one she had given it on forty-one nights on a stone behind a cooperage — "*you told me about Pim Carder's hands.*"

She shut her eyes.

"*So say it out loud,*" said her own voice, kindly. "*Ask me anything you like, and I'll tell you. Is it true? Did he say it exactly like that? Did he cry when he said it, the second winter? You can ask. Nobody's listening but me.*"

She did not answer.

It was the single hardest thing she had ever done, and she had sung a woman down inside a tree, and she had held a wire in her throat for four hours, and she stood in a doorway at the Cloister of the Lost Choir with her hands over her mouth and tears coming off her jaw and did not answer.

---

So it changed tactics.

"*Hold us in the little light,*" it sang.

In Lyra's voice. The Cloister's hymn, from out in the fog, forty feet off: the tune Sable had cracked her way through an hour ago, played back perfectly, in Lyra's whole unbroken voice, by something that had heard it once.

Behind her, the Aegis Sentinel turned toward the door.

Lyra got in front of it.

She did not think about it; there was no time to think about it. Nine feet of bronze came off its mark with the floorboards flexing under it, DEFEND THE SINGERS stencilled across its chest and a singer outside in the fog, and Lyra Ashgrove put herself in the doorway with her back to the thing that wanted her and her arms out wide across the frame.

The Aegis stopped.

It stopped with its leading foot eighteen inches from her and its head bowed under the lintel, and for the second time in two hours Lyra watched a machine try to hold two instructions at once. The chest louvres lifted an inch and closed. The tick in its chest went very fast and then settled. The lilac light moved from her face to the fog and back to her face.

"*Hold us in the little light,*" sang the fog, "*until the morning comes.*"

Lyra kept her arms across the door.

It sang the hymn seven times.

Then it sang "Apples in the Rain," off-key on the fourth bar, in her father's voice. Then it sang the harvest home, all the lines, in Davin Hale's. Then it did the thing that she had not braced for, because nothing could have braced her for it: it made the sound of a seven-year-old boy whistling a falling fifth, badly, with too much air in it, the way Pim had done it on his own doorstep in the sunshine the day before the Rite.

Lyra stood in the doorway of the Cloister of the Lost Choir and bled from the mouth where something in her throat had torn again, and did not make a sound, and did not move.

---

It stopped when the others came up the road.

They came at a run and they came loud — Vane bellowing, the press on a hand-cart with two wheels, Mags one-armed on the shafts, Tobiah under one corner of it, Kaelen behind with his ribs and a woman slung over his shoulder who was screaming the same four words over and over, and behind all of them the Office's shift-bell still going and a column of smoke standing up over the Malting.

The fog went out of the orchard the way a held breath goes.

Lyra came down off the step with her arms shaking and her mouth full of blood, and Kaelen saw her and stopped dead in the road with the screaming woman on his shoulder.

"What happened," he said.

She looked at him.

Seven years. The stile when she was eight and he was nine and hopeless at the Root note. The letters that stopped. The arm in the glass. *I was supposed to hold something together, and I didn't.* Her hands on his wrist in her father's kitchen with the green warmth going up into the blue. *Don't shake.*

"Lyra. *What happened?*"

She picked up the slate. There was nothing on it; she had wiped it against her skirt at some point without knowing she had done it. She stood in the road with the chalk in her fist and found that she could not write the question either, because the moment it existed in chalk it would exist, and then one of two things would be true, and she was not ready for either of them to be true in a road in the fog with a woman screaming eight feet away.

She wrote: *IT FOLLOWED US. IT'S GONE NOW.*

And watched him read it, and watched him believe her, which was the worst part.

"Get her aboard," said Vane, behind them. "Get *everything* aboard, there'll be an Office cutter over this wood inside the hour—"

And as they went down the Cloister road through the orchard with the press jolting on the hand-cart and the Aegis bringing up the rear with a red cloak folded in its fist, the fog on their left said, in Kaelen's voice, so quietly that Lyra was almost certain nobody else heard it:

"*Ask him, lark. He'll tell you. He's been waiting seven years for somebody to make him.*"
