## CHAPTER NINETEEN
### The Tuning Room

> *Engineer V. is not to be informed of the object of Stage 9. His calibration is measurably more accurate when he believes the object is extraction.*
>
> — Arbiterium cover-sheet, Rite of Pure Unison, 411 SR

*The Malting, Cloister road. Nullday morning.*

---

The presses were bedded on stone, exactly as the requisition said, and the requisition had been written by somebody who had never had to move one.

Kaelen got the kiln-floor door open at the sixth minute and had the tackle rigged by the tenth: a two-fall purchase off the malt-hoist beam, which was oak, ten by ten, and had been carrying two-hundredweight sacks since before the Synod existed. Tobiah Marsh ran the fall. Mags stood in the freight doorway with a broken collarbone and a hand-axe and looked enormous. Vane went along the wall lifting things and putting them down again in a manner that suggested that if anybody came in he would be delighted.

The press itself was four hundred pounds of frame and fork and ampoule socket, held down by eight coach-bolts through the stone bed. Kaelen had the plate off the first bolt-box in ninety seconds. He had cracked ribs, an arm that had rung flat since the choir-room, and a spanner, and he worked the way he always worked, which was the only thing he had that still functioned properly.

"Sir," said Tobiah, on the fall. "Sir, there's a door."

There was a door. At the back of the kiln floor, past the racks: a plain one, with a brass plate.

**ARCHIVE — OFFICE OF THE CATALOGUE — RECORDS RETAINED BY STATUTE**

"Bolts," said Kaelen.

"Yes, sir. It's only — you said the Office keeps everything, and if they keep everything—"

"Bolts, Marsh."

But his hands had already stopped.

---

The Office of the Catalogue keeps everything. It is the whole of its nature. It keeps intake sheets and requisitions and carriage notes; it keeps duplicate schedules; and because the Synod's departments bill each other for services and a billing department needs cause, it keeps the cross-files of every other department that has ever asked it for anything.

The Consortium of Bores had asked the Office for forty small clear voices in 411.

The archive was a long low room under the kiln, cold, dry, smelling of dust and brass polish, with shelves to the ceiling and a rolling ladder and a table with a lamp. The shelves on the north wall were cores: hundreds of them in racks, tagged, in the dark, murmuring faintly to each other like a room of sleepers. The shelves on the south wall were paper.

He found the box in four minutes because it was exactly where a man who had spent a decade in the Organum's filing system would have put it.

**CONSORTIUM — DAWNING WORKS — RITE OF PURE UNISON — 411/412.**

He put it on the table and opened it and stood there with the lamp.

His own pitch tables, in his own hand, from the Foundry. The daily calibration returns with his initials at the foot of each one, three hundred of them. The procurement order — one page, two hundred lines, line ninety-four *collars, tuning, brass, forty (40); wire, copper, filament, spools (40); needles, silver, curved (40)* — with *K.V.* against it in his own pen, because a chief engineer signs for what comes into his works, and he had signed for it between a requisition for lamp oil and one for scaffold clips.

Under that, the schedule of the Rite. Under that, the casualty return for the Great Foundry Rupture, eleven thousand and forty at Sol-Luminary, of which thirty-nine were listed as SKY CHOIR (CONSECRATED), which meant the Church had decided they were relics rather than corpses.

And under that, at the bottom of the box, clipped inside a plain grey folder with the Arbiterium's small scales stamped in the corner, four sheets of paper in a small, exact, very beautiful hand.

He had last seen that hand in a title block in the Greenhall in Oakhaven, eleven days ago, improving his own work.

The cover-sheet was dated the ninth of Greenleaf, 411 — eight months before the Fracture.

He read it standing up, with the lamp in his fist, and he read it twice, and then he sat down on the archive steps.

*The object of Stage 9 is not extraction.*

*The Anchors are not deposits and cannot be drained beyond their willingness. Eleven bores in forty years have demonstrated that a sustained extraction produces slippage, shatter, and collapse of the bore-head, and never yields above eleven percent of projection. The Consortium continues to believe this is a mechanical problem. It is not. It is a matter of consent.*

*An Anchor will yield without limit if it believes it has been relieved.*

*Accordingly the Rite of Pure Unison is designed to present to the Anchor of Light, in a form it cannot mistake, the appearance of relief: its own law, held externally, without deviation, for a period long enough to be credited. The instrument is forty throats tuned to a single pitch by wire. The duration is three hundred days. The date of the final morning has been selected to fall within the year the term expires.*

*The projected outcome is that the Anchor will release its law.*

*The secondary outcome, which this office regards as the primary value of the trial, is a demonstration that the method transfers to the remaining seven.*

There was a note at the bottom of the third sheet, in the same hand, smaller.

*Engineer V. is not to be informed of the object of Stage 9. His calibration is measurably more accurate when he believes the object is extraction. He has an unusually exact ear and a conscience of the ordinary sort; the two together are useful only if the second is not engaged. I would not waste him.*

*C. V.*

---

He sat on the archive steps for some time.

Seven years. He had done the arithmetic on that morning perhaps four thousand times: if he had checked the resonance at the risers on the ninth day instead of going back up the gantry; if he had read line ninety-four; if he had shouted a half-minute sooner. He had built an entire life out of the certainty that a better man standing where he had stood would have stopped it.

And here was a grey folder, eight months older than the morning, which said: *no, he would not have. That was the design. You were the part of the machine that had to believe.*

The relief came up in him like water in a cellar, and he put his head in his hands, and hated himself with a violence that astonished him, because he had no right to it — because relief is a thing you are allowed only if the dead get something out of it, and Coralie of the Sky Choir, fifteen years old, second riser, left shoulder, got nothing out of it whatsoever.

He put the four sheets inside his shirt.

Then he became aware that the room had been getting louder for some time.

---

It was the cores.

He had been hearing them since he came down the steps and had taken them for what they always were, four hundred sleepers murmuring in a rack. They were not doing that now. The whole north wall had come up in pitch — a hundred and forty voices in lilac glass, all saying different things, but saying them at the same *rate*, the way a crowd in a square falls into step without meaning to.

And under them, through the floor, through the stone bed the presses stood on, through the four hundred feet of clay and rock beneath that, the Anchor of Memory was reciting.

He had heard the Growth Chorister sing a lullaby that kept losing the tune. This was not that.

This was a voice going through a list.

It had the exact cadence of a thing being recited — the small pause, the rise, the drop, the next item — and it went on and on, and every so often it would stop, and go back, and take the same passage again from an earlier place, and get further, and stop, and go back again. It was the sound Kaelen had made at eleven years old, in a cold room in his father's house, learning the eight laws by rote with a tutor's stick on the table. It was the sound of something that has forgotten a piece in the middle and is trying to get past it by running at it.

They had drilled this one in 416. They had taken enough out of it to unname a city.

And it was still down there, three years later, working at the list, trying to get the whole way through.

Kaelen went down onto one knee on the archive floor with his glass hand flat on the flagstones, because he could not help it, and the sound came up his arm and filled him to the throat, and for a while he was not in the archive at all.

*— and the eighth is Decay, and Decay is the rest between, and the rest is not silence but the shape that holds the —*

*— no. The eighth. The eighth is —*

*— begin again. The first is Growth, and Growth is the word more —*

"You hear them too," said a voice.

---

It did not come out of the room.

It came out of his arm.

Kaelen had spent seven years with a piece of the Dawning Spire set around his bones, and he knew every sound it made: the high thin ring on a still night, the hard sympathy near a struck bell, the full low note after it fed. This was none of those. This was a voice, speaking, at conversational volume, from inside the glass, in the tone of a man who has come to sit by a bedside.

He got up off the floor very fast and stood with his back against the shelves and his right hand over the seam at his shoulder, as though he could hold it shut.

"Forgive me," said the voice. "There is no gentle way to do this. I have tried several and they are all worse. Sit down before you fall down; your ribs are broken and you have been on your feet since dawn."

"Who is this."

"You know who this is. You have been reading my handwriting for eleven days."

The archive was very cold.

"Casimir Vaelen," said Kaelen.

"Supreme Arbiter, yes, which is a title I have come to find embarrassing. I would much rather you used the name." A pause; the sound of an old man settling in a chair, absurdly, inside his own arm. "You are wondering how. It is not sorcery, Engineer, it is material. I am eighty percent Dawning glass; I have been since the year your grandfather was a boy. The glass in your arm came out of the same throat as the glass in my chest. Two pieces of the same Spire are not two things. They are one thing in two places, and if one of them is patient and listens for a very long time, it can hear what the other one hears."

"How long."

"Seven years," said Vaelen. "Since the morning it set around your arm. I have heard every word you have said aloud in an empty room since 412, and I want you to understand at once that I am not proud of that, and that I never once spoke, because you were in pain and it would have been an intrusion."

Kaelen thought about a charcoal-burner's hut, and seven years of saying things out loud because nobody heard.

"Then you heard—"

"Everything. Yes. Including the things you said in the second winter, which I will not repeat and have never written down." The voice was quite gentle. "And I heard you last night in the hold of that ship, saying nine names over a lantern while you wrote them on a beam. Tamsin Reed. Aldric Vance. Davin Hale. Tam, no surname, *ask*. Orla Hale. Hob Wendle. Ada Pellow. Eight men at the pumps, no names, *find them*."

"Stop."

"You are keeping a list," said Casimir Vaelen. "It is the first sensible thing anyone in the Reach has done in a hundred years, and I would like you to know that I have one as well. Mine is longer. Mine has eight names at the top of it and one of them is dead because of a calculation I made in a grey folder, and you have just read the folder, and I am the only other person alive who knows what is written on the third sheet."

Kaelen stood in the cold with his hand on the seam.

"You used me."

"I did."

"You wrote that I had a conscience of the ordinary sort and that it was useful if it wasn't engaged."

"I did," said Vaelen. "It was accurate, and it was contemptible of me to write it down, and I have thought about that sentence more often than you would believe." A breath. "Engineer, listen. You have spent seven years believing you murdered a god by being careless. You did not. You asked it a question, in a form it could not mistake, because I designed the question and I put the words in your mouth." Very quietly: "But it *answered*. That is the part nobody else on this continent understands, and you and I both heard it. It was asked whether it might stop, and it said yes, and it said yes *immediately*, and it was so eager that it did not even wait for the end of the morning."

The cores murmured on the north wall. Under the floor, something enormous went back to the beginning of a list it could not finish.

"They are in pain," said Vaelen. "All eight. They have been in pain for longer than there has been a word for it, and they were promised relief at the end of ten thousand years, and the ten thousand years ran out in the year your Spire broke, and nobody came. I hear them every hour I am awake. So, now, do you. How long do you think you will last?"

"You want me to help you kill them."

"I want you to help me let them *stop*," said Vaelen, "and I would like you to notice that you did not, in fact, ask me what I wanted. You told me. Because you already know, because you were kneeling on this floor two minutes ago with your hand flat on the stone, listening to the Anchor of Memory try to get past the eighth law, and some part of you was thinking: *somebody should help it*." A pause. "I am not going to argue with you today. It would be premature and you would resent it. I only wanted you to hear a voice that is not lying to you, once, before the Church gets to you, because the Church will."

Kaelen said, through his teeth: "Get out of my arm."

"In a moment." The voice did not change at all. "Two things, and then I will leave you alone for a while.

"First: the Cantor lived. Stage Nine was run and the source survived it, which has never happened, and the Arbiterium's file on her is two inches thick and growing, and the Church has already asked for her back. You will not be able to keep her hidden, and you should stop trying, and you should start thinking about what she is *for*, because she is the most important person alive on this continent and neither of you has understood that yet.

"Second." A pause; and for the first time, something moved under the gentleness, and it was not gentle at all. "You have four sheets of my handwriting inside your shirt, and a boy upstairs with a copy of Revision Seven with my margin on it, and if those reach the High Patriarch or the Consortium's seven chairs, they will not read them as I intend them. They will read them as a method. Every drill on this continent will have a Rite bolted to it inside a year, and there will be forty children with wire in their throats at the foot of every Spire, and it will be done badly, by people who do not hear them, for fuel.

"I would very much rather that did not happen. You may take that as a warning or as a plea; I find I do not care which, provided you take it."

Above him, through the kiln floor, the shift-bell began to ring and did not stop, and a woman started screaming somewhere at the south end of the building, and Vane's voice roared something about the tackle.

"That will be your archivist," said Casimir Vaelen. "Go. And Engineer — the eight men at the pumps. Whisper-Grove stock, consigned to Oakhaven in the ninth month of 417, which means they will be in the carriage file in the box behind your left shoulder, third shelf, grey spine.

"I told you. I keep a list too."

The glass went quiet.

Kaelen stood in the archive for three more seconds. Then he turned and pulled the grey-spined box off the third shelf and tore it open and found the carriage file, and the manifest, and eight names, and he tore that page out and put it inside his shirt with the other four, and went up the steps into the noise with his ribs grinding and his arm ringing flat, and did not let himself think about how easy it had been to do exactly what he had been told.
