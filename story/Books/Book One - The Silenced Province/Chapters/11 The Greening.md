## CHAPTER ELEVEN
### The Greening

> *Growth is the kindest of the eight laws and the least merciful. It has no idea what it is doing. It only knows the word more.*
>
> — T. Ashgrove, Cantor's notebook, 398 SR

*Oakhaven. Hearthday afternoon.*

---

There were four hundred feet of service ladder between the Root-vault and the daylight, in a stone chimney a yard and a half across, and they went up it in the dark with the world growing underneath them.

Lyra climbed. Kaelen came behind her, which meant that when her hands failed on the fortieth rung and the sixtieth and the hundred and tenth, there was a glass shoulder under her boot, and a voice saying *up*, and nothing else, because he did not have breath for anything else.

The shaft below them was filling.

You could hear it. Not a roar — a roar would have been easier. It was a rustling. It was the sound a hayfield makes when the wind crosses it, except that it came from beneath her and it was getting closer, and every so often something would go past them in the dark: a tendril, rising up the wall of the chimney, pale as a root-hair and thick as a wrist, feeling its way toward the light with slow deliberate turns of its tip, the way a snail's horns turn.

She counted as she climbed. She could not help it. She had been counting since the cage went up without them.

*Twelve men on the pumps. Four in the cage. Eight left.*

Eight men in grey smocks standing at the handles four hundred feet down, working up and down, up and down, with nobody left on the platform to tell them anything else.

At the hundred and seventieth rung the chimney went suddenly warm, and green light came up past them, and Lyra looked down between her own boots for the first and last time.

The shaft beneath was full of leaves.

---

They came out through the winch-house into a world that had already ended and was extremely busy about it.

The quarry was a bowl of green.

It had been grey stone that morning. Now every terrace had split, and out of every split came growth — not the neat green of a field but the ugly hurrying green of something that has been held back and let go. Grass a foot high on the second terrace and three feet on the first. Brambles pouring over the tanker bays. A young iron-oak, already twice the height of a man, standing in the middle of the censer-platform with the white cloth of the Rite hanging off it like a torn sail.

The headframe was coming apart. Something underneath it — the shaft, the Root, the whole vast wounded thing below — was pushing up, and the iron legs of the frame were bending outward, very slowly, with a noise of rivets going like gunshots one at a time.

The day shift was running up the quarry road. The Legionnaires were not. Four of them stood at the gate exactly where they had been posted, bells lifted, with grass coming up through their pewter feet, because nobody had told them to do anything else.

"Cantor!"

Tobiah Marsh, at the rim, with his charts under his arm and half a shift-crew behind him, hauling an old man by the belt. He had lost his cap. He looked at Lyra, and at Kaelen behind her, and his face did something complicated and unfinished, and then he shouted, "The road's still open! It's going over the fields — the road's still—"

And then he was gone in the crowd, and the two of them came out onto the quarry road and saw the town.

---

Oakhaven was drowning in its own ground.

The Greening had gone up the quarry road ahead of them and it was going through the town from the north, a swell of green moving over roofs and walls the way water moves over a weir: not fast, exactly. You could walk faster. You could not walk faster for an hour.

The iron-oaks had doubled. That was the thing she could not make sense of at first, standing on the road with her hand over her mouth. The great trees on the common, which had been a hundred and fifty years old that morning, were now three hundred years old, and still going, their crowns swelling and splitting the sky, their roots heaving the common into long ridges like something shrugging under a blanket. Every one of them was ringing. The whole town rang like a struck carillon, on and on, and there was not one bell in it.

The Hedge was rising.

She had helped plant it, two summers ago, with the whole town on the bank and the Consortium's cartloads of wool. It had been three men deep and twice her height. It was four times her height now and thickening as she watched, blackthorn and bramble braiding themselves into a wall around the town the way bread rises in a pan, and the wall was closing over the North Gate.

The Hedge had been built to keep the town's sound inside.

"The gate," Lyra said, and could not hear herself. "*Kael* — the gate—"

"I see it."

---

They went down into the lanes.

Afterward she could never put the next hour into an order, but she kept the pieces, and the pieces did not go away.

The churchyard, heaving. The sexton's salt lay in white drifts along the paths, and it had not mattered at all; the grass over the graves stood waist-high and dark and unnaturally glossy, and the mounds beneath it had risen so that the ground looked like a bed with somebody stretching in it, and out of one of them, in the corner where the paupers went, something pale had come up through the turf that was not grass and not root and was still moving.

The bakery, with the door burst outward and dough coming down the step in a slow white tongue, and inside, the oven cracked open like an egg, and the baker's wife standing in the middle of it with her hands in her hair, saying *I punched it down, I punched it down, I punched it down.*

A carter on his knees in the middle of Weavers' Cross with his hands pressed to the cobbles, unable to lift them, watching the fine white root-hairs come out of his palms and go down between the stones and take hold. He was not screaming. That was the part she would keep. He was looking at his own hands with an expression of dreadful embarrassment, as though he had spilled something in somebody's house.

And the Hollow, when she reached it, where every person in Oakhaven who had ever had a root in them was —

— she did not have words for the Hollow. She had a Cantor's training and thirty-three years of the Reach and an entire language for the ways a body can go wrong, and standing at the mouth of the tannery lane she found that she had nothing at all.

She went in anyway.

---

She started singing at the well-head, because there was a crowd there and the green was coming up the lane behind them and they had nowhere to go.

The falling fifth. Full voice, torn, ugly, with the wire-hole still weeping under her jaw. The Root note, and down; and up; and down.

*Go back. Go back where you came from. Go back to being a road.*

The grass at her feet slumped.

It was not a miracle. It was a half-step let down, on a scale she had never in her life tried, and it cost her the way it had always cost her, and it worked in a circle perhaps eight feet across — a circle of stillness in a world that had stopped being able to stop — and where she walked, the circle walked with her.

"*Behind me!*" she shouted, between phrases, and her voice came out like a torn sheet. "In a line! Hands on the shoulder in front! *Behind me!*"

Hetty Bram got them into a line. Bettany got the children into the middle of it. The dye-works women came out of a doorway and took the old ones by the elbows, and Surgeon-Grafter Rooke came up the lane in his shirtsleeves with his bag in one hand and blood on both, and started pulling people out of doorways and pushing them into the column, shouting names.

And Lyra Ashgrove walked down the middle of her own town singing a corridor open, with sixty people in a line behind her and the green closing over the road behind the last of them, three feet back, the way a wake closes behind a boat.

She got them as far as the square.

---

"Orla," she said.

Rooke's face, up close, was grey. "Lyra—"

"You promised me you'd—"

"I could not get near her." He said it flatly, the way he said *past cutting*, and she saw what it cost him. "The floor's gone. The whole floor of that house is roots up to my knees and moving, and I am fifty-two years old and I could not get across it with a bag in my hand. I tried twice." He held up the little brown bottle of long drops, unbroken, unopened. "Take it. I'll hold the line here. Go on, girl. *Go.*"

She went.

The house with the blue door was gone under bramble to the lintel. Orla's, two doors along, was not. It was worse. The roof had come off it — not fallen; *lifted*, pushed up and aside by the thing that had been a chair — and the walls stood open around a tree.

It was a young tree, ten feet high, green-barked, shining, with a crown of tender new leaves that were still unrolling as Lyra came through the door frame, and it had grown out of the floorboards and up through the middle of Orla Hale's chair, and Orla was inside it.

Not crushed. *Included.* The bark had come up around her to the chest and closed there, and her hands were gone into the arms of the chair that were gone into the trunk, and roots the thickness of Lyra's wrist stood out of the floor around her like the buttresses of a chapel. Her grey hair was full of leaves. Her face was the only thing left that was hers.

Her eyes moved. They found Lyra, and lit.

"You're late, lovey," said Orla Hale.

Lyra crossed that floor. She did not remember how. There were roots in it that turned toward her boots as she came, and she stepped over them with the great care of someone stepping over sleeping dogs, and she got both hands on the old woman's face.

"Does it hurt," she said. It did not come out as a question. Her voice was almost gone.

"No." A rustling, delighted whisper. "I told you. It's like being held." Her eyes went past Lyra, up, at the missing roof, at the sky. "It's very green, isn't it. I can hear it growing. I can hear the whole Reach growing. Isn't that a sound."

The tree shivered. The bark at Orla's collarbone tightened by a finger's width, and the old woman's breath went out of her in a small grunt, and her eyes came back, and she said, quite clearly:

"Now, girl. *Now.* All the lines."

Lyra got the stopper out of the brown bottle with her teeth and gave her the long drops on her tongue, three of them, the way she had watched Rooke do it forty times.

Then she stood in the ruin of a house in the middle of a dying town, with her hands on either side of a face in the bark of a tree, and she sang the harvest home.

Full voice. Every line. *Oh, the last sheaf's in and the barley's high, and the swallows are low in the harvest sky.* Her throat tore on the third line and she went on through it and the sound that came out was barely human and it did not matter, because it was loud, and because Orla Hale's eyes were shut and her mouth had come open a little and she was listening, the way she had listened on the risers of fifty harvest homes to a whole town sing the thing she had started.

The fourth line, which Davin could never hold, and which his father could never hold, and which Orla Hale had held for fifty years in a voice like a rusty gate.

Lyra held it.

Somewhere around the sixth line, the old woman stopped breathing. Lyra finished the song. All of it. And then she put her forehead against the bark for the length of three breaths, which was all she had, because by then the hounds had heard her.

---

They came into the Hollow through the canopy.

Kaelen met the first one at the door of the house with his arm bare and blazing, and she did not see what he did to it, only the light of it go across the wall, and the sound — the sound she could not hear over her own ears ringing.

"Lyra." He had her by the wrist. "*Lyra.* The line's moving. Rooke's got them to the square and the square's going. The gate—"

"The gate's closed," she said.

"Then we open it."

---

They did not open it.

Four hundred people of the town of Oakhaven stood at the North Gate in the last clear ground left inside the Hedge — a lozenge of trampled mud maybe sixty feet across, with the green coming in on three sides at a walking pace, and the Governor's men and the Consortium's men and the Church's men all shoulder to shoulder in it with everybody else, which Lyra would think about for years.

And where the gate had been there was a wall of thorn thirty feet high and twenty feet thick, and it was still growing.

They went at it with axes. They went at it with hedging-bills and hay-knives and a Legionnaire's halberd somebody had pulled off a Legionnaire who was no longer using it. The thorn grew back faster than they cut, and after a while the cutting stopped, and the town stood in the mud in the green light and listened to itself breathing.

And the Hedge said:

"*Lark.*"

Lyra shut her eyes.

"*Lark, there's a way through down here. I can see it from this side. Come along the thorn to me, lark, and bring them with you.*"

Her father's voice. Warm, and a little hoarse, and patient.

Four hundred people turned their heads.

"*Tammy,*" said the Hedge, in old Tam's cracked tenor, from thirty feet further down. "*Tammy, there's a gap here. Bring the little ones first.*"

"*This way,*" said the Hedge, in Lyra's own voice — her whole voice, the one she had given it on forty-one nights on a stone behind a cooperage. "*This way. Follow me. I know the way out.*"

"*The last sheaf's in,*" sang the Hedge, very softly, in Davin Hale's voice, "*and the barley's high—*"

Somebody in the crowd made a sound like a hinge.

"*DON'T ANSWER IT!*" Lyra screamed.

It took everything she had left. It came out of her ruined throat like a plank splitting and she felt something tear that was not going to come back for a long time, and she got up on the mounting-block by the gatepost with Kaelen holding her by the belt so she could be seen, and she screamed it again.

"*DON'T ANSWER IT! IT ISN'T THEM! IT WAS NEVER THEM! WHATEVER IT SAYS, WHATEVER VOICE — DON'T ANSWER, DON'T GO TO IT, DON'T LET GO OF EACH OTHER—*"

And down at the north end of the thorn, Mistress Pellow said, "Tobin?"

She was fifty-one years old. Her youngest had had a marsh-fever two springs ago, and had been going quiet in her cot, going out like a lamp, and an orchard-cantor had stood up in that kitchen at four in the morning and said *Well, there's nothing for it*, and opened his mouth, and sung her back, and walked out into the yard singing so that the hounds would follow him and not the house. Mistress Pellow had put a bowl of new milk on Lyra's step every week of the two years since, and never once said why.

"Tobin Ashgrove," she said, to the thorn, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "is that you?"

"*Hello, Ada,*" said the Hedge.

She went into it.

The thorn did not part for her. That was the thing Lyra could not stop seeing afterward: it did not part. She climbed into it, with her arms up over her face, and it closed over her, and for four or five seconds there was the sound of a woman going into a hedge.

Then there was no sound at all.

And then, from somewhere on the other side, in the voice of Ada Pellow, the Hedge said: "*Oh — it's easy, come on. It's easy from this side.*"

Nobody else moved. That was the only mercy in it. Four hundred people stood in the mud and held onto each other and did not move, and the Hedge went on talking to them in the voices of their dead, patiently, name by name, for as long as they stood there.

---

Across the town, the Chapel of the Pure Note fell down.

They heard it go: a long grinding crash from the square, and a great gout of green going up where the roof had been, and then the thing that everyone in that crowd noticed in the same half-second, the thing that made four hundred people look up at once.

The Note had stopped.

Five days. Day and night, one note, without a break, until the town had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own blood. And now it was gone, and in the place where it had been there was nothing but the Greening: the crackle and stretch and sigh of ten thousand things growing at once, and the ringing of the iron-oaks, and the Hedge, saying their names.

The silence where the Note had been was the most frightening sound Lyra had ever heard.

Kaelen's hand closed hard on her shoulder.

"Lyra," he said. "Look up."

She looked up.

Out of the low green sky over the ruin of Oakhaven, brass-ribbed and patched and riding light as a leaf, with her lift-tanks burning cobalt and a line already uncoiling from her winch, came the *Drift-Kestrel*.
