# BOOK FOUR: THE RELIEF SHIFT

## PART ONE: SEVEN VOICES

## CHAPTER ONE
### The Keystone

> *You're heavy? — Heavy enough.*
>
> — The greeting and its answer in Bastion Fortis, where to be light is to be unreliable, and to be heavy is to be oneself

*Null-Watch, and Bastion Fortis. Driftday the sixteenth of Frostmonth, to Nullday the eighteenth.*

---

He noticed, on the morning they parted, that he did not mind.

He noticed it the way he had once noticed a hairline crack in a bore-collar at the Conservatory. Not with alarm. With interest. A thing that should not be there, and was, and would have to be accounted for. He stood on the wall of Null-Watch in the first snow of the winter, with the sack on his arm and the green under the skin of his throat. He watched the *Kestrel* rise off her mast in the grey and turn west toward the Rust Coast with Vane at the wheel and Mags at his shoulder and Vesper at the bow. He looked inside himself for the thing that should have been there, the way you look for a step in the dark.

It was not there.

He knew what it should have felt like. He had the memory of it, exact, the way he had the Vance Sequence: every figure, every tolerance. Three months ago, a year ago, seven years ago, a ship leaving with people on it he loved would have taken hold of him somewhere under the breastbone and pulled. He remembered that. He remembered lying awake. He remembered measuring the glass on his collarbone every morning with Lyra's knotted string, and the way his hand shook.

His hand did not shake now. He looked at it. Quite steady.

*You'll want to account for that*, said the engineer in the back of his head. *You'll want to write it down. Mote. Under the willows. Forty minutes after the cutter went into the trees. It found the worst thing and ate it.*

He had written it down. He had written it on a deck-beam of the *Equity* in grease-pencil, under a copy of the list of names he had started on a deck-beam of the *Kestrel* on the night after Oakhaven, the night Hob Wendle rooted. *Mote took the fear. 28 Ashfall. Edge of the Woods.* And under it, in the same square careful capitals: *I CANNOT FIND IT.*

He had looked at that for some time, the night he wrote it. He had thought: *I ought to be frightened by that sentence.*

He was not.

---

They had planned the parting on the Shelf, the night after the last card, while he sat on the cutter four miles off with the sack on his arm and Vesper's quiet round him. They had sent the plan up to him on a slate in Lyra's square capitals. It was the first time in two months they had told him a plan before it happened. He understood why. It no longer mattered what he heard, because he had said aloud to the glass on the first night of the press exactly what he meant to do, and there was nothing left to hide.

*I'm going to listen to them first,* he had said. On the cutter's foredeck, in the dark, to nobody, to the arm. *All seven. Before I come. I'd like to know what I'm talking about.*

And the old man had answered. Out of the bone, very quietly, a doctor at a bedside.

*Thank you. That's very fair. Listen carefully. Then come and tell me I'm wrong.*

So now they were going to listen. All of them, all over the continent, in the first week of winter, with the Longest Night fourteen days off. That was the night the old man meant to do it, under the Organum, with all eight. Tobiah had worked it out from the Engine Writ's cover-sheet on the Rust Coast, five weeks ago, and nobody had said the date aloud since. They said it now. There was no longer any reason not to.

*The Longest Night. The thirtieth of Frostmonth. Fourteen days.*

Vane and Mags and Vesper to the Rust Coast, to the trench off Cape Dissonance, where the Velocity root ran under the sea. And to Driftwood Cay, where Nell Carder and nine children had been waiting since Ashfall, because nobody trusted the Church to leave the Coast alone.

Sable alone to Whisper-Grove, to the Memory Anchor. She had written *alone* on the slate and underlined it twice, and nobody had argued, because nobody had ever won an argument with a line Sable had underlined twice.

Lyra to the Reach. To Oakhaven, what was left of it, to the Root, where she had sung the falling fifth four hundred feet down in Harvestmonth until a god let go of a question. With the Aegis, and Seraphina, and the woman with the broken wings and the priest. Then on, north and west, to the Heights, and the Light, and Sol-Luminary, where the line was three tiers down and not coming back up.

And him to Fortis. To the Keystone. Mass. With Tobiah on the *Equity*'s tanks, because Tobiah had asked, and because the *Equity* needed an engineer, and because the boy who had learned the Vance Sequence at fourteen had looked at him across the Shelf's fire with an expression Kaelen had catalogued and could not feel.

He had read the plan on the slate twice. Then he had written under it: *GOOD.* And handed it back.

Lyra had looked at the word for a long time.

---

She came up the rope to say goodbye at the first hour, before the *Kestrel* went, in the snow.

He was on the wall-walk between Towers Five and Six, where the Watch had kept him the first week, with the voiceless wind coming over the obsidian off the edge of the world. Two-Eleven had gone a little way off along the wall to talk about his dog to a battery crew, so that they would have the stretch of wall to themselves. Lyra came along it toward him in a heavy coat that had been Hetty Bram's, with the slate round her neck and her burned hand bound in clean linen. Her face was the colour of the snow.

She stopped in front of him.

She did not write anything. She looked at him. He let her. He stood there with the sack on his arm and the snow coming down between them and let her look, because he understood that she was looking for something and he wanted to know whether she would find it.

She looked at his eyes. At his mouth. At his throat, where the edge of the glass came up out of his collar, cyan, and above it, in the skin, the fine sea-green thread. At his hand, the right one, the flesh one, lying quite still on the parapet.

Then she took the slate off her neck and wrote, and held it up.

*ARE YOU THERE?*

He read it.

He thought about it carefully, the way he would have thought about a question on a calibration return. Not to be kind. Because it was a real question and it deserved a real answer.

"Yes," he said. His voice came out of him ringing, two notes now: the Light, and under it the drowned bell, green, like wind in reeds. "I'm here. All of me that was here before. I remember everything. I remember the orchards, and the Hedge, and the falling fifth, and you at eleven with your knees scabbed, telling me I'd hummed the root wrong." He looked at her. "I remember loving you. I can see it. It's all there, like a drawing. Every line of it."

She did not move.

"It just doesn't hurt," said Kaelen. "That's the only thing. The part that hurt is gone. And I'm finding, Lyra, that the part that hurt was the part that made it—" He searched for the word. He had always been bad at words. Figures were easier. "Heavy," he said. "In Fortis they'd say it was the part that made it heavy."

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she rubbed the slate clean with the heel of her burned hand and wrote again. It took a long time. Her hand was not steady. When she held it up the letters leaned, and the chalk had broken half-way through, and she had gone on with the stub.

*I CAN CARRY IT FOR BOTH OF US. FOR A WHILE.*

And under it, smaller:

*NOT FOREVER.*

He read it twice.

He knew that this was the moment. He could see the shape of it, the way he could see the shape of a load-path in a bore-frame. This was the moment where a man took a woman's burned hand on the wall of Null-Watch in the snow and said something, and the something mattered, and she would carry it to Oakhaven and back. He could see the shape of where it went. He could not find what went in it.

So he did the only thing he could find. He took her burned hand in his right one, gently, the way you take a bird, and held it.

"Not forever," he agreed.

She looked down at his hand holding hers. Then up at his face. And he watched her see it: that he meant it, and that it did not cost him anything to mean it. He watched that go into her like a blade going into a sheath, slowly, all the way to the hilt.

She took her hand back. She put the slate round her neck. She turned and walked away along the wall-walk toward the stair and the rope and the *Kestrel*, in the snow, and did not look back.

He watched her go.

*You ought to go after her*, said the engineer in the back of his head.

He knew that. He watched her go down the stair. He did not go after her. Not because he decided not to. Because there was nothing in him that moved.

---

The *Equity* went up off her stake on the plain at the third hour with Tobiah at the tanks and Kaelen at the wheel. Neither of them was a pilot. They flew her north-east toward the Titan's Spine the way two engineers fly anything: by the book, carefully, arguing about the trim.

Tobiah did most of the arguing. He was nineteen, and he had been nineteen the whole time Kaelen had known him, a long-faced boy with ink on his fingers and a gaff-wound under his ribs that still opened when he laughed. He had learned the Vance Sequence at fourteen and asked its author to sign his copy in a quarry office in Oakhaven three months ago. He had taken Pennick's tanks over the Valley with his side bleeding, and had not let go of the valve all night. He talked to the tanks the way Pennick had. *There's my girl. Don't rush her.* And he talked to Kaelen, all the way across the plain, about lift and ballast and the shocking state of the *Equity*'s number two cell, as if nothing had happened.

Kaelen knew he was doing it on purpose. He had watched Two-Eleven do it at the parapet for Vane. Laying a blanket of talk over a thing so that the thing would not have to be looked at.

He let him.

At the fifth hour, somewhere over the brown hills, Tobiah stopped talking about the cell. He stood at the tank-valves with his hand on the master, and looked at Kaelen at the wheel. For a while the only sound was the hiss of the tanks and the wind in the black bag.

"Sir," said Tobiah. He had called him *sir* since the quarry. He had never stopped. "Can I ask you something?"

"Yes."

"Does it hurt? The throat. Where the green is."

Kaelen considered it.

"Yes," he said. "Quite a lot. It burns when I talk. It burned when I took the hawser at the Watch, and when I threw the cutter in the Woods. It's burning now." He moved the wheel a quarter-spoke. "I can feel it. I just don't—" He stopped. "It doesn't frighten me. It hurts, and I know what it means, and it doesn't frighten me."

Tobiah was quiet for a while.

"I used to watch you," he said. "In the quarry. Before you knew who I was. I used to watch you come down the shaft at night with your arm in that sack, and you'd stop at the bottom of the ladder, every time, and stand there. For a minute. Just stand there with your hand on the rail." He looked at his own hand on the master-valve. "I thought you were checking the air. Afterward I worked out you were deciding whether to go on."

Kaelen said nothing.

"You don't stop at the bottom of the ladder any more," said Tobiah. "That's all. I noticed. That's all I wanted to say."

He went back to talking about the cell.

---

Fortis came up out of the sleet on the morning of the second day, and it was falling.

Not all at once. Not like a wall going down. Slowly, the way a man falls who has been standing too long, from the bottom up.

He saw it first as they came over the last ridge of the Titan's Spine into the valley under the Bastion. The city was carved into the mountain, the old drawings said, its battlements rain-swept, its lower levels descending into the Lodestone Vaults nine known levels deep. He had seen the drawings at the Conservatory. He had seen the Bastion wall once with his own eyes, in his first year at the Conservatory, from the window of a Tramway carriage. A sheer grey face of cut stone four hundred feet high across the mouth of the valley, with the city climbing the mountain behind it in terraces, and the magnetic mist lying over everything like breath on a cold window.

The wall was still there. The terraces were still there. The mist was still there, grey and heavy, dragging the *Equity*'s brass fittings sideways so that Tobiah swore and fought the trim.

But the bottom of the city was going.

He could see it from a mile off, in the sleet. At the foot of the terraces, where the lowest levels of Fortis met the valley floor, the ground was moving. Not shaking. Settling. The way wet sand settles when a wave goes back off it. Whole streets were tilted. Houses leaned against each other at angles houses should not lean, their roofs touching across lanes. A watchtower at the north end of the lowest terrace stood at twenty degrees from true with its bell still hanging plumb inside it. And as he watched, a long grey warehouse at the edge of the lowest terrace sank. Slowly. Into the ground. Like a man going down into a bog. Its roof was at street level, and then below it, and then the street was closing over the top of it like water.

No sound came up through the sleet.

"*Light*," said Tobiah, at the tanks, very quietly. "*Oh, Light. The floor's going.*"

"Mass," said Kaelen.

He watched the warehouse go under. He watched the street close over it. He felt the arm, under the sack, begin very faintly to hum. Not five hundred and twenty-eight. Lower. Three hundred and ninety-six. The note of the Keystone, coming up out of the mountain, through the mist, into the glass. Coming up thin and tired and wavering, like an old man's voice reading aloud a list he can no longer finish.

"The law's failing," said Kaelen. "From the bottom up. Something's taken the floor out from under it."

---

They came down at the Bastion's mast, on the third terrace. A woman met them at the foot of it who did not look as if she had slept in a month.

She was fifty, square, grey-haired, in a soldier's coat of heavy wool with the rain running off it and no mark of rank but a brass weight on a chain round her neck. A tuning-weight, calibrated. In Fortis, they said, the commander wore the city's weight. She stood at the foot of the mast with her feet apart and watched them come down the ladder. Her eyes went to Kaelen's arm in its sack and his open collar and stayed there.

"You're heavy?" she said.

"Heavy enough," said Kaelen.

She looked at him a moment longer.

"Hadda Steyn," she said. "Compact-Marshal. We had a chime from the Watch. The General said you'd come, and why." She did not offer her hand. In Fortis, he remembered, you did not offer your hand to a stranger; it was considered presumptuous, a claim on their weight. "She said you'd want to go down. To the Keystone."

"Yes."

"She said you'd want to go down whether I let you or not."

"Yes."

Hadda Steyn looked at him for a long time with her flat grey Fortis eyes.

"The General also said," she said, "that you're not afraid of anything any more. That something ate it. She said it in iron-cant, through a boy who reads it for her now, and the boy said it very carefully, as if it frightened him to say it." She looked at his throat. "I'll take you down. Not because you'd go anyway. Because I want someone who's not afraid to see what's down there. My Sounders are afraid. They're right to be. And I want someone to see it who'll tell me the truth about it afterward, and not what fear makes them say."

---

She told him on the way down. In complete sentences, and no more of them than necessary, which he had heard was the way of Fortis.

There had been twelve golems in the deep vaults. Crystalline. Living quartz, grown out of the mountain in the First Compact, ten thousand years ago, when the stone agreed to be the floor. That was the old phrase, and in Fortis it was not a phrase. The golems were the agreement. They lay in the deep vaults, in the dark, below the Lodestone, one to a chamber, and held the Mass law in the stone the way the Keystone held it in the world. Twelve. Three had cracked past function in the last hundred years. Eight were dormant, the way they had always been.

And one was walking.

"Orm marked it," said Hadda Steyn. "The Vault-Warden. Five years ago. *Ambulatory. Descending.* He reported it four times. The Synod sent a surveyor twice and the surveyor wrote *settlement* and went home." They were on a stair, cut into the mountain, going down, lit by oil lamps on iron brackets. The lamps swung very slightly, all in the same direction, toward the wall, as if something were drawing them. "It moved a yard a year. Then a yard a month. Since the summer, a yard a day." She did not look round. "Since Ashfall, it has been walking."

"Where?" said Kaelen.

"Down," said Hadda Steyn. "Past the ninth level. Past the vaults. Into the root-shaft. Toward the Keystone." She stopped on the stair and turned and looked at him in the swinging lamplight. "And as it goes down, the floor goes up. You saw it from the air. The lower town. Three hundred houses since the month turned. We had twelve thousand people living below the second terrace. We have moved nine thousand. The other three will not leave, or cannot, or were asleep." She turned and went on down. "The floor does not make a sound when it goes. Did you know that? You hear the house creak. Then you don't."

---

The Sounders were waiting at the ninth level.

Six of them, in heavy coats and iron-shod boots, with long tuned rods of black iron in their hands, as tall as a man. Their captain was a big man of forty with a broken nose and a face like a cliff, who looked at Kaelen's arm and then at his face and then said nothing at all. Hadda said his name was Ulf Garrow, and that he had lost eleven Sounders to soft floor in nine years. That he could hear the dead note in stone before any man in Fortis.

"Like tapping a drum with the skin off," said Ulf Garrow, when Kaelen asked him what it sounded like. "You tap. Stone answers. Good stone, heavy stone, it rings. Soft stone—" He tapped the floor of the stair-head with the foot of his rod. It rang. Deep, flat, solid. "That's good. That's heavy. Soft stone, you tap, and there's nothing. Like the skin's off. Like there's no drum. Just your rod going down into—" He stopped. "Into nothing."

"And then?"

"And then you've about two seconds," said Ulf Garrow, "if you're lucky. And you go back the way you came, and you don't run, because running is heavy and heavy goes through." He looked at Kaelen. "You'll walk behind me. You'll step where I step. You won't talk. If I stop, you stop. If I say back, you go back, and you don't ask why."

"Yes," said Kaelen.

Ulf Garrow looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned to his Sounders and nodded, and they went down into the dark below the ninth level, into the root-shaft, tapping.

---

He would remember the root-shaft as the place where he first understood what he had lost. Not the fear. What the fear had been for.

It was a natural cleft in the mountain, widened by hand ten thousand years ago, going down at a steep slant toward the heart of the Titan's Spine. The walls were black lodestone veined with silver, and the magnetic mist lay in it so thick that the lanterns burned sideways in their cages, and every iron thing on every man pulled toward the walls. The floor was a ramp of cut stone, worn smooth in the middle by ten thousand years of feet.

And it was loud with tapping.

The six Sounders walked ahead in a wedge, Ulf Garrow at the point. Each one tapped the floor ahead of him with the foot of his rod at every step: *tock*, *tock*, *tock*. A sound like a slow clock, six clocks out of time with each other. And every tap rang. Deep and flat. *Heavy.*

He walked behind Ulf Garrow, stepping where he stepped. Tobiah walked behind him, stepping where he stepped. Hadda Steyn walked behind Tobiah. And the arm under the sack hummed three hundred and ninety-six, louder with every step down, the Keystone's note coming up out of the dark ahead of them like heat out of a forge.

And somewhere below the Keystone's note, very faint, very far down, another sound. A slow grinding. A long sliding scrape, like a millstone turning, and then a pause, and then another. Rhythmic. Patient.

Footsteps.

"That's it," said Hadda Steyn behind him, very quietly. "That's the golem."

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

At the third hour of the descent, the tapping stopped.

---

Ulf Garrow had stopped. He was standing with his rod raised a little off the floor ahead of him, and his head on one side. All five Sounders behind him had stopped too, each with his rod raised, each with his head on one side. Like dogs at a sound.

Nobody moved.

Ulf Garrow lowered his rod. Very slowly. He touched the foot of it to the floor a yard ahead of him. He tapped.

*Tock.*

It rang. Deep. Flat. Heavy.

He let his breath out. Kaelen heard it go.

He tapped again, a yard further.

Nothing.

Not a dull sound. Not a hollow sound. *Nothing*. The foot of the rod struck the stone, and the stone made no sound at all, and the rod went on. A finger's width. Two. Into the floor. As if the floor were not there. As if Ulf Garrow had put his rod into still water.

Kaelen felt the Sounder's whole body go rigid in front of him.

"*Back*," said Ulf Garrow.

---

They went back.

All of them, at once, the way he had told them. Not running. Running is heavy and heavy goes through. Stepping backward, carefully, each man in the steps of the man ahead. The five Sounders turned. Ulf Garrow did not turn. He stepped backward up the ramp with his rod still held out in front of him, the foot of it in the floor where the floor was not. He was drawing it out slowly, the way you draw a stick out of a bog.

And the nothing came after them.

Kaelen saw it. He would see it afterward the way Lyra saw Bridie in the chair, the way Vane saw the hawser. He saw it, and he watched it, and he did not feel anything about it at all.

The floor of the root-shaft went quiet. That was the only word for it, the word Lyra had used for the Shelf. The stone did not crack or crumble or fall. It went quiet. It stopped being heavy. A line came up the ramp toward them in the lantern-light, out of the dark below. On one side of it the stone was stone, grey and worn and ringing. On the other side it looked exactly the same, and it was not there.

It came up the ramp faster than a man could walk backward.

The Sounder at the right of the wedge went first. A young man with a red beard. The line went under his heels as he stepped back. He went down into the floor. Not falling. *Going*. The way you go down into sleep. He went down to the waist, and stopped, and looked at his own legs in the stone. Then he looked up at Ulf Garrow, three yards away. His face did not do what a face should do.

It went soft.

He went the rest of the way down without a sound.

"*Back*," said Ulf Garrow. "*Back, back, back—*"

The second went. The third.

Kaelen was walking backward up the ramp in Ulf Garrow's footsteps with Tobiah's hand knotted in the back of his coat, and he watched. He watched the Sounders go into the floor one after another, without a sound, their rods going down with them. He watched the line come up the stone. He was counting. He noticed he was counting, the way Vane counted. *Three. Four.* He noticed that his heart was beating at its ordinary rate. He noticed that Tobiah behind him was making a sound, a high thin sound, over and over, and that he himself was not.

The line reached Ulf Garrow.

---

The big man did not try to step back. Kaelen saw him decide not to. He was standing on good stone, a yard in front of Kaelen, with his rod in both hands. The line came up the ramp toward his boots, and he looked down at it. Then he looked over his shoulder at Kaelen. At Tobiah. At Hadda Steyn behind them, on the ramp, with her hand on the tuning-weight at her throat.

"*Marshal*," said Ulf Garrow.

She looked at him.

He turned his rod round in his hands. The long tuned rod of black iron, as tall as a man, that had walked ahead of every patrol in Fortis for nine years and listened for the dead note in the stone. He held it out to Kaelen. Across the yard between them. Butt first.

"Tap," said Ulf Garrow. "Every step. All the way up. Don't run."

Kaelen took it.

The line went under Ulf Garrow's boots.

He did not go soft. Kaelen would remember that. The others had. Every face Kaelen had ever seen go into the quiet had gone soft, and relieved, at the end, and the big Sounder's did not. He went down into the floor to the knees, and the thighs, and the waist. His face stayed set like the cliff it looked like, and his eyes stayed on Hadda Steyn on the ramp above him. And his lips moved, and Kaelen heard what they said, because the shaft was very quiet now. All the tapping had stopped.

"*Heavy enough*," said Ulf Garrow.

And went down.

---

Kaelen tapped.

He turned round on the ramp with the dead man's rod in his right hand and the sack on his left arm. He put the foot of the rod to the stone ahead of him and tapped. It rang. He stepped. He tapped. It rang. He stepped. He went up the root-shaft in the dark, tapping, with Tobiah behind him holding his coat and weeping, and Hadda Steyn behind Tobiah not making any sound at all.

The line did not follow them.

It had stopped where Ulf Garrow went down. Kaelen looked back once, from thirty yards up, and saw it in the lantern-light. A place on the worn stone of the ramp where the stone did not look any different, and was not there. Beyond it, going down into the dark, the root-shaft. Below that, faint, very far down, the slow grinding scrape of footsteps, and a pause, and another.

Patient.

He tapped.

---

They came up out of the root-shaft at the ninth level at the seventh hour. Hadda Steyn went to the stair-head and stood with her back to them and her hand on the brass weight at her throat. She stood like that for a long time, and nobody spoke.

Then she turned round.

"Six," she said. "Ulf and five. That's seventeen Sounders in nine years." She looked at the rod in Kaelen's hand. "He gave you that."

"Yes."

"He gave it to you because you're not afraid." Her voice did not change. "Because you'd tap all the way up without running. He knew it. He'd heard about you from the chime. He gave it to the one man in the shaft who wouldn't run." She looked at Kaelen's face. "Was he right?"

"Yes," said Kaelen.

"Did you feel anything? When they went?"

He thought about it. He did not lie. He had stopped lying, he found, at about the same time he stopped being afraid. Lying had been for keeping things from hurting.

"I counted them," he said. "I noticed my heart was steady. I noticed Tobiah was crying and I wasn't." He looked at the rod. "I noticed he didn't go soft. Ulf. The others did, and he didn't. I noticed that. I filed it." He looked at her. "That's all."

Hadda Steyn looked at him for a long time.

Then she held out her hand.

He looked at it. In Fortis you did not offer your hand to a stranger.

"The rod," she said. "It goes to a Sounder. Not to you. You're not heavy. You're—" She stopped. She found the word. "You're *light*. Here, that's the worst thing we say about a man. It means you'll go where you're put. It means nothing holds you down." She held out her hand. "Give me Ulf's rod."

He gave it to her.

She held it for a moment in both hands, the long black iron rod that had walked ahead of every patrol in Fortis for nine years. Then she turned and went to the stair-head, where the only Sounder left on the ninth level stood. A young woman of thirty with a scarred face who had not gone down into the shaft, because somebody had to stay at the top. Hadda Steyn held out the rod to her.

"Dalla," she said.

The young woman looked at the rod. She did not take it.

"I can't hear it," she said. "The dead note. Not like Ulf. Nobody can."

"No," said Hadda Steyn. "Nobody can. Take it anyway."

Dalla took it.

---

They weighed him that night, in the Weighing Hall, because that is what Fortis does with its dead.

Kaelen had read of it at the Conservatory, in a footnote, and not believed the footnote. He believed it now. The Weighing Hall stood on the third terrace, under the Bastion's shoulder: a long vault of grey stone lit by oil lamps, with the sleet loud on the roof. Down the middle of it, in a row, stood iron scales. Great balances with pans the size of cart-wheels hung from beams of black lodestone that had been cut, Hadda said, from the first vault the Compact ever opened. And every wall of the Hall, from the floor to the curve of the roof, was cut with numbers.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Small, neat, chiselled into the grey stone in close columns, each with a name beside it and a date. *Garran Holt, 371, eleven stone four. Mera Vesk, 388, nine stone one. Thom Ard, 402, twelve stone eleven.* The weights of the dead of Fortis. Not their bodies. *Them*.

"When a soldier of the Compact dies," said Hadda Steyn, "we put his kit in the pan. His coat. His boots. His blade, his rod, his pack, whatever he carried. Everything that was his to carry. And we weigh it against the calibrated masses, and we cut the weight in the wall." She stood in front of the nearest scale with her hands behind her back. "In Fortis we don't ask whether a man was good. We ask what he carried. That's what heavy means here. Not his body. His load."

"And Ulf?" said Kaelen.

Hadda Steyn was quiet.

The floor had taken everything. His coat, his boots, his pack, his blade. Six men had gone down into the stone of the root-shaft with everything they carried, and the stone had closed over them, and there was nothing to put in the pan. Five Sounders and their captain, and the Hall had no kit to weigh.

Except the rod. And the rod was Dalla's now.

Hadda Steyn stood in front of the scale for a long time. Then she put her hands up to the back of her neck and unfastened the chain there, and took off the brass tuning-weight that hung at her throat. The commander's weight. The calibrated mass that every Compact-Marshal of Fortis had worn since the Bastion was cut, that was said to be the true weight of the city. She held it in her palm for a moment, looking at it. Then she laid it in the empty pan of the scale.

The beam tipped.

Not far. A tuning-weight is a small thing. But it tipped, and the other pan rose, and the lodestone beam above them groaned very slightly in its iron saddle.

"Ulf Garrow," said Hadda Steyn. "Sounder-Captain. Nine years at the head of the wedge. Six of his men with him." She looked at the scale. "He carried the city. Every patrol. Every tap. He carried all of us down that shaft and back for nine years." She did not take the weight back out of the pan. "That's his weight. Cut it."

The Hall-keeper, an old man with a chisel and a mallet, looked at her. Then at the weight in the pan. Then he went to the wall without a word and began to cut. *Ulf Garrow, 419, the Marshal's weight.*

Kaelen stood in the Weighing Hall with the sleet on the roof and watched the chisel go into the stone. He looked at the tens of thousands of names on the walls. He thought about what Hadda had said on the ninth level. *You're light. It means nothing holds you down.*

He looked down at his own hands. He wondered, with mild clear interest, what would go in the pan for him. The glass, perhaps. A hundredweight of it. Nothing else.

He filed that too.

---

Orm came to Hadda's rooms that night with the register.

The Vault-Warden was seventy and bent and white-bearded, with lodestone dust ground so deep into the creases of his hands that they looked carved. He carried a book under his arm the size of a paving-stone, bound in iron plates. He set it on Hadda's table by the lamp without being asked and opened it, and turned the pages with great care.

"Twelve," said Orm. "Since the Compact. One to a chamber, below the ninth. The Strata, the old books call them. The First Stratum to the Twelfth." He turned the iron-bound pages. Each page had a name at its head in the old cant of the Compact, square angular letters Kaelen could not read. Under each, in column after column of different hands down the centuries, a record. *Dormant. Dormant. Dormant.* Ten thousand years of it. "Every Vault-Warden since the first has walked the chambers once a month and written what he saw. Every month. I've done it fifty-one years."

He turned to a page near the end. The Ninth Stratum. Kaelen saw the column: *Dormant, dormant, dormant,* in a hundred hands. Then, five years ago, in a shaking old hand he guessed was Orm's: *Ambulatory. Descending.* And under it, every month since, the same two words.

"It left marks," said Orm. "In its chamber. Before it went. I found them when I went in after it." He looked at Kaelen with old pale eyes. "On the walls. Cut into the stone with its own hand. Tally-marks. Rows of them. Floor to ceiling, round the whole chamber. Thousands." He laid his carved hand flat on the page. "I counted them. It took me a year. Ten thousand and six tallies, in rows of a hundred. And then, at the end, by the door, one more row, a different shape, deeper, cut fresh." He looked down at the book. "I had the cant-scholar from the Weighing Hall read it. It was words."

"What did it say?" said Kaelen.

Orm closed the book.

"*IT IS THE TEN THOUSAND AND SEVENTH YEAR*," said Orm. "*THE TERM IS SERVED.*"

He sat in Hadda Steyn's chair with the iron-bound register under his hands and did not say anything else for a while. The lamp burned. The sleet beat on the shutters.

"The Compact of Stone had a term," said Hadda Steyn at last, quietly. "That's in the oldest books. Everybody in Fortis knows it the way you know a story. The stone agreed to be the floor for a term of years, and then the term would be served, and the stone would—" She stopped. "Nobody knows. The books don't say. It was ten thousand years away. It didn't matter."

"It was served in 412," said Kaelen.

They both looked at him.

"The Fracture," he said. "The year the Light let go. It wasn't the Rite that did it. Not only. The Light had been holding for ten thousand years on a promise. The Rite was the thing that broke it, but the term had run out underneath." He looked at the closed register. "They've all been counting. Every one of them. The Keystone. The Light. The Root. And the golems were the stone's own clerks, keeping the tally. And the Ninth finished its sum and cut the answer in the wall and got up and went down to tell the Keystone it was over."

Nobody said anything.

"I'm sorry," said Kaelen. "I should have said that more gently. I don't know how to any more."

Orm looked at him for a long time across the table.

"There is no gentle way," said the old Vault-Warden. "I've had five years to look for one." He put his carved hands on the register. "But there is a thing I haven't told the Marshal. There's a page in this book I can't account for. At the back. After the Twelfth. One more page, in the oldest hand of all, the first Warden's. With a name at its head and nothing under it. No record. No *dormant*. Not one line in ten thousand years." He opened the book again at the very back and turned it so that Kaelen could see. A name in square angular cant, alone on an empty page. "The cant-scholar read that too."

"What is it?"

"*The Fourteenth Stratum*," said Orm. "There's no thirteenth. I've looked. There's no chamber for a fourteenth. It's not in any vault. It's not anywhere." He closed the book. "But the first Warden wrote its name, and left the page empty. As if he expected someone, one day, to have something to write."

---

They went down again the next morning, Nullday, by another way.

There was an older shaft. Hadda Steyn showed it to them on a chart in her rooms on the third terrace, by lamplight, with the sleet beating on the shutters. A spiral stair cut by the First Compact, narrower and steeper, coming down on the Keystone chamber from the north. Nobody had used it in four hundred years, because the main shaft was easier. Nobody had tapped it since. It might be sound. It might not.

"I'll go," said Kaelen.

"I know you will," said Hadda Steyn. "I'm coming with you. So is Dalla. With Ulf's rod." She looked at Tobiah, sitting by the fire with his face grey and his knees drawn up, staring at nothing. "The boy stays."

"No," said Tobiah.

They looked at him.

"No," said Tobiah again. He did not get up. He did not look at any of them. He looked at Kaelen. "I'm coming. Somebody has to. Somebody has to be there who still minds." His voice cracked. "He can't. You can't, Marshal, you're too heavy, you'll just keep going. Somebody has to be there who'll *stop at the bottom of the ladder*."

Nobody argued.

---

The Keystone chamber was at the bottom of the world.

That was what it felt like. Four hours down the old spiral stair, in the dark, behind Dalla tapping with Ulf's rod. *Tock. Tock.* Every tap ringing. Down through the lodestone and the mist and the silver veins. Below the vaults. Below the root-shaft. Down to a place where the stair ended at a doorway of black stone, and beyond the doorway, a cave.

It was round. Perhaps two hundred feet across, domed, the walls and ceiling and floor all one smooth surface of something that was not stone and not glass and not metal. Dark grey, faintly lustrous, like the inside of a pearl in shadow. And in the middle of it, rising out of the floor, the Keystone.

He had seen drawings. They were wrong.

It was not a stone. It was a *weight*. That was the only way he would ever be able to say it. A shape about the size of a cart, roughly cubical, dark, hanging an inch above the floor of the chamber, not touching it. And it was so heavy that the eye could not hold it. Looking at it was like trying to lift it. He felt his own eyes drag downward. He felt his knees want to bend. He felt the whole of the mountain above him leaning on that one shape. Everything that had ever been heavy in the world held there, an inch off the floor, in the dark, the way a man holds a weight above his head when his arms are failing.

And it was humming. Three hundred and ninety-six. Low, thin, wavering. Under it, very faint, the sound of breath. Slow. Labouring. A breath in. A long pause. A breath out.

The arm under the sack began to burn.

He pulled the sack off.

---

The glass came out into the chamber, cyan, lit. The green vein in it blazed. The light of it went across the pearl-dark walls and onto the Keystone, and the Keystone took it, and the humming changed.

It did not stop. It *leaned*. The way the tenor bell of Wendmere had leaned toward his arm forty feet under the sea. The way the Light had leaned on Lumina. The whole of the Keystone's note turned toward him, slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice in the room.

And he heard it.

Not words. It had never been words. The Light, on the three hundredth morning, had not spoken; Solenne had said so on Lumina, and he had felt it himself. It was a shape. A shape in the hum, in the breath, in the weight. He felt it come into the glass and up through the bone and into his throat, into the green, where it burned.

*Is it you?*

He stood in the doorway of the Keystone chamber with his bare arm blazing and the god's question going through him.

*Have you come?*

And under the question, the thing Solenne had heard on Lumina. The thing the old man had heard for eighty years. The thing that made the old man weep in his white room and plan the Longest Night.

*Can I put it down now?*

---

He took a step forward.

He did not decide to. He noticed it after it had happened, the way you notice you have stood up. He was in the doorway, and then he was a step inside the chamber, on the pearl-dark floor. The Keystone was thirty yards in front of him, an inch off the floor, so heavy his eyes would not hold it. It was asking. *Is it you?*

He took another step.

He knew exactly what he was doing. That was the thing. He understood it with perfect clarity, the way he understood a load-path. The god was tired. It had been holding the weight of the world for ten thousand years. It had been promised that someone would come and take a turn. The Ninth, Sable called it; the old books in the Tomb of the Astrolabe; *the ninth will be born*. And he was glassed, and set, and singing, and the green was in his throat. He could feel how close he was. He could feel exactly how the weight would come off it and onto him. Like a load transferred from one beam to another. Clean. Simple. A matter of engineering.

He could do it now.

And there was nothing in him to stop him.

That was what he understood, in the Keystone chamber, at the bottom of the world, with his bare arm blazing and the god asking. That was what the fear had been *for*. It had not been weakness. It had not been a flaw in the bore-collar. It had been the thing that stood at the bottom of the ladder every night for seven years with its hand on the rail, deciding whether to go on. It had been the brake on the drum.

And Mote had eaten it. Gently. Believing it had been asked. And now there was a man with a glass arm walking toward a god in the dark, step by step, toward the weight of the world. Nothing in him said *stop*. Nothing in him said *wait*. Nothing in him said *Lyra*. Only a clear calm voice, his own, saying: *it would be easy. It would be right. They have been waiting so long.*

He took another step.

*Is it you?*

He opened his mouth to answer.

---

Something hit him from behind.

Not hard. A boy's weight. Tobiah Marsh, nineteen, with a gaff-wound under his ribs that opened when he laughed. He came across the pearl-dark floor of the Keystone chamber and threw both arms round Kaelen from behind, round his chest, round the bare blazing glass of his arm, and *held on*.

Kaelen felt the glass burn him. He heard Tobiah gasp. He heard the wound under Tobiah's ribs tear; he felt it, through his back, a wet warmth spreading. Tobiah did not let go. He dug his heels into the floor and pulled, backward, toward the doorway, with all the weight of a long-faced boy who had never been heavy in his life.

"*No*," said Tobiah into his back. "*No. No, sir. No. Not here. Not like this. Not when you can't even — not when you don't even—*" His voice broke in half. "*You stop at the bottom of the ladder. You stop. You always stop. Stop, sir. Please.*"

Kaelen stood still.

He did not stop because he wanted to. He stopped because there was a boy wrapped round him from behind, bleeding into his coat, holding on, and the boy weighed something. Not much. Enough.

He looked down at Tobiah's arms locked round his chest. At the ink on the fingers. At the burn coming up red on the inside of Tobiah's forearm where it lay across the glass.

*Heavy enough*, he thought. He did not know why.

He took a step back.

And another.

And the Keystone, thirty yards away in the dark, an inch off the floor, so heavy the eye would not hold it, went on asking. *Is it you? Have you come?* And its note did not stop leaning toward him. Not even when he reached the doorway, and Hadda Steyn took his bare glass arm by the wrist in both her hands, the way you take hold of a thing that is about to fall. Not even when Dalla put the foot of Ulf's rod against his chest and held it there like a bar across a door.

It went on leaning. It went on asking. All the way up the stair.

---

The old man spoke to him on the stair, in the dark, at the second hour of the climb.

He had been waiting for it. He had felt the attention come close in the chamber, closer than it had ever been. It had been leaning in through the glass as the Keystone leaned, listening as hard as the Keystone listened. Now, on the old spiral stair, with Dalla tapping ahead and Tobiah behind him with a bandage round his ribs and his arm in a sling, it spoke. Out of the bone, very quietly. Not a doctor at a bedside, this time. Something else. Something Kaelen had not heard in its voice before.

*You heard it.*

Kaelen did not answer. He was not refusing to answer, as he once had. He simply had nothing to say.

*You heard it ask. All of it. The way I hear it. Every day for eighty years.* A breath, slow and careful, with the bottle-sound at the end of it. *And you walked toward it.*

"Yes," said Kaelen. Aloud, on the stair. Tobiah behind him went still.

*Why did you stop?*

Kaelen thought about it.

"Tobiah held on," he said.

The old man was quiet for a long time. The stair went round and up in the dark. Dalla tapped. Every tap rang.

*I have never once walked toward it,* said Casimir Vaelen. Very softly. *In eighty years. I have listened to every one of them asking, and I have never once taken a step. I always knew I would not be able to stop.* A breath. *I'm glad the boy held on. I want you to know that. I'm glad.*

The attention eased back. Not gone. Never gone. A hand taken off a shoulder.

*Come to the Organum,* said the old man. *When you've heard the rest. Before the Longest Night. I'd like to talk about what it feels like to take a step. I've never been able to ask anyone.*

---

He wrote it on the deck-beam of the *Equity* that night, by lantern-light, with the cutter moored at the Bastion's mast in the sleet. Under the list of names. Under *Mote took the fear*. Under *I CANNOT FIND IT*.

*The Keystone asked. 18 Frostmonth. I walked toward it. 3 steps. Tobiah held on.*

*Ulf Garrow did not go soft.*

He looked at the second line for a while. He did not know why he had written it. He thought it was important. He could not feel why.

He filed it.

Then he went aft. Tobiah was asleep in the deckhouse on a pile of sailcloth, with his ribs bound and his arm in a sling and the burn on his forearm dressed in Hadda Steyn's salve. Kaelen sat down on the deck beside him in the dark. He did not know why he did that either. He sat there with his back against the bulkhead and the sack on his arm and the green burning in his throat. He listened to the boy breathe, slow and even. Under it, very faint, always, through the glass, he heard the Keystone four miles down in the dark, asking.

*Is it you?*

Somewhere below that, fainter, patient, a long slow grinding scrape. A pause. Another.

Footsteps. Going down.

He sat in the dark and listened to all three. The boy breathing. The god asking. The golem walking down to answer it. And he found that he was doing what he had once done at the bottom of every ladder for seven years. He was standing still. Not because anything in him was holding him. Because there was a boy asleep beside him who had held on, and he did not want to wake him by getting up.

*Heavy enough*, he thought.

He still did not know why.

## CHAPTER TWO
### Site Nine

> *What's she singing? — Ask the reef.*
>
> — The greeting and its answer on the Rust Coast, where the sea changes key with the tide, and a captain who forgets a shanty loses a ship

*Port Cantabile, Driftwood Cay, and the trench off Cape Dissonance. Hearthday the twentieth of Frostmonth, to Massday the twenty-first.*

---

They heard the Mole-bell before they saw the town.

Vane was at the wheel in the grey before dawn, riding the last of the high westerly down out of the cold. Four days out from the Watch, with two set-downs on the way, the *Kestrel*'s patched bag stiff with frost and her three tanks singing thin in the cradle. Mags was at his shoulder. Vesper was at the bow with her coat drawn tight round the thing in it, which had been leaning west since the Watch. Leaning toward the sea, toward the trench, toward the place it had come up out of.

And across forty miles of black winter water, faint, from the north, a bell.

One stroke. A long pause. Another.

He knew it before he knew he knew it. Everybody who had ever spent a week in Port Cantabile knew it. The bell at the head of the Long Mole, under the derrick, which the Harbour rang once for every soul that went into the water. Once. That was the rule. One stroke for one death, and then silence, so that the whole harbour would stop what it was doing and count.

He counted.

It did not stop.

---

He had stopped at forty by the time the town came up out of the dark. The cliffs of the Rust Coast, black and wet, rising out of the sea. The harbour in the bight of them, the Long Mole running out like a crooked arm, the derrick at its head. And above the harbour, climbing the cliff in six wet flights, the Salt Stair, which at night had always been a zigzag of small gold points, lamps on every landing, all the way up to the counting-house at the top.

Half the lamps were out.

Not out. *Gone*. The Stair went up the cliff in the grey, landing over landing, and from the third landing to the sixth it was black. Not dark: black. Burned. He could see it as the *Kestrel* came in low over the harbour mouth. The shops on the landings were roofless. The timber of the stair-rails was charcoal. And in the Lantern Yard below the Stair, where Hesper had kept four houses and the first vox-bottle had ever been opened, there was nothing at all but a long smear of ash running down to the water.

The Mole-bell rang.

*Forty-one.*

"*Oh*," said Mags, aloud, in the flat over-careful voice of the deaf. She had read it on the town. She did not need to hear the bell. "*Oh, no.*"

At the bow, Vesper did not turn round. She was looking at the black on the Stair. At the third landing, where there had been a chandler's shop with four blue bottles on the counter. At the top, where there had been a counting-house, which the Church had kept, and which she had walked into one night in Ashfall with her hooks. Every Flambeau in it had sat down against the wall with two crossed cuts across his belly and frost on the edges of the wounds.

"They came back," said Vesper. Very low. The hum of five years without air. "For that. They came back for that."

---

Oona Salt met them at the mast-tower in her admiral's coat with a burn across the back of one hand and a face like a closed fist.

She was fifty-six, built like a capstan. She read executions in the same voice as the tide-tables, and she did not nod to anybody. She stood at the foot of the sixty feet of iron rungs in the grey with the Mole-bell going behind her, and watched Vane come down. She did not say anything until he was standing in front of her.

"Five days ago," said Oona Salt. "Six cutters. Church. Under the sun-disc, with glass-fire. Two hundred Flambeaux out of the Basilica, the second chapter, the one the Patriarch keeps at home. They came in at the dead hour on the slack of the tide when the reefs were quiet. They went up the Stair from the bottom." She looked up at the black landings. "They burned the Yard first. Then the Stair, landing by landing. Then they went to the counting-house at the top and stood in the doorway and read something out of a book about the wages of sin, and then they went down again and burned what they'd missed."

"How many?" said Vane.

"The bell will tell you." Oona did not look round at it. "When it stops. Sixty-three, last count. We're still finding them. Under the Stair. In the water." She looked at him with her flat sea-grey eyes. "They didn't take anyone. They didn't arrest anyone. They didn't read anyone a writ. They just burned it. All the way up and all the way down. The man who led them said it was a *correction*." She said the word the way you would hold something dead at arm's length. "For the counting-house. For what was done to their brothers. They knew who did it. They said her name."

Vane did not look at the bow, where Vesper stood.

"And the Cay?" he said.

Oona Salt was quiet for a moment.

"They went out to the Cay at first light," she said.

---

He did not remember the next part as a sequence. He remembered it the way he remembered the Hanging Water: a number of things that were all true at once.

The *Kestrel* going out of the harbour mouth in the grey at a speed she had no business going at, on two tanks and a prayer, with Mags at the windlass and the trooper called Sixty at the valves. Sixty was a lean silent man from the Watch with a face like a closed door, who had asked to come and been told he could because the ship needed hands.

Driftwood Cay coming up out of the sea ahead of them, three miles out past the harbour mouth. A long low island of grey rock and grey scrub, with the burial ground on its spine where the Coast put its children in earth. Coll Madder's grave was there on the high rock. And at its north end, sheltered in a fold of the rock, the orphan-house. Hesper's. A long stone building with a slate roof and a yard, where the Coast's wreck-orphans lived, and nine children from Oakhaven, and a woman called Nell Carder. And a foundling boy of eleven from under a kitchen table in a counting-house, whom a pale woman with violet eyes had spared.

Smoke coming up from the north end of the Cay. Not much. A thin grey column, leaning in the wind.

Two cutters drawn up on the shingle below the orphan-house. White, with the sun-disc on their envelopes.

And Bettany, beside him at the wheel, who had come because she had brought those nine children into the world in Oakhaven with her own red hands and wanted to see their faces, saying, very quietly, "*Captain. Faster.*"

---

They came down on the Cay's north end at the second hour of the morning in a hail of glass-fire.

The Flambeaux had seen them coming. Of course they had; a brown skyship with a patched bag, low over the water in the grey, with a pale woman at the bow. Vane saw the crimson figures on the shingle by the cutters turn and look up, and saw the censers come up on their chains, and saw the glass-fire in the censers flare white-gold. Then something came up off the shingle toward the *Kestrel* like a thrown lamp, burning, and hit the bag over his head.

It did not burn through. The bag was oiled canvas and Pennick had treated it, once, against exactly this, with something that stank of fish. But it stuck there, burning, white-gold, the way glass-fire burned, high and bright and untroubled. And another came up and hit the cradle-frame beside Sixty, and Sixty swore, the first word Vane had ever heard him say, and beat it out with his coat.

"*Put her down*," said Vesper from the bow. "*Put her down, Vane. On the rock. By the house.*"

He put her down.

He brought the *Kestrel* in over the shingle and the cutters and the crimson figures with their censers, and over the spine of the Cay where the graves were. He put her down hard on the grey rock at the top of the yard of the orphan-house, a hundred feet above the beach, with her keel grinding and her bag sagging and the glass-fire still burning on it. Before she had stopped, Vesper was over the rail.

He saw her go. He saw her land on the rock in the yard in her quiet, with one whole side of her white and her coat open, and the hooks in her hands.

And he saw the doorway of the orphan-house.

---

It was a plain doorway. Grey stone, with a wooden door, standing open, and a lintel of grey slate with something carved on it that he could not read from the deck. And in it, filling it, with her arms spread across it from jamb to jamb, a woman.

Not Nell. Older. Broad, grey, square. He did not know her at first, because she was not in Bettany's grey apron with Bettany's bag. She had been standing at his shoulder at the wheel a minute ago. She had gone over the rail before Vesper, and he had not seen her go.

Bettany stood in the doorway of the orphan-house on Driftwood Cay with her arms across it. Behind her in the dark of the house, he could see faces. Small ones. Many of them. Pressed together in the dark, very quiet.

And coming up the yard toward her from the shingle, through the smoke, eight men in crimson with their censers lit.

"*Bettany*," said Vane.

She did not look round.

---

The Flambeaux stopped at the foot of the yard. Ten yards from the door. Their censers swung on their chains, white-gold, and the glass-fire threw their shadows long and black up the grey stone of the house toward the woman in the doorway. The man in front was older than the others, with a grey beard and a face that had been burned once, long ago, down one side. He had a book in his hand. He held it up.

"*Woman*," he said. "*Stand aside. This house sheltered the murderess of the counting-house. It has been named for correction.*"

"There's children in here," said Bettany.

"*The house has been named.*"

"There's children in here," said Bettany again. Not loud. In the voice Vane had heard her use in the hold of the *Kestrel* for two months, to men with gaff-wounds and women in labour and a boy who would not let go of a valve. "Twenty-six. The youngest is four months. I brought nine of them into the world myself. In Oakhaven. In the Reach. Before you sang it under." She did not move her arms from the doorframe. "You want the house, you'll have to come through me."

The man with the burned face looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked past her, into the dark of the house. At the small faces. And Vane saw something go across his face. Not mercy. Something else. Something tired and terrible and entirely certain.

"*The Pure Note does not distinguish*," said the man with the burned face. "*That is its mercy.*"

He lifted his censer.

---

Vesper reached him first.

Vane had seen her fight. On the Mole-head in Ashfall, in the dark, at the dead hour; he had been on the other end of it. He had never seen her fight like this. She came across the yard from the rock in her quiet, low and fast, without a sound. Her coat was open, and the thing inside it was not straining toward the sea any more. It was very still, the way a cat goes still when it has seen what it is going to do.

She went through the eight Flambeaux like a scythe through standing wheat.

He did not see all of it. Nobody could have. He saw the hooks, which were not metal and were not anything, which looked like the place on a wall where a picture used to hang. He saw them go into crimson cloth, and out, and the frost come up white on the edges of the cuts, and no blood anywhere. He saw a censer fall on its chain and roll down the yard, still burning, white-gold, high and untroubled. He saw the man with the burned face turn toward her with his censer up and the glass-fire flaring.

He saw the glass-fire hit her.

It caught her on the white side. The left, the side the Bleach had taken in Sol-Luminary when she held her quiet over seven of the Unison in open light. Glass-fire, the Church's fire, that burned as long as faith did, white-gold. It took her coat and her shoulder and the side of her face. And she did not scream, because she never made a sound, and she did not stop.

She put both hooks into him.

He sat down in the yard of the orphan-house against the low stone wall, with his censer in his lap still burning and two crossed cuts across his belly with frost on them. He looked up at her. His burned face was quite calm.

"*It does not distinguish*," he said again. As if it were a comfort.

"No," said Vesper. Her voice was very low and very dry. Her coat was on fire. "Neither do I."

---

The second cutter on the shingle had men in it.

Vane saw them too late. He was coming down off the *Kestrel*'s rail onto the rock with *Last Rites* in his hand and Mags behind him with the boat-hook. Vesper was in the yard among the bodies, beating at the fire on her coat. He saw, down on the shingle, on the deck of the second cutter, a Flambeau standing at the rail with a long gun. Not a censer. A gun. A long-barrelled thing with a brass bell on the muzzle, the kind the Church's marines carried. Loaded with something that was not lead.

He saw it come up. He saw where it was pointing.

Not at Vesper. Not at him.

At the doorway.

"*Bettany*—"

He fired *Last Rites*. A hundred feet, downhill, at a man on a moving deck, in the grey. The long Fortis-made pistol with the brass furniture and the bell engraved on the barrel, which had never once in twenty years missed anything he pointed her at. The Aether-Glass pellet went down the yard over the low stone wall and across the shingle like a struck spark.

It took the man with the long gun in the throat.

He fell. The long gun fired as he fell. Vane saw the flash, and heard it a moment later, a flat hard crack off the rock. And something went up the yard from the shingle. Not a ball. Something that burned. A streak of white-gold, glass-fire in a shell, and it went into the doorway of the orphan-house.

---

He reached her before Nell did. He would always remember that. He was nearer.

She was still in the doorway. She had not moved. Her arms were still across it, jamb to jamb, and her face was set like a woman carrying water up a hill. The front of her was on fire.

Not much. A small fire. White-gold. High and bright and untroubled, on the front of her grey dress, over the place where her heart was. Burning very quietly, the way glass-fire burned, without smoke, without sound. Burning as long as faith did. And the man who had fired it had faith, Vane thought, with the cold clerk in the back of his head. He had a great deal of it, and he was lying on the shingle with a pellet of Aether-Glass in his throat, dying, believing, and as long as he believed, it would burn.

Bettany looked at him.

"*Don't let them*," she said. Very calmly. "*Captain. Don't let them out the front. Take them out the back. There's a door to the kitchen-garden. Take them out the back.*"

He beat at the fire with his hands. It would not go out. He tore his coat off and threw it over her and it went through his coat. White-gold, quiet, untroubled. He could feel the heat on his face. It was not like ordinary fire. It did not spread. It did not leap. It simply burned, in one place, steadily, down.

Behind her in the doorway, the faces. Small ones. Very quiet.

"*The back*," said Bettany. "*Captain.*"

And then Nell was there, Nell Carder, thin and fierce and twenty-eight, with Pim at her skirts. Nell took Bettany's face in both hands and said her name. Bettany looked at her. Her face changed. Not pain. Something else. She took one arm down off the doorframe, the left. She put her hand on Nell's cheek, red to the wrist, the way it always was, from a hundred births.

"*Twenty-six*," said Bettany. "*Nell. Count them. Every one.*"

"I will," said Nell. "I will. I will, Bet, I'll count every—"

"*Count them every night,*" said Bettany. "*So you know.*"

And down on the shingle, a hundred feet below, on the deck of a white cutter with the sun-disc on its envelope, a man with a pellet of Aether-Glass in his throat stopped believing, because he stopped. And the fire on the front of Bettany's grey dress, white-gold, high and bright and untroubled, went out.

She was already gone.

---

They counted the children in the kitchen-garden behind the orphan-house at the fourth hour, in the wind, with the smoke going up from the shingle where Mags and Sixty had fired the two white cutters. Nell did it. She stood in the garden among the frozen bean-rows with a child on her hip and Pim at her skirts, and counted them, out loud, touching each one on the head.

Twenty-six.

Hesper was alive. She was sitting on an upturned bucket by the garden wall with her arm in a sling, a big slow woman with a voice like a foghorn, and she did not say anything. She watched Nell count.

And the foundling boy. Eleven, thin, dark, with the face of a child who had been under a kitchen table when the world ended once already. He was standing at the end of the row of children, a little apart. He was looking at Vesper.

She was sitting on the low wall of the kitchen-garden with her back to them all. Mags was binding the burn on her shoulder and face with strips torn from a petticoat. The coat was gone. Burned off her. The thing that had been inside it was in her lap, small and pale and soft, lit from inside with a night sky. It was not purring. It was looking at the boy.

The boy looked back at it.

Then he looked at Vesper. At the white side of her face, and the burn across it now, raw and weeping, over the white. At the violet eyes, which were looking at nothing.

"You," said the foundling boy.

Vesper did not turn round.

"You were the one," said the boy. "In the counting-house. Under the table. You looked at me and you didn't." He swallowed. "And they came here because of you. They said your name. On the beach. They said it before they came up." He was very still. "Miss Bettany's dead because of you."

"Yes," said Vesper.

The boy looked at her for a long time.

Then he walked down the row of children and across the frozen garden to the wall where she sat. He stood beside her. He did not touch her. He looked at the thing in her lap, which looked back at him with its night-sky eyes. Then he sat down on the wall next to her, a foot away, not touching. Facing the other way, the way she was facing. Looking out at the sea.

He did not say anything else.

Neither did she.

---

They buried Bettany on the high rock at noon, next to Coll Madder, because the Coast put its children in earth and Hesper said she had been a mother to more children than anyone on the Coast had ever buried.

Vane carried her up. He would not let anyone else. He carried her up the path along the spine of the Cay past the little graves. There were so many little graves, rows of them on the grey rock, each one with a flat stone and a name cut in it and a small bell hung on an iron hook. The Coast hung a bell on a child's grave so that the wind would ring it, and the child would not be alone in the silence. Hundreds of them. Ringing very faintly in the wind off the sea, a high thin scattered chime, like the sound of a shop door opening and closing far away.

Nell walked behind him with the twenty-six. Mags and Sixty and Hesper. And Vesper last, with her face bound and the thing in her arms, and the foundling boy a foot away from her, not touching.

They dug the grave in the grey earth on the high rock beside Coll Madder's. Coll had died on the Salt Stair under a rail the engineer pulled, a father of a nineteen-day-old daughter. They put Bettany in it in her grey dress with the burned place on the front. Nell put Bettany's bag in with her, because she had never gone anywhere without it.

Then Hesper said, in her foghorn voice, "She was a grown woman. She doesn't get a bell."

"She gets a bell," said Nell.

Hesper looked at her.

"She gets a bell," said Nell again. Her voice did not shake. "She brought nine of them into the world. She stood in the door. She gets a bell, Hesper, or I'll go down to the harbour and steal one off the Mole."

Hesper looked at her for a long time. Then she went down the path to the orphan-house and came back with a small brass bell from the kitchen. The one they rang for supper. She hung it on an iron hook at the head of the grave, and the wind off the sea took it.

It rang.

---

He went to Site Nine on Massday, in Oona Salt's own boat, because Oona told him what it was. And because he had nothing left on the Coast to do but count, and he was sick of counting.

She told him on the Long Mole on the night of Hearthday, after the Mole-bell had stopped at seventy-one. They stood at the head of the Mole under the derrick, where she had hanged Ormond Dace under the Twelfth Article in Ashfall with her hand flat on his chest. They looked out at the black water toward the south, where the sea went down to Cape Dissonance and the trench.

"After the counting-house," said Oona, "Sable gave me the Prior's book. The receiving-book. You know. Forty bottles a month as tuning stock, to the Hall of Carriage." She was looking at the water. "There was another book under it. In the strongroom. The Prior didn't keep it; he was only minding it. A survey. Synod survey, Siphon Corps, on Arbiterium paper. Charts of the trench off the Cape, from before the Fracture. Soundings. Bore-depths." She did not look at him. "Coordinates for a platform. On the lip. Marked on the chart in red, with a number. *Site Nine.*"

Vane did not say anything.

"I didn't know what it meant," said Oona. "Not at first. I'm not an engineer. I sent it to Hesper's brother, who was on a Synod bore in the Valley before the Valley broke. He wrote back. He said it was a siphon platform. He said they bored it into the trench in '13, to draw on the Velocity root that runs under the Cape. Under the trench. Where the trench goes down to the dark." She was quiet for a moment. "He said a siphon-bore opens the thing it draws on. Like a tap in a barrel. He said that the trench off the Cape was not always so deep, before '13. That it opened. That it was *opened*."

The wind came off the sea. Below them the reefs were singing, very faintly, the way they always sang, a low wandering chord in the dark water that changed key with the tide.

"In the spring of '14," said Oona Salt, "a ship called the *Wave-Cutter* was towing a hulk past the Cape. Over the trench. And the trench took the hulk, and started to take the *Wave-Cutter* with it. And her captain had ten heartbeats and an axe. And he cut the tow." She looked at him for the first time. "With his first mate on the hulk."

Vane looked at the water.

"It wasn't the sea," said Oona. "That's what I'm telling you. The sea didn't open under you. The Synod opened it. A year before. With a bore. To draw on the root. And it's still there. Site Nine. Still drawing." She looked back at the sea. "The same Synod that paid for your ship. The same Synod you carried crates for. Thirteen hundred and forty-two of them." She said it without cruelty. She said it the way she read the tide-tables. "I thought you should know. Before you go and die for something. I thought you should know which something it was."

---

Oona's boat was a fast cutter-rigged ketch called the *Merret*, after her brother. She took it down the coast herself on the morning of Massday, with Vane and Mags and Vesper. And an old woman of eighty-four, whom Oona carried aboard in her arms and sat in the stern-sheets wrapped in a sealskin coat.

Auld Bessa. The last Reef-Reader on the Rust Coast.

Vane had heard of her. Everybody had. The reefs off the Coast sang, and they changed key with the tide, so that the pitch of the ocean told you the hour, and the depth, and the set of the current. A captain who could read the reef by ear could take a ship through channels that would gut a ship whose captain could not. There had been a trade of it once: Reef-Readers, a guild, with apprentices. There was one left, and she was eighty-four, and she had two apprentices, and neither of them was good enough.

She sat in the stern-sheets of the *Merret* all the way down the Coast with her eyes closed and her head on one side, listening. Every so often she said something to Oona in a cracked thin voice, and Oona put the helm over, and they went through a gap in the white water that Vane would not have tried in a skiff.

"*She's singing in the low key*," said Auld Bessa, once, to nobody. "*The winter key. She's been in the winter key since the Fracture. Seven years of winter.*" She listened. "*And under it—*"

She stopped.

Oona looked at her.

"Under it what, Bessa?"

The old woman did not answer for a long time. The *Merret* ran south down the black coast in the cold, under a sky like iron.

"*Under it, she's asking*," said Auld Bessa.

---

Site Nine stood on the lip of the trench forty miles south of Port Cantabile, where Cape Dissonance ran out into the sea. It was the ugliest thing Vane had ever seen. He had seen the walls of Null-Watch.

It was iron. Four great iron legs going down into the sea, rusted red, crusted with weed and barnacle, as thick as towers. On top of them a platform, square, perhaps two hundred feet on a side, and on the platform a derrick. Not a crane like the Mole's. A drilling-derrick, a skeleton tower of iron girders going up a hundred and fifty feet into the grey sky. Through the middle of it, going down, a pipe.

He could see the pipe from a mile off. Black iron, as wide as a house, going down through the platform and between the legs into the sea, straight down, into the water at the very lip of the trench. Where the shelf of the Coast ended and the sea went from green to black.

It was moving.

Not the pipe. The water round it. A slow turning swirl at the surface, round the black pipe where it went into the sea, like water going down a drain. And from the top of the derrick, a hundred and fifty feet up, a long low sound came out across the water toward them. Rhythmic. Patient. A great slow pumping, *whuff*, *whuff*, *whuff*, like the breath of something enormous asleep.

"*Still drawing*," said Oona Salt. "*Six years. None of ours has been out here in six years. Nobody comes past the Cape but the Synod's tender. We thought it was dead.*"

Vane looked at it.

At the bow of the *Merret*, Vesper had stood up. She was standing very still, with her bound face and the thing in her arms. It was not looking at the platform. It was looking down. Into the water. Straining, the way it had strained toward the edge of the world on the wall of Null-Watch.

"*That's it*," said Vesper.

Her voice was very low.

"*That's where I was. Five years.*" She did not look round. "*Not near it. In it. Down the pipe. Where it opens. I could feel it pulling, the whole time. Like something breathing in. I never knew what it was. I called it the cord. I used to lie there in the dark and feel the cord pulling, and think, that's how it feeds. That's the umbilical cord.*" She looked at the black pipe going down into the sea. "*I didn't know it had a top. I didn't know somebody built it.*"

---

They went aboard at the third hour.

Oona brought the *Merret* in under the platform, between two of the great iron legs, into the shadow. The water there was black and moving, turning slowly round the pipe. There was a ladder, iron, rusted, going up one of the legs. Vane went up it first, with *Last Rites* reloaded in his coat. Then Mags. Then Vesper, one-handed, with the thing in the crook of her arm.

The platform was manned.

He saw them as he came up over the edge onto the iron deck. Thirty of them, perhaps forty, scattered across the platform in the grey. Men and women in rags, thin, barefoot on the iron in the cold. They were at the pumps. Great iron pump-handles like the handles of a fire-engine, ranged round the base of the derrick in a ring, and at every handle two people, pumping. Up. Down. Up. Down. *Whuff, whuff, whuff.* Slowly, patiently, in perfect time.

Hollows.

He knew them at once. He had seen them in the quarry at Oakhaven, at the pumps, in Harvestmonth. He had seen them in the plazas of Whisper-Grove, walking their loops. Their faces were smooth. Their eyes were on nothing. They pumped the way the man on the Shelf had moved his nine stones, the way old Nan had twisted her hands: because it was the last thing they had been doing when the name was taken out of them, and there was nothing left in them to stop.

"Six years," said Vane. "Six years. Out here. On their own." He looked round the platform at the iron deck, the rust, the weed growing in the seams. At a pile of something by the derrick's foot that he did not look at twice. "Who's been feeding them?"

Nobody answered.

Nobody had been feeding them. Not lately. There was a tally chalked on the derrick-housing in a Synod clerk's hand: *tender, quarterly, meal and hands*, five years of it, the same mark every quarter. The last mark was Harvestmonth. Then nothing, since the Writ was read and the Synod split and nobody at a desk in the Organum remembered a platform off Cape Dissonance. He understood it slowly, as you understand cold. They were thin. So thin. Thinner than anyone on the Shelf. And the pile by the derrick's foot, which he did not look at twice. Hollows did not stop. Hollows did not feel hunger. Hollows went on doing the last thing they had been doing until they could not, and then they lay down by the derrick and somebody else, with the same smooth face, took the handle.

Six years, and nine weeks of nobody.

He stood on the iron deck of Site Nine and counted the people at the pumps. Thirty-eight. He did not count the pile.

---

Mags felt it first.

She had been standing at the edge of the platform with her one good hand on the iron rail, watching. She went very still. Then she knelt down on the deck and put her palm flat on the iron, the way she put her palm on a signal line, and closed her eyes.

Vane watched her.

After a while she opened her eyes and looked up at him. Her face was white.

She signed. Slowly, one-handed, the *Kestrel*'s hand-speech, a word at a time.

*Deck. Moving. Up. Up. Up.*

He did not understand. She signed it again, impatient, and then did something with her hand he had never seen her do. She laid it flat in the air, palm down, and moved it slowly upward, rising, the way a voice rises. Then, at the top, she turned it. A small tilt. A lift at the end.

*Up at the end*, she signed. *Like asking.*

And at the bow of the platform, at the rail, Vesper said, "*She's right.*"

She was standing with the thing in her arms, looking down at the black pipe going into the black water. The thing had stopped straining. It had gone quite still against her chest, with its night-sky eyes on the pipe, and it had begun, very softly, to hum.

Not five hundred and twenty-eight. Not Solenne's note. Lower. Six hundred and thirty-nine. The note of the drowned bell of Wendmere, the note in the green vein in the engineer's throat. The Velocity note, humming out of a small pale thing the size of a cat at the top of a pipe that went down into the dark.

"*It's coming up the pipe*," said Vesper. "*From the root. Under the trench. I can hear it. I could always hear it, down there. I didn't know what it was.*" She did not look round. "*It's the same thing. The same as the engineer heard on the mountain. The same as the Light.*"

And Auld Bessa, in the stern-sheets of the *Merret* in the shadow between the iron legs, a hundred feet below, eighty-four years old, with her eyes closed and her head on one side, said it aloud. In her cracked thin voice, to the black water turning round the pipe. Vane heard it come up through the iron.

"*She's asking*," said the last Reef-Reader on the Rust Coast. "*The whole sea. Every reef from here to the Siren Shallows. She's been asking for seven years. I've heard it every tide. I thought I was going mad.*" A long pause. "*It goes up at the end. Like a child does. Like a child at a door, in the dark, asking if it's you.*"

---

The Hollows began to sing.

He did not know how it started. He was standing by the derrick looking at Vesper at the rail, and Mags with her palm on the deck, and the black pipe. And the people at the pumps, thirty-eight of them, pumping, slowly, in perfect time, opened their mouths.

Not words. They did not have words. They did not have anything. They were Hollows; they had been emptied in a chair at a harvest-house and sent out here to pump, and the last thing they had been doing was pumping. And the thing that came up the pipe came up into the iron of the derrick, and the iron of the pump-handles, and their hands on the handles. And it came out of their mouths.

One note. Six hundred and thirty-nine. Thirty-eight smooth-faced people at the pumps of Site Nine, singing it together. In perfect unison, slowly, rising, the way the deck rose under Mags's palm. And at the top, at the end of each stroke of the pump, a lift. A small tilt upward. A question.

*Is it you?*

*Have you come?*

He stood on the iron deck in the grey in the cold, with thirty-eight people who had no names singing the god's question to him out of the mouth of a pipe the Synod had driven into the sea in '13. He did not hear any more of it. He found that he could not. He found that he had turned round and was looking at the pipe, the great black iron pipe going down through the platform into the water. At the turning swirl of the sea round it. At the place where, a year after it was driven, a hulk had gone down, and a tow-line had come taut, and a man with an axe had had ten heartbeats.

*It wasn't the sea.*

He had spent five years drinking so that he would not hear it. The ten heartbeats. The axe, and the spray, and her face. He had told himself every night for five years that the sea had taken her and he had only cut the line. That it was the right call. That anyone would have.

It was the right call. He had always known it was the right call. That had never been the thing that kept him drinking.

The thing that kept him drinking was that the sea had opened under him for no reason. Nothing had done it. There was nobody to hate. Only the sea, and the axe, and a choice.

And there had been a reason. It was standing in front of him. Four iron legs and a derrick and a pipe, and thirty-eight starving people at the pumps singing a god's question. Somebody had built it. Somebody had sat at a desk, as the scribe had sat at a desk and written *disposal*. They had drawn a red mark on a chart and written *Site Nine* beside it, and a year later the sea had opened under the *Wave-Cutter*. Vesper had gone down the cord into the dark and been held there and eaten and loved for five years. And the same somebody had paid for his ship, and he had carried their crates, thirteen hundred and forty-two of them, and not asked.

He laughed. The short one.

It did not die three feet from his mouth. It came out of him and kept coming, and it was not a laugh by the end. He stood on the iron deck of Site Nine with his hands on his knees, and Mags was beside him with her hand flat on his back, holding him up. He could not stop.

---

Vesper came and stood in front of him.

He looked up at her. At the bound face, and the burn under the binding. At the white side. At the violet eyes with the cold small points of light in them, like holes into a night sky a long way below the sea.

"You knew," he said. "The cord. You called it the cord."

"I didn't know it was built," said Vesper. "I knew it pulled."

"It was them," said Vane. "All of it. The trench. The hawser. You. It was them. The same ones." He straightened. Mags's hand stayed on his back. "The same ones I carried for."

"Yes," said Vesper.

"I cut the line," said Vane. "Over their hole. Over this. I cut the line, and you went down their hole, and it held you for five years. And then I went and carried their crates for two." He looked at the pipe. "Thirteen hundred and forty-two."

Vesper looked at him for a long time.

"I forgave you on the Mole," she said. "For the hawser. I told you it was the right call. It was." Her voice was very low. "I'm not going to forgive you for the crates. That's not mine to forgive. That's theirs." She looked at the Hollows at the pumps, singing. "Ask them."

He looked at the Hollows.

Thirty-eight smooth faces at the pump-handles of Site Nine, singing the god's question in perfect unison to the grey sky. Up and down. *Whuff, whuff, whuff.* None of them looked at him. None of them could.

---

They took the Hollows off Site Nine at dusk. All thirty-eight. It took four trips in the *Merret*, between the iron legs, in the black turning water. Each of them had to be lifted off the pump-handle, gently, finger by finger, and carried down the ladder. Each of them, when they were lifted off, stopped singing.

When the last one was off, the pumps were still.

And the pipe went on drawing.

He had not thought it would. He had thought the Hollows were the pump, that if you took them off it would stop. He stood on the iron deck of Site Nine at dusk with the empty pump-handles round him and the derrick going up into the dark. He listened to the pipe go on breathing. *Whuff. Whuff.* Slower. Deeper. Something at the bottom of it, far down in the trench, in the dark, drawing on its own now. Like a child that has learned to feed without being held.

"*It doesn't need them any more*," said Vesper beside him. "*It hasn't needed them for a while. They were just—*" She stopped. "*They were just still holding on.*"

The thing in her arms was humming six hundred and thirty-nine, very softly, with its night-sky eyes on the pipe.

Vane looked at the pipe. The great black iron pipe as wide as a house, going down through the deck and between the legs into the sea. At the top of the derrick, where it was hung from iron cables as thick as his arm. Four of them, going up to a great iron collar at the derrick's head and down to the pipe's flange.

He looked at the cables for a long time.

"*Somebody's going to have to cut that*," he said.

He said it quite quietly, to nobody, to the dark. Mags was beside him with her hand on his back. He felt her hand go still. He looked round, and she was looking at his mouth. She had read it. And her face, in the last of the light on the iron deck of Site Nine, did a thing he had seen it do only once before. On the deck of the *Wave-Cutter*, five years ago, with an axe in his hand and a hawser going taut over the trench, and ten heartbeats.

She signed one word.

*Who?*

He did not answer her.

He looked at the cables. At the black pipe. At the dark at the bottom of the trench, drawing, on its own now, like a child that has learned to feed. And he did the arithmetic. The weight of the pipe. The tension in the cables. The swirl of the water. What would happen to the platform, and anything on it, when the cables parted and the pipe went down into the trench with nothing to hold it.

He did not like the answer.

He did it again.

It came out the same.

## CHAPTER THREE
### The Shortening

> *— and I'll fetch the other one.*
>
> — Loop recorded in the Plaza of the Willows, Whisper-Grove, by the Silent Veil, in the first week after the Silencing, 416. In that year it was nine words long. The first three are not recorded anywhere, because the scribe who heard them wrote them down wrong and struck them out

*Whisper-Grove. Rootday the nineteenth of Frostmonth, to Hearthday the twentieth.*

---

It had snowed in the Woods. She had not known it could.

In three years she had never seen the Spectral Woods in winter. The Office kept its scribes in the Organum from Ashfall to the thaw, because ink froze in the plazas and the Hollows did not mind the cold and nobody else wanted to be there. She had always imagined it would look the same. Lilac. Silver. Luminous. The willows weeping into the phosphorescent pools, and the mist at the height of a man's chest, and the loops.

The *Kestrel* set her down at the edge of the Woods at the first light of Rootday, on its way west. It did not stop. Vane did not want to stop, with Nell and the children waiting on the Cay, and she did not want him to. She went down the mast-ladder with the Ledger under her arm in its oilcloth. She stood on the frozen ground and watched the ship lift away into the grey without her. Then she turned round and walked into the Woods.

It was white.

Not lilac. White. The mist had frozen. It hung in the air between the willows in a fine motionless rime, like the breath of something that had stopped breathing. It had settled on every branch and twig and every weeping silver strand, so that the willows were not silver any more. They were white, and stiff, and still, hanging over the pools like frozen fountains. The pools themselves were iced, and under the ice, very faintly, the phosphorescence still burned. Lilac, deep down, like a lamp seen through frosted glass.

And there was no sound at all.

She stood at the edge of the first clearing in the frozen mist and listened, and could not hear anything. Not her own breath. That was the cold, she told herself. Sound did not carry in cold like this. She stood and listened and told herself that, carefully, the way she would enter a figure in the Ledger she did not believe.

Then, faint, from somewhere ahead, from the direction of the first plaza, a voice.

A woman's. Saying two words. Over and over, at conversational volume.

"*—other one. —other one. —other one.*"

---

She knew the voice. She knew it before she reached the plaza, before she saw the doorway.

She had heard it in the first week after the Silencing, in the spring of 416. She had stood in the Plaza of the Willows with her pen and her Ledger and her Veil-grey coat, the youngest scribe the order had sent, nineteen years old, or perhaps twenty; she did not remember her own age now. She had listened to a woman standing in a doorway on the north side of the plaza, saying something over and over. And she had written it down, because that was what she had been sent to do.

*— and I'll fetch the other one.*

Nine words, the woman had said, that spring. The scribe who had stood beside Sable that day, an old woman called Hennet Moor who had been with the Veil since before Sable was born, had said them aloud as she wrote. *Nine words, girl, count them.* And Sable had counted them, and written three of them down wrong, because she had been nineteen and frightened and had never heard seventy-four thousand people forget themselves at once. And then Hennet had said, *never mind, we'll have it right tomorrow, she'll still be saying it tomorrow*, and Sable had crossed out the first three and left the last six. *— and I'll fetch the other one.*

And the next day the woman had said only eight.

She came into the Plaza of the Willows.

It was a sunken square of white marble, a foot under water in the spring. She remembered the water lit from below, and two hundred Hollows walking their loops through it, splashing. The water was ice now. White. Thick. The Hollows were standing in it.

Not walking. Standing. Frozen into the ice to the ankles, to the shins. Two hundred of them. Some were upright. Some had fallen and lay on the ice where they had fallen, with their faces turned up to the white sky. Their mouths were moving.

Some of them.

Most of them were not.

And on the north side of the plaza, in a doorway, a woman. Apparent forty. Grey dress. One hand on the doorframe, the way she had always stood, half-turned, as if to call back into the house. Her mouth was moving.

"*—other one.*"

Two words. Then a pause. Then the same two.

"*—other one.*"

Sable stood at the edge of the frozen plaza and looked at her.

*Nine words in the spring of 416*, said the scribe in her, who never stopped. *Four words in Harvestmonth of 419, when you sat in this plaza with the Ledger and Halder Wick set his table for four. Two words now. Two months.*

She did the sum.

She did it again.

---

She sat down on the steps at the edge of the Plaza of the Willows, in the snow, with the Ledger open on her knees. She did what she had come to do, what she had always done. She went round the plaza and wrote them down.

Not their names. They had none. Their loops. The thing each one said, or did, the only thing left of them. She had a list of them in the Ledger from Harvestmonth, two hundred and four loops for the Plaza of the Willows alone. Each one entered in her small clear hand with the date and the number of words. She went round the frozen plaza with the list and the pen and checked each one against it.

*A man by the fountain. "Mind the step, Wenna, it's wet." Harvestmonth: six words.*

He was lying on the ice by the fountain with his face turned up. His mouth was moving. She bent close.

"*—wet.*"

*One word.*

*A woman with a basket. "Two loaves and the small one, please." Harvestmonth: seven words.*

The woman was standing by the frozen fountain-rim with her basket on her arm. Her mouth was not moving. Her hand was. It went into the basket, and came out, and went into the basket, and came out. Taking two loaves and the small one. Over and over. Without a sound.

*Silent. Gesture only.*

*A boy—*

She stopped. She looked at the boy. He was sitting on the ice with his back to the fountain. He had sat there in Harvestmonth too, saying four words that she had written in the Ledger, *four, and then we go*. He had not aged, because aging requires memory of having been younger. He was twelve, perhaps. He would always be twelve.

His mouth was not moving.

His hand was. It lay on his knee, and the fingers moved, one after another, slowly. Counting. One, two, three, four. And stopping. And beginning again. One, two, three, four.

*And then we go.*

She wrote it down. *Silent. Gesture only.*

She did not look at the boy again.

---

It took her until noon to go round the plaza. Two hundred and four loops. She stood at the top of the steps with the Ledger and counted the marks she had made.

Eleven still spoke. One word, or two. Never more.

A hundred and seventy-one were silent, and moved. A hand into a basket. Fingers counting. A turn of the head toward a door. The gesture that had gone with the words, still going on after the words had gone, the way a clock's hands go on moving after the chime is broken.

Twenty-two did not move at all.

She went and looked at those. She knelt on the ice beside each one and looked. They were not dead. She could see them breathe, very slowly, a faint cloud at the lips in the cold. Their eyes were open. They were not looking at anything. They were not doing anything. They lay or sat or stood in the ice of the Plaza of the Willows with their eyes open, breathing, and there was nothing left of them that moved, or spoke, or did anything at all.

*Loop-shades*, the Office called the silent ones. She had read the term on a report in her first year and not liked it. The Office had a column for them. *Loop decayed to gesture.* And under it, a second column, which she had never seen filled. *Loop decayed to null.*

She looked at the twenty-two.

She wrote, in the Ledger, in her small clear hand: *Loop decayed to null. Twenty-two. The Plaza of the Willows. 19 Frostmonth.*

And then, because she was a scribe, and because there was nobody else to do it, she sat down on the steps in the snow and worked the curve.

---

She had worked it once before. In Harvestmonth, in this plaza, by the light of the pools, with Halder Wick setting his table for four. She had taken the loops she had recorded in 416, and the loops she had recorded in 419, and drawn the line between them. Nine words to four, in three years. She had extended the line. It had reached zero in 421. Two years and a little more. She had written it in the margin of the Ledger: *I will be here to find out. That is the entire point of me.*

She worked it again now. Four words to one, or none, in two months.

It was not a line.

It was a curve. And it was not going down gently. It was falling. Like a stone. Like a thing that has been held up for a long time and has been let go.

She extended it.

She sat on the steps of the Plaza of the Willows with the pen in her hand and looked at where the curve reached the bottom of the page.

*Eleven days.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she counted forward from Rootday the nineteenth, on her fingers, the way a child counts.

*The thirtieth of Frostmonth.*

*The Longest Night.*

---

She went to the Veil-house in the afternoon.

She had not meant to. She had meant to go to the Canopy. The Memory Anchor, the Weeping Canopy, the kilometre-high spire of fused wood and crystal at the heart of the Woods, where the Chorister of Memory had been reciting a list it could no longer finish since 416. That was what she had come for. To listen. All seven of them, the engineer had said. *I'd like to know what I'm talking about.*

But the Veil-house was on the way. And she found, when she came to the fork in the frozen path where the left way went to the Canopy and the right way went down into the deep Woods, that her feet had gone right.

She let them.

---

The Silent Veil had kept its house in the deep Woods for eleven hundred years before the Synod absorbed it. Everybody in the Office called it the Mausoleum of Chords now. That was the Synod's name, from a survey; it was on the maps. The scribes who had lived there had called it the House. Just that. *The House.* As if there were no other.

It was a long low building of grey stone, roofless now, on a rise above a frozen pool. Its walls were thick and its windows narrow, and its doorway was a round arch of carved stone with words cut over it in the old pitched notation of the Veil. She did not need to read them. She had walked under them every morning for — she did not know how many years. She did not remember. She remembered walking under them. She remembered the words. *What is written is not lost.*

She walked under them.

The roof was gone. The *Kestrel* had come down through it in Harvestmonth, holed, when they ran from the Office cutter with Jory Quill's footprints on the foredeck. The beams had been rotten for years before that. The snow had come in. It lay on everything. On the long tables of the refectory where she had eaten her breakfast. On the floor of the cloister, where she had walked. On the stairs.

She went up the stairs.

The scriptorium was on the first floor, at the east end, where the light came in first. A long room with narrow windows down both sides and desks under them in two rows, sloped, with ink-wells cut into their tops. A stove at each end. She had sat at the third desk from the east end on the north side for — she did not know how long. She remembered the desk. She remembered the stove at the east end, which was lit by the duty-scribe at the second bell. She remembered sitting at her desk in the dark before the second bell, in winter, before the stove was lit, because she liked to be first. She remembered the smell.

Cold stone. And ink. And the dust off the vellum. And very faintly, coming up through the floor from the kitchens underneath, somebody's breakfast.

It was the last thing she had of herself that she could identify. She had told Tessa Wray so on the Shelf. She had felt it in her for five weeks like the last coin in a purse, the weight of it in the corner, worn thin since the cellar at the Hall of Carriage. The smell of the Veil's scriptorium on a winter morning.

She stood in the doorway of the Veil's scriptorium on a winter morning, with the snow on the desks and the roof open to the white sky.

And breathed in.

---

It smelled of snow.

Of snow, and of wet stone, and of rot. The beams that had come down when the *Kestrel* came through the roof lay across the desks on the south side, black and soft. And under that, faint, of something she did not at first recognise, and then did. Old ash. The stoves had not been lit in three years. Somebody had burned something in the east stove, once, long ago, and the ash had lain there in the cold.

No ink. The ink-wells in the desks were dry, cracked, full of snow.

No vellum. The shelves along the west wall where the vellum had been kept were empty. Taken by the Office when it absorbed the order, to the Organum, to the archive, to be catalogued.

No breakfast. The kitchens underneath had been empty for three years.

She stood in the doorway and breathed in, and breathed in, and it was snow and rot and old ash, and nothing else.

And in her, in the corner of the purse, the last coin. The smell of this room on a winter morning. Cold stone and ink and vellum-dust and breakfast. Exact. Perfect. She could smell it now, in her, more clearly than she could smell the snow. It was not a memory of the room. It was the room. The room as it had been. And the room in front of her was not that room any more, and never would be again. Nobody would ever light the east stove at the second bell. Nobody would ever sit at the third desk from the east end in the dark before it, because they liked to be first.

The only place in the world where that room still existed was in her.

She understood it standing in the doorway, and it went into her slowly, the way cold goes into the hands.

If she spent it, it would not be in the world anywhere. Not in the room, which was snow and rot. Not in any Ledger, because you cannot write down a smell. It would simply stop. The last copy of a room that had stood for eleven hundred years, in the head of the last scribe who had ever sat in it.

*What is written is not lost.*

She had built her whole life on that sentence. She had believed it the way Seraphina believed in the Pure Note. And she stood in the scriptorium of the Silent Veil with the snow on the desks and understood that it had always been a lie. Or not a lie. A hope, written over a door. What is written is not lost. What is not written is.

And some things cannot be written.

---

She went to the third desk from the east end on the north side and brushed the snow off the seat and sat down.

It was cold. The wood was wet. She sat at the desk anyway, with the Ledger on the sloped top in front of her, where she had always put her work, and her hands on either side of it. She looked out through the narrow window at the frozen pool below the House and the white willows round it. And she sat there, the way she had sat every winter morning before the second bell, because she liked to be first.

There was nobody else. There would never be anybody else. She was first, and last, and only.

After a while she opened the Ledger and began to write. Not the loops. Not the curve. Something she had never written in the Ledger in three years, because the Ledger was for names and this was not a name.

*The scriptorium of the Silent Veil. The House, in the deep Woods. The east end, first floor. On a winter morning, before the second bell, before the stove was lit, it smelled of:*

She stopped.

She looked at the words. She tried to write the next line. *Cold stone.* She could write that. *Ink.* She could write that. *The dust off the vellum.* *Somebody's breakfast, from the kitchens underneath.* She wrote them. She looked at them on the page.

They were not it.

They were words. They were a list. They were what you would write if you had read about the room in a book. They were not the thing in the corner of the purse. The thing in the corner of the purse was not cold stone and ink and vellum-dust and breakfast. It was *this room*. It was the exact particular weight of all of it at once, on a particular morning, in the dark, to a particular girl who liked to be first. And she could not get it onto the page. She could only get the list.

She put the pen down.

She sat at the desk in the cold, with the Ledger open in front of her and the list on the page. She did not cry. She had not cried since the doorway of the harvest-house in 416, and she did not cry now. She sat very still with her hands flat on either side of the Ledger, the way she had sat at this desk ten thousand times. She looked at the list until the light went.

---

She heard the Chorister at dusk.

She had not gone to the Canopy. She had sat at the desk in the scriptorium until the window went grey and then dark. And in the dark, under the House, under the floor, very faint, she heard it.

She knew what it was. She had heard it once before, in Harvestmonth, through the floor of the Tuning Room at the Malting, where the engineer had sat with the old man's voice coming out of his arm. The engineer had said it then, and she had written it down. *Beneath the floor the Chorister of Memory recites a list it can no longer finish.*

It was reciting now. Under the House. Under the Woods. The roots of the Weeping Canopy ran under everything here, a kilometre down. And the voice came up through the stone of the House and the wood of the desk and into her hands where they lay flat on either side of the Ledger. Seven hundred and forty-one. Low, thin, wavering. Not words, exactly. The shapes of words. Names.

A name. And a name. And a name.

She knew some of them. That was the first thing. She sat in the dark and listened, and some of the names were names she knew. Names from the Ledger. Names she had written down in the plazas of Whisper-Grove in the spring of 416, when the Memory Anchor was silenced and seventy-four thousand people forgot themselves overnight. The Chorister was reciting them. One after another. Slowly. In order.

*Halder Wick.*

She went cold.

*Halder Wick*, entry twelve thousand two hundred and seven, who had set a table for four in this plaza every evening for three years and been killed walking his loop through the middle of a fight. The Chorister said his name, and then the next, and the next. They were the names of the Unstrung. The Hollows of the Grove. Every one of them. The god was saying them over, the way you say a list you are afraid of forgetting, the way the cores said their own names in the dark of the hold. It had been saying them since 416, she understood. Every one of seventy-four thousand. Over and over. So that somewhere, somebody would still know them.

It stopped.

In the middle of a name. *Wenna Hol—*

And there was a silence under the House. A long one.

And then, very faint, wavering, it began again. From the beginning. *Halder Wick.* And the next. And the next. As if it had lost its place, and could not find it, and had gone back to the start, the way a child does with a poem.

She sat at the desk in the dark and listened to it go through the list again. *Halder Wick.* A hundred names. Two hundred. And stop. Further back than the last time. In the middle of a name she knew. *Mira—*

Silence.

And from the beginning again. *Halder Wick.*

---

It was forgetting. She understood it sitting in the dark. Not the Hollows: the Hollows had forgotten themselves in 416. The god.

The Chorister of Memory had held their names for them since the Silencing, every one of seventy-four thousand, saying them over in the dark so they would not be lost. And it could not hold them any more. It was losing them. One at a time. From the end of the list backward. Every time it went through, it got a little less far before it lost its place. And every name it could not reach was a name that was not being said anywhere in the world any more.

Except in her Ledger.

And the loops in the plazas, shortening. Nine words, four words, two, one, none. That was the same thing. The god's hold on the names was going, and as it went, the last thread that tied each Hollow to the last thing they had said was going with it. When the god could not reach a name, the loop that went with the name lost a word. And then another. And then the gesture. And then nothing.

*Eleven days.*

She sat in the dark and heard the Chorister of Memory reach the hundred and forty-third name on its list. *Corwin A—*

And stop.

And from the beginning again. *Halder Wick.*

---

She would not remember deciding to go down. She would remember the stair.

There was a stair under the scriptorium. Everybody in the Veil knew it was there, and nobody used it. It went down from a door at the back of the kitchens, through the rock of the rise the House stood on, into what the Synod's survey called the Mausoleum. The Veil had called it the stacks.

She had been down once, in her first year, with Hennet Moor, on a lamp-lit walk that every new scribe was given and nobody was given twice. Down through a labyrinth of passages cut in the rock, narrow, turning, built to a pattern that the Veil said was a chord written in stone. A silencing chord. Because below the labyrinth were the stacks. And in the stacks were the archived dead.

Ten thousand years of them. Every soul that had died in the reach of the Memory Anchor since the First Compact, caught as it decayed and held in the roots of the Weeping Canopy, the way the old faith said heaven held the dead. The Veil had not called it heaven. The Veil had called it the stacks, and catalogued it, and built a labyrinth over it to keep it quiet. Because the dead in the stacks were not at rest, Hennet had said, on the lamp-lit walk, very quietly, in the labyrinth. They were held. They were aware. And if the labyrinth were ever broken, the living would hear them.

*Since the Silencing*, said the scribe in her who never stopped, *the Echo has not caught. The dead decay in the open air. The battlefields hum. Because the Anchor is silenced, it cannot archive any more.*

*But it still holds what it already has.*

*It still holds ten thousand years of them.*

*And it is forgetting.*

She went down the stair with a candle from the kitchens and the Ledger under her arm.

---

The labyrinth was broken.

She knew it at the first turning. The passages were the same, narrow and turning, cut in the grey rock. The pattern was the same; she remembered Hennet's hand on her shoulder at each turn, steering. But the silence was gone. Not the silence: the *silencing*. The labyrinth had been built to make a chord in stone, Hennet said, a chord that cancelled sound, so that nothing below it could be heard above. And as Sable went down through the turnings with her candle, the chord was not there.

She could hear them.

Faint. From below. From very far below. A murmur. The sound the *Kestrel*'s hold made at night, the sound the racks in Sorting made, tens of thousands of voices each saying something very fast and very quietly. But more. Not tens of thousands. More than she had any number for. A sound like the sea heard from inside a shell, if the sea were made of voices.

Ten thousand years of the dead, aware, in the dark.

And under the murmur, under all of it, something else.

She stopped in the labyrinth with her candle and listened. She did not want to. She listened anyway, because she was a scribe and a scribe does not take another's word for a thing she can hear for herself.

Under the murmur of the dead, there was a place where the murmur stopped.

---

She would never be able to write it. She tried, afterward, in the Ledger, a dozen times. She could not.

She went down to the last turning of the labyrinth, where the passage ended at a doorway of black stone. Beyond the doorway were the stacks, the great dark hall cut in the rock where Hennet Moor had stood with her lamp in Sable's first year. The roots of the Weeping Canopy came down through the ceiling there, a kilometre of them, thick as towers, white and silver, and the dead were in the roots. You could not see them. You could hear them.

She stood in the doorway with her candle.

The roots were there. Great white silver shapes going up into the dark. And the murmur was there, in the roots, all round her, every voice of ten thousand years at once, saying their names very fast and very quietly so as not to forget.

And at the far end of the hall, where the roots went down into the floor, the murmur stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. As if there were a line across the floor of the stacks, and on one side of it the roots were full of voices, and on the other side the roots were white and silver and exactly the same, and silent. And the line was not still. It was moving. Toward her. Slowly. A root at a time. As she watched, the root nearest the line went quiet. A great white silver shape going up into the dark. One moment it was full of murmuring, a thousand voices saying their names. The next it was silent, and white, and the voices in it were not there.

They had not stopped. They had gone *down*.

She understood it the way she understood a column of figures. The fourth mouth. Under the Woods. Where the loops went when they shortened to nothing. Where the god's list went when it lost its place. Where the dead in the stacks went, one root at a time, out of the hold of the Memory Anchor and down into the dark beneath it, into the thing that kept what it took, and did not hurt, and went on.

She was watching the dead go into the Hush.

---

She did not scream. Nobody did. She would think about that afterward, writing it, and understand it.

The voices in the roots did not scream as they went. She listened. She stood in the doorway of the stacks with her candle and listened to a root go quiet, and then another. There was no scream in it. No cry. Not even a change in the murmur. The voices went on saying their names, very fast and very quietly, right up to the moment they were not there. And then they were not there.

And the voices in the next root, the one beside it, did not change either. They did not cry out. They did not try to get away. They went on saying their names.

And then they were not there.

She stood and watched for a long time. She did not know how long. She watched the line come across the floor of the stacks toward her, a root at a time, through ten thousand years of the dead. And the dead went down into the dark without a sound, one root after another, saying their names right up to the end, so as not to forget.

When the line was a hundred yards from the doorway, she turned round and went back up through the labyrinth with her candle.

She did not run. She walked. She had learned that from Orrin Hale.

---

The Chorister spoke to her on the stair.

Not words. It had never been words. She was half-way up the stair from the labyrinth to the kitchens, with the candle guttering and the murmur of the stacks below her growing faint. The voice that had been reciting the list under the House, under the Woods, came up through the stone of the stair into her hands on the rail, and her feet on the steps, and stopped her.

It had stopped reciting. It had lost its place again, she supposed, and this time it did not go back to the beginning.

It turned toward her instead.

She felt it turn. The way the engineer had described the Light on Lumina, and the tenor bell of Wendmere. The whole of the god's attention, slow and vast and very tired, turning in the dark toward the one living thing in the stacks that had come down and listened, and written it down. Toward the one thing in the deep Woods that was still keeping names.

And it asked.

She had heard the engineer say it. She had heard Solenne say it on Lumina, and Auld Bessa, Vane would tell her later, would say it two days afterward to the black water under Site Nine. She had written it in the Ledger as testimony, in quotation marks, the words of a witness. She had never heard it herself. She was not glassed. She had no Anchor in her. She was a scribe with one piece of herself left and a face going smooth.

It asked her anyway.

*Is it you?*

She stood on the stair with the candle in one hand and the rail in the other.

*Have you come?*

She understood what it was asking. She understood it the way she understood a requisition in her own hand. The god of Memory had held ten thousand years of the dead, and seventy-four thousand of the living, every name, saying them over in the dark. And it could not hold them any more. It was letting them go one root at a time into the Hush. And here, on its stair, was a woman who had spent her life keeping names. Who had a Ledger under her arm with seventy-four thousand of them in it, written in her own hand. Who had one piece of herself left, and was going smooth, and would soon have nothing of her own at all to carry.

Except the names.

*Can I put it down now?*

*Will you take it?*

---

She could have.

She would write that in the Ledger, afterward, in her small clear hand, because it was true and she was a scribe. *I could have.* She did not know how. She did not know what it would have meant. But she felt the shape of it on the stair as clearly as the engineer had once felt the Light ask. The god of Memory, holding out the list. And her, with nothing left of herself to keep. Holding out her hands.

She would have been the list. She knew that. Not Sable, not a scribe, not a woman with a face. The list. Seventy-four thousand names and ten thousand years of the dead, said over in the dark for ever, so that they would not go down. She would not have been anyone at all. She would have been the thing that remembered everyone.

And she wanted to.

That was what she would write. *I wanted to.* Not because she was not afraid. She was. Of all of them she had the least left to be afraid with, and she still was. Because it was the only thing she had ever been for. Since the doorway of the harvest-house. Since before. Since the third desk from the east end on the north side, in the dark, before the second bell.

*To keep them.*

She opened her mouth.

---

She did not answer it.

She would never be able to say why. Not to Lyra, not to Orrin, not to the engineer, though he asked her once, very calmly, in the way he asked everything now. Not even to the Ledger. She stood on the stair with her mouth open and the god's question in her hands. The thing that came into her head was not a reason. It was a picture.

A page. A blank one. At the end of a book.

The last page of a chronicle. Somebody had to write it. Somebody had to sit at a desk at the end of everything and write down what had happened, all of it, so that it was not lost. The fire in the galley, and the knot in the rope, and the girl at the end of the line. And then read it aloud to somebody. Because there had to be somebody left to read it to. Otherwise what was written was only written. It was not kept.

If she became the list, there would be nobody to write the last page.

"*Not yet*," said Sable.

Aloud. On the stair. To the god of Memory, in the dark under the House, with the candle guttering. She heard her own voice. It was quite steady. It was the voice for entries.

"*I'm sorry. Not yet. I have to go and write the end.*"

The god did not answer.

She felt it turn away. Slowly. The whole vast tired attention, going back down into the dark under the Woods. And after a while, under the House, under the stair, very faint, wavering, it began to recite again. From the beginning.

*Halder Wick.*

She climbed the rest of the stair in the dark. The candle had gone out.

---

The Choir came at dawn on Hearthday.

She heard it from the Veil-house. She had sat all night at her desk, with the Ledger closed, in the cold. At the first grey of the morning, through the narrow window, across the frozen pool and the white willows, from somewhere deep in the Woods to the east, she heard singing.

It was beautiful.

That was the first thing. She had read about the Choir-That-Eats in the Office's field reports. A collective, forty to two thousand voices, migratory, arriving as distant singing. It recruits; anyone who hears it for more than a minute begins to sing along and walks toward it. *Four settlements consumed. Getting better at harmony.* She had read it and filed it and never once thought of it as beautiful.

It was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard.

Hundreds of voices. Perhaps thousands. Coming through the frozen Woods in the grey, far off, singing. Not in unison. Not the Pure Note, not the Church's single held tone. In parts. In harmony. A great slow rising chord, soprano and alto and tenor and bass, and voices between and above and below those, every one of them a little off from the one beside it, so that the chord shimmered. It moved and breathed like a living thing. It was singing Hymn 9.

She sat at the desk and listened, and felt her own mouth begin to open.

---

She had sung Hymn 9 once. In the Cloister of the Lost Choir, in Harvestmonth, because the Cantor had no voice and pointed at the hymn board. A nine-foot iron thing was standing up out of its alcove with *DEFEND THE SINGERS* on its chest, and somebody had to sing, and Sable was the only one in the room who had stood in the doorway while eleven children sang it, counting them. She had sung it badly. She had sung it in a voice she had not used for singing since she was a child. And the Aegis had knelt.

She felt it come up in her now. The first line. The soprano's.

*No.*

She put her hand over her mouth.

The Choir came on through the Woods. Nearer. She could hear the parts now, separately. She could hear individual voices in it. A man's tenor, rough, a little flat. A woman's alto, very sweet. And a voice under all of them, so low it was hardly a voice, a bass like a held breath. And over them, very high, a single clear voice, a girl's, singing the soprano line as if she had sung it every day of her life.

She felt the second line come up in her. She felt her feet want to stand.

She opened the Ledger.

---

She read it aloud. That was all she had. She had read the names of the forty-seven on the Shelf aloud at the fire, in the voice for entries, so that the Shelf would hear them said once more before they were only ink. She opened the Ledger at the first page now, the Plaza of the Willows, the spring of 416. In the voice for entries, flat and clear and steady, as loud as she could, over the singing, she began to read.

"*Entry one. The Plaza of the Willows. A woman in a doorway on the north side. Apparent forty. Grey dress. Loop: '— and I'll fetch the other one.' Nine words.*"

The singing came on through the Woods. Beautiful. Rising.

"*Entry two. A man by the fountain. Apparent sixty. Loop: 'Mind the step, Wenna, it's wet.' Six words.*"

She felt the third line of Hymn 9 come up in her throat. She read over it.

"*Entry three. A woman with a basket—*"

She read. She read for an hour. She sat at her desk in the roofless scriptorium of the Silent Veil at dawn, in the snow, and read the Ledger aloud in the voice for entries, name after name, loop after loop. The most beautiful thing she had ever heard came through the frozen Woods toward her, singing a children's hymn in a thousand voices, wanting her to stand up and walk to it and sing.

She did not stand up.

She read.

---

It did not come to the Veil-house. She would never know why. Perhaps because the House was roofless and on a rise and the Choir kept to the low ground. Perhaps because a woman reading names aloud in a flat voice was not a thing it knew how to want. Perhaps it was only luck.

It went past. A quarter of a mile away, through the white willows below the rise, eastward. Toward the plazas.

She stood at the narrow window of the scriptorium with the Ledger in her hands, still reading, and watched it go.

She could not see it. It was not a thing you could see. She saw the frozen mist move, in the Woods below, as something went through it. A long slow ripple through the white, like wind through standing grain, going east. And she heard the singing go with it, beautiful, rising, the chord shimmering. And she understood where it was going.

The Plaza of the Willows. And the Plaza of the Fountains beyond it. And every plaza in Whisper-Grove where the Hollows stood in the ice, with their loops shortened to one word or none.

People who had nothing left to say.

And a thing coming through the Woods that would give them something to sing.

---

She went down to the Plaza of the Willows at noon. She did not want to. She went.

It was empty.

The ice was there, white and thick. The fountain. The steps. The doorway on the north side. But there was nobody in the ice. Two hundred and four people had been frozen into it to the ankles and shins yesterday. Standing, lying, sitting, moving their hands. Some still saying one word. They were gone.

There were footprints. In the snow on the ice. Hundreds of them. Going east, out of the plaza, toward the far side, toward the Woods. Some were bare. Some were in shoes worn through by three years of the same nine steps. All of them were going the same way, and none of them were coming back.

And on the north side of the plaza, the doorway. Empty. A grey stone frame, with the door standing open into a dark house, and nobody in it. Nobody with one hand on the frame, half-turned, as if to call back into the house.

She stood in the doorway.

She looked down at the threshold, where the woman had stood for three years. There was a mark on the stone. A pale worn place, where one pair of feet had stood in one spot every day for three years, saying the same nine words, and then eight, and then four, and then two.

*— other one.*

She would never know what the first three words had been. Hennet Moor had said them aloud as she wrote, in the spring of 416, and Sable had written them down wrong and crossed them out. *Never mind, girl, we'll have it right tomorrow, she'll still be saying it tomorrow.* Hennet was dead, in the harvest of 416, pressed, at the Malting, at seventy-one, for being a Veil scribe who would not sign. And the woman in the doorway had walked east out of the Plaza of the Willows that morning with two hundred others, singing Hymn 9 in harmony, toward something that would keep her.

She was not in the doorway any more. She was singing. Somewhere in the Woods, in the Choir, in a thousand voices, a little off from the one beside her.

Perhaps she was happy.

Sable stood in the empty doorway and could not tell whether that was the worst thing she had ever thought, or the kindest. She could not find the difference. She was not sure there was one.

---

She wrote it all down that night, at her desk, by the light of a fire she had made in the east stove out of the beams that had come down through the roof. It was the first fire in that stove in three years. It did not smell of anything but burning wood.

*19 Frostmonth. The Plaza of the Willows. 204 loops. 11 speaking, 171 gesture, 22 null.*

*The Chorister of Memory recites the names of the Unstrung, all seventy-four thousand, and loses its place. Each time, it reaches fewer. I heard it stop at the 143rd. Each name it cannot reach, its Hollow loses a word.*

*Under the House, the labyrinth is broken. The dead in the stacks are going down into the Hush. One root at a time. They do not cry out. They say their names until they are not there.*

*The Chorister asked me if it was me. I said not yet.*

*20 Frostmonth. The Choir-That-Eats passed the House at dawn going east. It sang Hymn 9 in harmony. It took the Plaza of the Willows. All 204. They walked. Their footprints go east.*

*Curve recalculated. At this rate the last loop in Whisper-Grove falls silent on the 30th of Frostmonth. The Longest Night. Whatever the Supreme Arbiter means to do under the Organum that night, the god of Memory will have nothing left to hold by then. It will have let everything go. It will be very tired.*

*It will be ready to say yes.*

She looked at that line for a long time. Then she wrote under it, in the same small hand:

*I think this is what he meant. I think he has been waiting for them all to get this tired. I think he has been listening to them get tired for eighty years, and counting, the way the golem counted. And I think the Longest Night is not the night he has chosen. I think it is the night they have.*

She put down the pen.

She sat in front of the first fire in the east stove in three years, at the third desk from the east end, in the roofless scriptorium of the Silent Veil, in the snow. In the corner of her, worn thin, the last coin. Cold stone and ink and vellum-dust and breakfast. A room that was not in the world any more, except in her.

She held it.

She did not know for how much longer she would be able to. She held it anyway, the way you hold a candle in a draught. She sat very still, and kept it, and did not spend it. Outside the narrow window, deep in the frozen Woods to the east, far off, so faint she could not be sure she heard it, a thousand voices sang a children's hymn in harmony, beautifully, to nobody.

## CHAPTER FOUR
### The Root

> *Does it ring?*
> *— True enough.*
>
> — The greeting of the Reach. Children learn the question before they learn their own names, and the answer before they learn what it is for

*Oakhaven. Rootday the nineteenth of Frostmonth, to Massday the twenty-first.*

---

There was snow on the Greenroot road. There had never been snow on the Greenroot road in her life.

Lyra had been born in the Reach and had lived in it for thirty-four years. She had learned the seasons as every orchard child learned them: as a list of things that grew. Frostmonth was not cold, in the Reach. Frostmonth was the month the iron-bark oaks dropped their mast and the second reed-crop came up green in the water-meadows. You hummed the tending note into the hollow of the oldest tree in the orchard on the Longest Night, and the tree hummed it back, and you went home to your supper in your shirt-sleeves. That was Frostmonth. The cold stopped at the edge of the Reach, the way the sea stops at the shore. It always had. The Root kept it out.

The *Kestrel* had set them down at Greenroot Hamlet on Memoryday, on its way to the Woods and the Coast. They had walked for two days. The snow had begun on the first night. It was ankle-deep now, grey and wet, and it lay on the road and on the iron-bark oaks on either side. And the oaks did not ring.

She had not noticed at first. She had noticed the cold, and the snow, and the silence. She had thought it was the silence of snow. Then at the second milestone a branch came down in the wind, an oak-limb as thick as her thigh, and fell across the road ten yards ahead of the Aegis. It struck the frozen ground with a dull wet crack.

No bell.

In the Reach, since 417, when the law went soft, any blow on wood had rung. That was the first thing outsiders learned. *No quiet fighting in a forest.* An oak-limb falling in the Reach should have tolled like a struck bronze. It should have brought every hound for a mile. It lay across the road in the snow and was only wood. Grey, and dead, and silent.

Seraphina looked at it. Then at Lyra.

"Is that—"

Lyra nodded.

"Is that good?"

Lyra looked at the limb in the snow for a long time. She did not know how to answer. She had no slate free; her good hand was holding the coat shut at her throat and her burned hand was in her armpit for the warmth. So she did not.

---

There were graves along the road.

She had known there would be. Sable had told them about the relief station at the Malting, where the Office had registered the Reach refugees walked out of the Greening, and processed them, in the first room. So she had known people had walked this road in Harvestmonth. She had not known how many had stopped on it.

They lay along the verges under the oaks, in the snow. Low mounds of turned earth, a stride long, some shorter. Each had a stone at its head, and each stone had been set the Reach way: flat side up, with a smaller stone on top of it.

The stone on the tongue.

In the Reach you buried the dead with their jaws bound shut and a flat river-stone laid on the tongue, so that they could not ring. That had been the custom since the law went soft. A body in the Reach, after 417, did not lie still in the ground. It grew. And if it grew with its mouth open, it hummed. People had dug up their own grandmothers in the first year because of the humming. So you bound the jaw and laid the stone, and when you filled the grave you set a second stone on the marker to show that it had been done. *Stone on the tongue, stone on the stone.* So that the next person to come along the road would know that this one was quiet, and would not dig.

There were a great many of them. She stopped counting at the fourth milestone, at sixty. They went on.

Some of them did not have the second stone.

She saw the first at the fifth milestone. A small grave, a child's. A stone at the head, and nothing on it. Whoever had buried this one had not had time to do it properly. Or had not had a stone. Or had not known how. Or had been the last of their family on the road and had buried the child alone and walked on.

She stopped by it.

The Aegis stopped behind her, the way it always did, with a long grinding in its right knee and a hiss of steam from the thinned plates of its back. Inside the helm, faint and rosy, like a hand over a candle, the light of the core. Wren Halloway. Ten years old. Alto.

Lyra knelt in the snow beside the grave and listened.

Nothing. She put her good hand flat on the turned earth. It was frozen hard. Nothing came up through it. No hum. No growth. She had braced for it. She knew what it was to put your hand on a grave in the Reach and feel the person in it still growing. And there was nothing at all. Cold earth, and a small shape under it that had stopped.

She did not know whether to be glad.

She found a stone under the snow at the side of the road, flat, the size of her palm. She set it on the marker. *Stone on the stone.* So that the next person to come along the road would know.

Then she got up and went on. Behind her, she heard the Aegis's knee grind as it bent, and the click of iron on stone. When she looked back, it had found a stone too. It had set it on the next bare grave along.

It did that for the rest of the road. Every bare grave. It did not hurry. It found a stone, and bent, and set it, and came on. Seraphina began to do it too, after a while, and then Ignatius Crane with his one good hand. And at the last, without looking at any of them, the tall woman with the broken black-gold wings. Lyra did not stop them. It took the rest of the afternoon.

---

The Hedge was grey.

She saw it from the last rise before the town, in the dusk. She had lived inside it since she was thirty-two. She had watched it planted, in the autumn of 417, the same autumn the Synod sank the quarry bore and took her father. A wall of blackthorn and briar three men deep round the whole of Oakhaven, with fleece packed into the gaps between the roots, raw wool from every sheep in the Reach, to swallow the town's sounds. So that nothing inside would carry. So that the Hush would not hear a child cry at night, or a woman laugh, or a man call out. The Synod's engineers had planted it, and the law had made it grow, and in a year it was twenty feet high and so thick a hound could not get through. Oakhaven had lived inside it for two years like a voice inside a closed hand.

It had been green. Always. Even in the Greening it had been green. It was grey now. The thorn was bare and grey and the wool between the roots was grey and sodden and the whole long wall of it curved away round the hill in the dusk, under the snow, like the spine of something dead.

The North Gate was sealed. It had been sealed since the Greening. A great iron grille set in an arch of stone, rusted shut, with the thorn grown through it. The Greening had been only two months ago. It looked as if it had been shut a hundred years.

They would have to go round, to the river gate. She knew the way.

She did not go round. She stood in the road in front of the North Gate in the dusk with the snow falling on her, and looked at it.

This was where Ada Pellow had gone in. On the day of the Greening, with the town going under the oaks behind them and sixty people in the square whom Lyra had walked there with a corridor of song. The Hedge had spoken. Out of the thorn. In the voices of Oakhaven's dead, every one it had ever swallowed. *Come through. Come home. There's a way through, love, come through.* And Ada Pellow, who was seventy, and a widow, and had buried her husband with a stone on his tongue in 418, had heard a voice in the thorn and answered it.

It had been Tobin Ashgrove's voice. Lyra's father's.

She had watched Ada Pellow climb into the thorn toward her father's voice. She had not been able to stop her. She had had sixty people behind her and the town going under. She had kept walking.

She stood in front of the North Gate and did not hear anything. The Hedge was grey and silent in the snow. The wind went through the bare thorn and did not even whistle.

*Good*, she thought.

She turned to go round to the river gate.

"*Lyra-love.*"

---

She did not turn round.

She stood in the road with her back to the Hedge and her hand at her throat and did not turn round, and did not breathe.

"*Lyra-love. Is that you?*"

Low. Warm. A little hoarse, the way it always was at the end of the day, from singing in the orchards. A man's voice. Her father's, as it had been before they took him. She had heard it since, on forty-one nights, at this Hedge, at the dead hour, coming out of a thing that wore it, and she had gone back every night knowing what it was. And once at this gate, in the Greening, when it called to Ada Pellow. The thing that wore it had burst in front of her in the autumn, when she sang her father's funeral office at it, and let everything it had ever eaten out at once, ending with him.

The Wearer was dead. She had watched it die.

The Hedge was not the Wearer. The Hedge had been planted to swallow sound. Everything Oakhaven had said for two years had gone into the thorn and been kept there. Every word, every cry, every song. And her father had lived inside it for the last weeks before they took him, in the autumn it was planted, saying her name every evening at the supper table.

"*Lyra-love. Come round where I can see you.*"

She did not turn round.

She heard Seraphina come up beside her in the snow, and stop. She heard the Aegis's knee grind. She felt Solenne behind her. Solenne had heard it too. Solenne heard everything. The copper in her throat caught sounds out of the air the way a bell catches the wind.

"Lyra," said Seraphina, very low. "Who is that?"

Lyra took her good hand from her throat and lifted the slate on its cord, and found the chalk in her pocket. Her fingers were stiff with cold. It took her a long time to write it.

*NOBODY.*

She held it up. Then she walked away from the North Gate along the outside of the Hedge, toward the river gate, in the dusk. She did not look back. Behind her, very faint, through the grey thorn, the voice said her name once more, the way it had always said it, with the little fall at the end. *Lyra-love.*

Like a question.

---

Oakhaven was a wood.

She had known that too. She had seen it go under from the square, with the oaks doubling and the churchyard heaving and the Chapel coming down. She had seen it from the deck of the *Kestrel* at dawn, four miles back, green, a forest where a town had been, ringing like a carillon in the wind. She had sung the calling-song after the ship with the last of her voice.

She came through the river gate in the dark, and stood, and did not know where she was.

The oaks had come up through the town. That was the Greening. The Root had drunk forty thousand gallons of Aldric Vance's purge-water into an open wound and screamed and cracked and begun to grow. Every iron-bark oak in the Reach had doubled in an hour. Then doubled again. Then gone on. They had come up through the cobbles and the cellars and the floors of houses, and lifted the houses with them. They had carried them up into their crowns the way a flood carries a cart. And they had stopped there, two months ago, when the *Kestrel* had gone, and the Root had drunk all it could, and the growing had slowed.

She stood in the river gate with a lantern and looked up.

Houses. In the trees. Forty feet up, sixty, eighty. Wedged in the forks of great grey trunks as thick as towers. Tilted, broken-backed, with their doors hanging open on nothing. She saw a cottage turned on its side in the crown of an oak above the river street, with its chimney pointing at the river. She saw a shop-front, the painted board still over the door, *HALE & DAUGHTER, CORDWAINERS*, sixty feet up in the snow. She saw a cart. She saw a bed, iron-framed, caught in the fork of a branch, with its blankets frozen to it.

The leaves were gone.

Every one. She had expected them to be green. The Greening was green, Oakhaven was green, it had been green when she last saw it. Every tree in the town had been in full leaf when the *Kestrel* went, two months ago, in the middle of Harvestmonth, ringing.

There were no leaves on any tree in Oakhaven. They lay on the ground. All of them. A foot deep in the streets, under the snow, so that you waded through them. Brown and grey and black, and frozen, and they did not rustle when you walked through them. They lay where they had fallen, all together. As if every tree in the town had let go of every leaf at once.

"When?" said Seraphina. Her breath smoked. "When did they fall? They'd fall slowly. In autumn. It's not—"

Lyra did not know. She stood in the river gate with the lantern and the dead leaves to her knees, and looked up at the bare grey trees with the houses in them, and did not know.

Somebody else did.

---

"Nullday," said a voice from the dark. "Nullday night. Last night but one, at the dead hour. All at once."

A man. Old. He came out of the trees on the far side of the river street with a lantern of his own, wading through the leaves, and stopped, and held the lantern up, and looked at them. At the Aegis, nine feet of iron with *DEFEND THE SINGERS* on its chest and the rosy light in its helm. At the knight in white with the Sun-Blade. At the tall woman with the wings. At the priest with the translucent hand, in a woman's apron. And at Lyra.

His face did a thing.

"Cantor," he said.

She knew him. It took her a moment. He was thinner than he had been, and his beard had gone white. He was Dorran Sedge, who had kept the river gate for twenty years and the reed-beds at Reedside before that. He had a wife called Mabbie and a voice like a bullfrog and he had sung bass in the orchard choir every Longest Night that Lyra could remember, before 417.

She lifted her good hand.

"Cantor," said Dorran Sedge again. He did not move. He stood in the leaves with the lantern held up and looked at her as if he were afraid that if he moved she would not be there. "Cantor Ashgrove. They said— The ones that walked out. Harvestmonth. They said you'd gone up in the airship and the airship'd gone down in the Woods. They said the Church had you. They said—"

He stopped.

He had seen the slate round her neck. And the way she was holding her throat.

He came across the river street to her through the leaves. He was a big man and he had always been slow. He came across it slowly, and stood in front of her, and looked at her throat for a long moment. And then, very carefully, the way you lift something that might break, he put his free hand on her shoulder.

"*Does it ring?*" said Dorran Sedge.

The oldest thing in the Reach. The thing they taught children before their own names. *Are you well.* She had heard it a thousand times. She had said it a thousand times, to the burl-sick in the Hollow, to women in labour, to old men in the orchard, to children who had fallen out of trees.

She could not say the answer.

She stood in the river gate with Dorran Sedge's hand on her shoulder and opened her mouth. Nothing came out. Only breath, and a long raw pull low in her throat where the torn place was. She shut her mouth. She stood there.

Then she took up the slate and wrote on it, very slowly, with her stiff fingers, in the square capitals she had been writing for two months.

*NO.*

Dorran Sedge looked at it for a long time.

"No," he said. "No. Nor me." He took his hand off her shoulder. "Nobody says it now, Cantor. Not since Nullday. Nobody's said *true enough* since the leaves came down." He turned, and held his lantern up toward the dark at the top of the river street, toward the square. "You'd best come and see the Governor. Before you see anything else. He's been asking for you. He's been asking every night." He stopped. "He doesn't ask so loud now. You'll have to go close."

---

It was called the Grove now. Dorran told her that on the way up the river street, wading. The ones at Reedside had called it that since Ashfall, when the Governor had done it, and the name had stuck. *The Grove.* As if there were no other.

The square of Oakhaven was gone.

She remembered it the way she remembered her own hands. Cobbled. Sloping a little to the south. The Chapel on the north side, where the Church had come in Harvestmonth and put up its glass-fire and its Pure Note. The Moot-hall on the east side, where the Moot had voted every lease the Synod ever asked for. The Governor's House on the west, with the seal of the dead Queen Maelis over its door. And in the middle, where the old well had been, the black iron head of the Synod's quarry bore. The Root-shaft. Four hundred feet of it, straight down, to the vault where the god was.

She stood at the top of the river street with Dorran's lantern and looked at where the square had been.

There was a ring of trees.

Not oaks. Not iron-bark. Something else. She did not know them. Slender, the height of a tall man and then twice that and more, with smooth pale bark, grey-white, like the bark of the silver birches in the Woods. They stood in a ring round the iron head of the Root-shaft, close, so close that their trunks touched, and their branches had grown together overhead into a single grey lattice against the snow. Two hundred of them. More. The ring was forty yards across, and two deep, and in places three, and it was closed. There was no gap in it. You could not have got a hound through it. You could not have got a Grafter's saw to the shaft-head without going through every trunk in the ring.

Each one had a face.

She saw it as Dorran raised the lantern. Not carved. Not a knot in the wood that looked like a face, the way children see faces in bark. A face. In the grey-white bark, at the height of a man's head, a little below where the trunk forked: a brow, and eyes closed under it, and a nose, and a mouth. Shut. Every one. Smooth and pale and still, like the faces of the dead in the Reach when the jaw is bound.

And under each face, lower down, where the trunk swelled, the shape of hands. Reaching out to either side. Grown into the hands of the trunks beside them.

They had rooted holding hands.

---

"Ashfall," said Dorran Sedge. "The second week. When the Grafters didn't come."

He stood beside her with the lantern. He did not look at the ring. He looked at the snow at his feet.

"There was three hundred and eighty of us, after the airship went. Standing in the mud in the square. You'll know. You were on it." He did not wait for her to nod. "Ninety-five walked. The first week. Down the Greenroot road, to the relief station, the Synod said, there'd be bread and a bed and a doctor. They went in a long line. Mabbie's sister went. We never heard."

Lyra had heard. She had heard from Sable, on the *Kestrel*, what the first room at the Malting was. She did not say so. She had no slate free. She did not want one.

"Thirty-one died," said Dorran. "Hounds, the first fortnight, before we got the river gate shut. And the cold. It was getting cold even then. And the growing. People had been rooting in the Greening, you'll know, the carters in the street, the baker's wife. It didn't stop when the airship went. It slowed down, but it didn't stop. Every morning somebody else couldn't get their feet up off the floor." He stopped. "And the Synod never sent the Grafters. We waited. Every day we thought they'd come up the road with the saws. That's the worst of it, Cantor. We *wanted* them. We'd have paid them. A man roots in the Reach, you send for the Grafters, and the Cantor sings him the felling-song, and they take him out of the floor before he's past it. That's how it's done. That's how it's always been done. And they didn't come. They'd done with us. The Church had come and gone and the Root had gone wrong and the Synod had what it wanted, and nobody was coming up that road with a saw ever again."

He looked up at the ring.

"So the Governor said, we'll not be took one at a time. That's what he said. Standing on the shaft-head in the square, with his feet already going. *If we're to be a wood, we'll be one wood.* And he said, any that wanted, come and stand with him. In a ring. Round the shaft. Holding hands. So that if anybody ever did come up the road, Synod or Church or anybody, to get at the Root again, they'd have to cut through all of us to do it." He stopped. "Two hundred and twelve went and stood with him. The rest of us couldn't. Or wouldn't. Forty-one. We went down to Reedside, to the Conservatory, to the Cantor-Master. We go up and see them, mornings."

He looked at the ring for a long time.

"It took nine days," he said. "For them to be like that. They sang while it happened. All nine days. Anything they knew. It was the best singing I ever heard in this town. And on the ninth day they stopped."

---

Aldric Vance was at the shaft-head.

Not in the ring. Inside it. Dorran took her through, and she did not know how. There was no gap. He went up to the ring at the south side, to two trunks that stood a little apart from each other, so that their hands, grown together between them, made a sort of low arch. He bent under it, and she bent after him, with her burned hand held against her chest. She felt the grey bark brush her shoulder as she went under, and it was not cold. It was not warm either. It was the temperature of skin.

The others stayed outside. She had made them. On the slate. Seraphina had wanted to come and Lyra had shaken her head, and Seraphina had looked at the ring of faces and understood.

Inside the ring, the snow was not so deep. The lattice of branches overhead had caught most of it. The iron head of the Root-shaft stood in the middle in a circle of grey trampled snow. It was a squat black iron collar the height of her waist, with the hatch on top rusted shut and the Synod's bore-mark stamped on its flank. And grown up against it, on its north side, with its back to the iron, was a tree.

It was not like the others. It was not slender or pale. It was thick, and its bark was rough and dark and deeply furrowed, like the bark of an old oak. It had not grown straight up. It had grown sideways. Its roots went into the ground on either side of the shaft-head, and down, and she could see where they went into the cracks in the iron collar and through them. They gripped it. They held it the way a man holds a door shut with his back.

It was not finished.

The face was in the bark at the height of a sitting man's head. It was not smooth like the others. It was lined, and grey-bearded with lichen, and its eyes were open.

Not wood. Eyes. Grey, and wet, and old, in a face she had known all her life.

They looked at her.

The mouth moved.

---

"*Ly—ra.*"

She knelt down in the snow in front of him.

She did not decide to. Her knees went, the way Kaelen's had gone at the edge of the world. She was kneeling in the trampled snow at the foot of the iron shaft-head in front of Aldric Vance, Governor of Oakhaven, who had sold the Root.

He had sold it in the Cascade famine, when the reeds turned to glass and blew away and the Reach had nothing to eat. He had kept the town fed by bleeding it, slowly, ever since. He had stood on the speaking-tube in Harvestmonth and counted down the purge that had made the Greening. He had stood at the bottom of the *Kestrel*'s rope on the last load, because he had been loading his town onto it for twenty minutes, and looked up, and waved them on. And Kaelen had said *do it*, and the *Kestrel* had leapt a hundred and fifty feet, and three hundred and eighty people had stood in the mud and waved them away.

She had hated him for most of her life. She had grown up in Reedside two streets from his house, and she had watched him sell the Root, and she had watched the Grafters come up the road. She had watched her father taken. She had sat in the Hollow behind the tannery with four hundred of the burl-sick and sung them the felling-song before the saws. And she had hated him for every one.

He looked at her out of the bark with his wet grey eyes.

"*Ly—ra.*" The mouth moved slowly. The voice was very faint. It came out of the wood, not the mouth, a dry creak like a branch in wind. "*You... came. They said... the airship...*"

She nodded.

"*Is he...*"

She knew what he was going to ask. She had known for two days, on the road. She had known since the slate in Kaelen's hand he had handed back on the Shelf that said *GOOD*. She had not known what she would write.

"*Is he... alive?*"

She nodded.

The grey eyes closed. For a long moment she thought he had gone. Then they opened again. Wet. Wetter.

"*Is he... well?*"

---

She had the slate. She had the chalk. She knelt in the snow in front of him with the slate on her knee and held the chalk over it for a long time.

She thought about lying.

She had lied once, to the Inquisitor, flattened onto a hundred and seventy-four, in the white room at Oakhaven. *I am fortunate.* She had told it with the wire in a fold of her skin. It had been the only lie she had ever told that saved anybody. She thought about telling Aldric Vance, rooted into the iron of the shaft-head, with his eyes open in the bark, that his son was well.

She wrote. Slowly. In the square capitals.

*HE IS ALIVE. HE IS IN FORTIS. THE GLASS IS AT HIS THROAT.*

She held it up. He read it. She saw his eyes go along the lines. Then she rubbed it out with her sleeve and wrote again.

*A CREATURE ATE HIS FEAR. HE ASKED IT TO. HE IS NOT AFRAID OF ANYTHING NOW.*

She held it up.

Aldric Vance read it.

And she watched his face, in the bark, try to do something. She did not know what. The lines round the eyes moved. The mouth moved. She heard the wood creak, deep in the trunk, as if something in there were straining against it. And the grey eyes looked at the slate, and at her, and at the slate, and filled.

"*He was... always... afraid,*" said Aldric Vance. "*Always. From a... boy. Of the dark. Of the bore. Of me.*" The creak again, deep in the wood. "*Of being... wrong. He went to the... Conservatory at seventeen... afraid. He came back... afraid. He calibrated the... Spire... and it fell... and he came home and sat in my... kitchen... and shook.*"

The eyes closed. Opened.

"*It was the... best thing in him. Lyra. It was... the only thing I... gave him... that was any good.*"

---

She stayed with him until the lantern went out.

He could not talk for long. The words came slowly out of the wood, a few at a time, with long silences between, and every word cost him. She could see it. She could hear the creak in the trunk getting deeper as the night went on. But he wanted to talk. He had been asking for her every night, Dorran said, and he had things he wanted to say, and he said them.

He told her about the Grove. The nine days. How it had felt. *Like going to sleep standing up. Like the floor coming up to... meet you. It didn't hurt. Nobody... screamed.* How they had sung, all of them, holding hands. How on the ninth day they had stopped, one after another, round the ring, the way lamps go out along a street. How he had not stopped. How he had felt the others go quiet in the wood beside him, their hands in his, and had not been able to follow.

*I'm on the iron,* he said. *The Root's... under me. I can feel it. I can't... go down into the wood like the others. The Root won't... let me. It's... holding on to me.*

He told her about Nullday night.

*The leaves came down at the dead hour. All at once. Every tree in the... town. I heard them. A sound like... rain. Like the whole world... sighing. And then the Root...*

He stopped for a long time.

*The Root... asked me something. Lyra.*

She went very still.

*Under the iron. Four hundred feet down. Through my... roots. I felt it ask. Not words. I don't know what it... asked. I couldn't... hear it right. I'm not a Cantor. I never had the... ear. But it was... asking. Something. Over and over. All that night. And at dawn it... went quiet. But it's still... asking. Under me. Very faint. And nothing's... grown since. Not a leaf. Not a root. The Reach is... stopping. Lyra. It's going... cold. The Root's... letting go of it.*

He looked at her.

*And the Grove. They're... in the wood. Lyra. All of them. Two hundred and... twelve. They're not dead. They're... in there. Awake. I can feel them. Through the hands. And the wood is... dying. The Root's let go of it and it's... going grey. And when it's... dead wood... they'll still be... in it.*

His eyes held hers.

*They'll still be... in it. Lyra. For... ever. In dead... wood. Awake.*

---

She did not sleep that night. She went down to Reedside with Dorran, to the Conservatory, in the dark. She lay on a pallet in the reed-hall among forty-one people, a dozen of them children, asleep round a fire of dry wind-reed stalks, and did not sleep.

She lay on her back and looked up at the high dark beams of the reed-hall where she had learned the tending note at eight years old. She thought about what Aldric Vance had not asked her.

He had not asked her for anything. He had told her. And then he had stopped talking, and looked at her out of the bark, and waited. She had known what he was waiting for, and she had got up and gone away without answering, because she could not.

The felling-song.

She had sung it four hundred times. In the Hollow behind the tannery, before the Grafters' saws. You sat with the burl-sick when they were past it, when the roots had gone too deep into the floor to be cut out without killing them. You put your hand on their chest and sang. Not the tending note, a hundred and seventy-four, that told a growing thing to grow. The other one. The one every orchard Cantor learned last, at sixteen, and hoped never to use. It was what you sang to an old tree in the orchard before the axe came. A falling fifth, held, and let go, and then a fall below the fall, down into a note under the tending note that you felt in the floor more than you heard. It told the sap to go down. It told the tree to sleep. When you sang it to the burl-sick, the roots let go of the floor, and the person in them let go with them, gently, and slept. And then the Grafters came in with the saws, and there was nobody left to feel it.

It was a mercy. Every Cantor in the Reach knew it was a mercy. It was the hardest mercy there was.

She could not sing it.

She lay on the pallet in the reed-hall with her burned hand on her chest and her good hand at her throat, where the torn place was, and understood it. There were two hundred and twelve people in the wood in the square. Awake. The wood was dying round them. In a week, a month, a year, it would be dead, and grey, and they would be in it, still awake, still holding hands, for as long as dead wood lasted in the cold. And the only thing in the world that could let them go was a song she could not sing.

She had thought, at the Shelf, that losing her voice was the worst thing that would ever happen to her.

---

The Cantor-Master found her at first light.

Lyra heard her before she saw her: the tap of a stick on the reed-hall floor, slow, and the rustle of a heavy skirt. She sat up on the pallet. The fire had burned down to embers and the forty-one were still asleep round it, in blankets, with the children in the middle where it was warmest. In the grey light from the high windows an old woman came down the reed-hall between the sleepers with a stick in one hand and a slate in the other.

Ilse Brennan was seventy-eight. She had been Cantor-Master of the Reedside Conservatory for forty years, and she had taught Lyra the tending note at eight, the falling fifth at twelve, and the felling-song at sixteen. She had lost her voice in 408. It was the winter the throat-fever went through Reedside, and she had sung nine nights running in the Hollow to the dying, and on the tenth morning she had opened her mouth and nothing came. She had taught for eleven years after that without a voice. With a slate, and her hands, and her breath.

*A Cantor's instrument is the breath. The voice is only the most common way of spending it.*

She had written that on the slate in the reed-hall the first morning she came back to teach, in 408. Lyra had been twenty-three, and a Cantor already, and had stood at the back of the reed-hall with the other junior Cantors and read it. And had not understood it at all.

Ilse Brennan came down the reed-hall between the sleepers. She was small, and bent, and white-haired, and her face had gone thin with the winter. She stopped in front of Lyra's pallet and looked down at her. At her face. At her throat. At the slate round her neck.

Then she lowered herself down, very slowly, onto the end of the pallet, with her stick across her knees. She held up her own slate. It was already written on. She had written it before she came in, Lyra understood. She had known Lyra was here since Dorran brought her down in the night. She had sat up writing it.

*I HEARD YOU WERE COMING HOME WITHOUT IT.*

Lyra looked at it.

Then she lifted her own slate. They sat facing each other on the pallet in the grey light of the reed-hall. Two Cantors of the Reach, the old one and the young one, with two slates and no voice between them.

*YES*, wrote Lyra.

Ilse Brennan looked at it for a long time. Then she wiped her slate and wrote, in the small upright hand that had marked Lyra's exercises for eight years.

*GOOD. NOW YOU CAN LEARN WHAT I MEANT.*

---

She had a letter.

She gave it to Lyra on the pallet. It was folded small and sealed with a blob of resin, and on the outside, in the small upright hand, it said *LYRA*. And under that: *NOT TO BE OPENED UNTIL SHE HAS COME HOME, OR UNTIL I AM DEAD.*

Lyra looked at it.

*I wrote it in Harvestmonth*, Ilse's slate said. *The week the Church came. When I knew they would take you down the Root. I thought you would not come back. I wanted it to be somewhere. In case.*

*In case what*, wrote Lyra.

*In case somebody found it who could do something with it. Open it.*

She opened it.

It was three pages. Close-written. The small upright hand. She read it on the pallet in the grey light, with Ilse Brennan sitting at the end of the pallet watching her, and the forty-one asleep round the embers.

---

*Lyra.*

*You were the best student I ever had and you never once listened to me. I am writing this because I think they are going to take you down the Root and I do not think I will see you again. There is a thing you do not know. I should have told you at sixteen. I did not, because the vault had been sealed for three hundred years and it seemed not to matter; and then in 417 they sank the bore, and I am old, and I did not want to be the last one who knew it. But I am, and it does matter. So.*

*You think the orchard craft is for the trees. Every Cantor in the Reach thinks it. We teach it so. The tending note for the growing, the falling fifth for the harvest, the felling-song for the end. We teach it as if it were husbandry. Like pruning. It is not.*

*The orchard Cantors were not founded to sing to trees. They were founded to sing to the Root.*

*Ten thousand years ago, at the First Compact, when the god of Growth was bound under the Reach, there was a woman. We do not have her name. The Veil might have had it. She was a Cantor, though there was no such word yet, and she was the first person to go down to where the god was and sing to it. She sang it a lullaby. Five notes. If they take you down, you will hear them in the Root-vault, under the grinding, losing the tune. That is hers. That is the oldest song in the world. She sang it to the god so it would sleep, and hold, and not be afraid of being bound. And when she grew old she taught it to a girl, and the girl taught it to a girl, and that is what we are. That is what the Conservatory is. That is what every Cantor of the Reach has been for ten thousand years. Not orchard-keepers. The god's nurses.*

*And once a year, on the Longest Night, every Cantor in the Reach went down. Did you never wonder why we hummed into the hollow of the oldest tree? That was the shadow of it. That was what was left after the Synod's grandfathers took the vault for a survey and sealed it, three hundred years ago, and we were not allowed down any more. Before that we went down. On the Longest Night. All of us. Down the old stair, which the Synod filled in when they sank the bore. Into the vault. And we sat round the Root in the dark and sang it the lullaby, all through. From the first note to the last. So that it would remember. Because it forgets, Lyra. It always forgot. A god holding a law for ten thousand years gets tired, and the tired forget, and every year by the Longest Night it had lost a little more of the tune. So we went down, and sang it through, and it had the whole song again for another year.*

*It waited for us. My teacher told me. Her teacher had told her. On the Longest Night, the Root listened for the Cantors coming down the stair. It listened all year. It was the only night it was not alone.*

*Nobody has gone down since the Synod sealed the vault. Three hundred years. It has had nobody on the Longest Night for three hundred years. It has been losing the tune and nobody has come. You will hear it, under the grinding. Losing its place, and going back to the start. That is what three hundred years without a Cantor sounds like.*

*If they take you down, sing it the lullaby. All through. You know it. You have always known it. It is the song you hum when you are tired and think nobody can hear. I heard you, in the reed-hall, at nine. I did not tell you what it was.*

*If you come back, come and tell me what it said.*

*Ilse.*

---

She read it twice. Then she sat on the pallet with the three pages in her lap and did not move.

*It waited for us. It was the only night it was not alone.*

*The Longest Night.*

She thought of the night on the Shelf when they had all said the date aloud. *The Longest Night. The thirtieth of Frostmonth. That's when he'll do it. Under the Organum. With all eight.* She had thought, as they all had, that the old man had chosen it for some reason of his own. Some reckoning of the stars, some Synod calculation, some old Arbiter's sense of occasion. The longest dark of the year. A good night for an ending.

She had not thought that the gods might be waiting for it.

That there might be eight of them, under eight cities, that had been listening every Longest Night for ten thousand years for somebody to come down the stair. That they had stopped coming, one by one, as the faiths died and the stairs were sealed. That the gods had gone on listening anyway. That on the thirtieth of Frostmonth this year, after three hundred years of nobody, somebody was finally going to come down the stair to every one of them at once.

And it was going to be him.

She lifted her slate. Her hand was shaking. She wrote: *HE KNOWS.*

Ilse Brennan read it. She did not ask who. She looked at Lyra's face for a long moment, and then she wiped her own slate and wrote, slowly:

*THEN HE HAS BEEN LISTENING LONGER THAN ANY OF US.*

---

She went up to the Grove in the morning and went round it.

She did not tell anybody she was going to do it. She did not know she was going to do it until she was there, under the low arch of hands on the south side, with the slate round her neck and Ilse's letter inside her coat. And then she knew. She went to the first trunk to the left of the arch, and stood in front of it, and put her good hand flat on the grey-white bark under the face. And she listened.

Kaelen had said it on the Shelf, Tobiah told her, alone on the cutter, to the glass. *I'm going to listen to them first.* All seven. Before he did anything. Before he decided. The old man was going to go down the stair to every god in the world on the Longest Night and give them what they asked for, and Kaelen was going to listen to them first, so that he would know what he was talking about.

There were two hundred and twelve people in the wood. Aldric Vance had told her what they would want. He had said it out of the bark with his wet grey eyes. *For... ever. In dead... wood. Awake.* He had not asked her. He had told her, and waited.

She was not going to sing anybody down on Aldric Vance's word.

She stood with her hand on the bark and listened.

It took a long time. She had not known how long it would take. The face in the bark was a woman's: Ennis Crowle, who had kept bees on the Flume road. Her eyes were shut. She did not speak; none of them could speak. The wood had closed over their mouths on the ninth day. But Lyra had sat with four hundred of the burl-sick in the Hollow, and she knew how to listen to a person in wood. You put your hand flat, and you were still. You waited. And after a while, faint, through the bark and into your palm, you felt them. The pulse of something slow. The person in there, breathing in wood. And if you were very still, and very patient, and you were a Cantor of the Reach, you could ask them a question with your hand. The way you ask a tree in the orchard if it is ready for the harvest. And you could feel the answer come back.

She asked Ennis Crowle.

She could not have said how. Not on the slate. Not to Seraphina, who asked her later. She stood with her hand on the bark and her eyes shut, and asked, in the way a Cantor asks. *Do you want to go?*

And after a long time, faint, through the bark, she felt it.

*Yes.*

Like a door unlatching. Like a held breath let out.

She took her hand away. She took up her slate, and wrote on it, in small letters, a name. *ENNIS CROWLE.* And beside it a mark.

She went to the next.

---

It took her all day.

She went round the ring in the snow, trunk by trunk, under the grey lattice of hands. She put her good hand on each one and stood with her eyes shut and asked. And she waited, for as long as it took, for the answer. Some came quickly. Some took so long she thought there was nobody there. And then, at the last, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, there was.

Seraphina came at noon, and stood outside the ring, and watched through the gaps between the trunks. She did not come in. Lyra had not asked her not to. She stayed outside anyway, all afternoon, in the snow, with the Sun-Blade on her back and her pale hands folded, the way she had stood in the gallery at the Spire for three hundred sunrises. But not looking away.

Ignatius Crane brought soup at the second bell. He had made it in the reed-hall kitchen out of what Reedside had, which was not much, in Hetty Bram's apron, with his one good hand. He brought it up the river street in a covered pot and set it inside the arch on the south side, in the snow, and went away without a word. Lyra did not drink it. It went cold. At dusk she found that somebody had come and taken the cold pot away and left a hot one.

The Aegis stood at the arch all day. It did not come in. It could not have fitted. It stood outside with its helm bowed and the rosy light in it, and at intervals, very softly, so that she felt it in the frozen ground more than heard it, it hummed. The alto line. Not of Hymn 9. Something else. She did not recognise it at first. Then, in the late afternoon, with her hand on the bark of a boy called Fennick Dray who had been fifteen, she did.

It was the tending note. A hundred and seventy-four. Hummed through iron, in a child's alto, very soft. Over and over. The way you hum it to a growing thing.

She had never taught it to Wren. Wren had heard it through the deck of the *Kestrel* for weeks, hummed in the hold, and then at the edge of the Woods, whistled with blood.

---

By dusk she had been round the ring.

She stood at the arch on the south side with the slate in her hand. It was covered on both sides in small letters, names and marks, and she had had to rub out and start again three times and keep the count in her head. She looked at it in the last of the light.

Two hundred and twelve.

One hundred and eighty-seven had said yes.

Twenty-five had said no.

She had not expected that. She did not know why. Aldric Vance had told her what they would want, and she had believed him, because it was what she would have wanted. Dead wood. Awake. For ever. Who would not want to be let go of that?

Twenty-five people had stood in the ring with her hand on their bark and said, as clearly as a door shutting, *no.*

She did not know why. She could not ask. You could only ask a person in wood one question, and only yes or no. But she had felt it, each time. Not fear. She knew fear in the bark; she had felt it in the Hollow a hundred times. This was not fear. It was something else. Something that held on. Like a hand holding another hand.

She thought of the woman in the grey dress in the doorway in Whisper-Grove, that Sable had written about once. *— and I'll fetch the other one.* Saying it over and over, for three years, because it was the last thing she had.

She thought: *Some of them are not ready. Some of them are holding on to someone. Some of them just do not want to stop.* And she understood that she would never know which, and that it did not matter. They had been asked. They had said no.

She wiped the slate, and wrote on it, carefully, in the square capitals, the thing that she would carry up to Sol-Luminary and on to the Organum. The thing she would hold up in front of Kaelen on the night he went down the stair. Because it was the only thing she had learned at Oakhaven that he did not already know.

*212 ASKED. 187 YES. 25 NO.*

*NOT ALL OF THEM.*

---

Aldric Vance was the last.

She went in to him at dusk, under the arch, to the shaft-head. She knelt in front of him in the trampled snow, and put her good hand flat on the rough dark bark under his face, where the heart would be.

He was looking at her. He had been looking at her all day, she understood. Through the gaps in the ring, going from trunk to trunk.

"*Did you... ask them,*" he said.

She nodded.

"*All of... them.*"

She looked at him and did not look away.

"*What did they...*"

She held up the slate. He read it. His eyes went along the lines, slowly, and stopped at the second line, and stayed there.

*25 NO.*

She watched his face in the bark. She did not know what it was going to do. She thought he might be angry. He had been Governor of Oakhaven for twenty years, and he had made the ring, and he had told her what they would want.

The eyes filled again.

"*Good,*" said Aldric Vance. "*Oh... good. Good for them.*"

She had not expected that either.

"*I... told you what they'd... want,*" he said. "*I always... told people what they... wanted. I told the town it wanted... bread. I told the Synod it wanted the... Root. I told Kaelen he... wanted the Conservatory.*" The creak, deep in the wood. "*I was right about the... bread.*"

He looked at her.

"*Ask me,*" he said.

She did not understand.

"*You didn't... ask me. You went round all of them... and you didn't ask... me. Because I... told you. Last night. So you think you... know.*" The eyes held hers. "*Ask me... Cantor. Properly. The way you... asked them. I want to be... asked.*"

So she did.

She knelt in front of him with her hand on the bark and shut her eyes and was still. She waited until she felt him, slow and deep in the wood. Not like the others. Stronger, and stranger, with the iron under him and the Root under that, holding on. And she asked him. The way a Cantor asks. *Do you want to go?*

She felt the answer come.

*Yes.*

And something after it. Not an answer. She had not asked anything else. Something that came up through the bark into her palm, unasked, the way a man in a doorway says one more thing after the goodbye.

*Tell him I was afraid. Every day. From the day I sold it. Tell him that's how he'll know it was me.*

---

They sang them down at the dead hour of Hearthday night.

She could not sing it. She had known that all day. She had known it in the reed-hall in the night, lying on the pallet. A hundred and eighty-seven people in wood, waiting, and a song that would let them go, and a Cantor who could not sing it. She had thought about it going round the ring with her hand on the bark. And somewhere in the afternoon, with the Aegis humming the tending note outside the arch in a child's alto, she had understood what Ilse Brennan had meant.

*A Cantor's instrument is the breath. The voice is only the most common way of spending it.*

She had a voice. It was just not in her throat.

She had Seraphina's. She had Wren's, in the iron. She had Solenne's: copper and void, split down the middle, the voice that had sung the soprano line of the Rite of Pure Unison for three hundred sunrises and then fallen into the dark. She had Dorran Sedge's bass, like a bullfrog, which had sung in the orchard choir every Longest Night before 417. She had Ilse Brennan's breath. She had her own hands.

She taught it to them in the reed-hall that evening, after dusk. Not with her voice. She had none. With the slate, and her hands, and her lips. She whistled the falling fifth with blood at the back of her tongue, once, so they could hear the interval. Then she stopped whistling, because Ilse put a hand on her arm and shook her head. After that she conducted. She drew the shape of the felling-song in the air with her good hand, the fall and the hold and the fall below the fall. And she pointed. *You. This line. You. This one. Lower. Lower. There.*

Seraphina learned it in an hour. She had been trained since she was six. She sang the falling fifth back in a clear high voice that had been made for the Pure Note, and cracked on the hold, and wept, and sang it again.

Dorran Sedge already knew it. Every man who had sung in the orchard choir knew it. He had sung it at his own father's tree.

The Aegis learned it from Seraphina, note for note, the way it had learned the tending note from Lyra's whistle at the edge of the Woods. It sang it back in Wren's alto, through iron, too loud, the way children sing when they are not sure yet.

Solenne would not.

---

She stood in the doorway of the reed-hall with her broken wings folded and her arms crossed and would not.

"No," said Solenne. Her voice came out of her in two halves, the way it always had since Lumina. One half copper, bright and thin and ringing. The other half under it, a dark hollow sound like wind over the mouth of a well. "No. I'll not."

Lyra looked at her.

"You don't understand what you're doing," said Solenne. "You think you do. You've thought about it all day, going round with your hand on them. You've made your little marks on your slate. But you don't understand it." She uncrossed her arms. Her hands were shaking. "*This is what he wants to do.* Lyra. Do you see? This. Exactly this. A hundred and eighty-seven tired things, held in a place they can't get out of, for longer than they can bear. Asking to be let go. And someone who loves them going round with a hand on each one, and then singing them down, gently, so that it doesn't hurt. *This.* For the gods. On the Longest Night. Under the Organum." The copper half of her voice cracked. "And you're going to do it. Tonight. In the square. And it's going to be right. I know it's going to be right. I can feel it from here, I can feel them in the wood, I can feel how tired they are. And if it's right for them, then it's right for the gods. And then he's right. And I've been right all along, and I've been fighting it all along, and I don't—"

She stopped. She was breathing hard.

"I've wanted him to be right since Lumina," said Solenne, very low. "Since the morning I heard the question. I've wanted it so much I can't sleep. And you're going to stand in the square tonight and prove it. And you want me to sing."

Lyra looked at her for a long time.

Then she lifted the slate, and turned it round, so that Solenne could see what was written on it. She had not wiped it since dusk.

*212 ASKED. 187 YES. 25 NO.*

*NOT ALL OF THEM.*

Solenne read it.

She read it for a long time. Lyra watched her face. She watched the copper half and the dark half of it move, the way the two halves of her voice moved, not quite together.

"Twenty-five," said Solenne.

Lyra nodded.

"You asked every one. And twenty-five said no."

Lyra held her eyes.

"And you'll let them stay. In the dead wood. Awake. For ever. Because they said no."

Lyra did not look away.

Solenne stood in the doorway of the reed-hall. Then, slowly, she unfolded her arms. She looked down at her hands. They had stopped shaking.

"He won't ask," she said. "Will he. He's listened to them for eighty years. He knows what they want. He's going to tell them."

Lyra did not nod. She did not have to.

"Which line," said Solenne Aubade.

---

They went up the river street at the dead hour with lanterns. Lyra went first. Then Ilse Brennan, on Dorran's arm, with her stick. Then Seraphina, and the Aegis, and Solenne, and Ignatius Crane with a lantern in his good hand, and behind him the forty-one from Reedside. All of them. Even the children. Lyra had not wanted the children. Ilse had written on her slate: *THEY HAVE MOTHERS AND FATHERS IN THE RING. THEY WILL COME OR THEY WILL HEAR ABOUT IT AFTER. WHICH IS WORSE.* And Lyra had looked at it, and let them come.

They stood round the outside of the ring in the snow, in the dark, with the lanterns. The faces in the bark were pale in the lantern-light, smooth and still with their eyes shut, two hundred and twelve of them. The lattice of grey hands went up overhead into the dark.

Lyra went round the ring with a lump of red chalk from the reed-hall. She marked the twenty-five. A small mark, low on the bark, under the hands. So that the others would know. So that nobody would sing to them.

Then she went in under the arch to the shaft-head, and knelt in front of Aldric Vance, and put her good hand on the bark under his face.

He looked at her.

"*Lyra,*" he said. Very faint now. The creak in the wood was so deep she could feel it in her knees. "*Tell him I... was afraid.*"

She nodded.

"*Tell him I... waved.*"

She pressed her hand flat against the bark, which was the same thing.

He shut his eyes.

She stood up and turned round and lifted her good hand.

---

Seraphina began it.

The falling fifth. High and clear, from outside the ring on the south side, in the snow. The voice that had been made for the Pure Note, singing a Reach felling-song in the dark. Down a fifth. And held.

Wren took it under her. The alto, through iron, from the arch. Too loud, and wavering, and exactly in tune.

Dorran Sedge came in below that. The bass. The note under the tending note, the one you feel in the floor. He sang it with his hat in his hands and the tears running into his white beard, the way he had sung it at his father's tree.

And Solenne.

Lyra had given her the line under Dorran's. The fall below the fall. The lowest note in the felling-song, that the old Cantors said was not a note at all but the sound the sap makes going down. Solenne sang it in her void voice. The dark half. The half that had come back with her from the place where Ignatius Crane's fire had thrown her. It came out of her like wind over the mouth of a well. It went down into the frozen ground of the square and Lyra felt it in her feet, in her knees, in the bones of her hand on the bark.

And under all of it, with no voice at all, Ilse Brennan breathed. She stood at the arch on Dorran's arm with her stick and her eyes shut and breathed: four counts in, and a long fall out. The tending breath, the first thing every Cantor learned at eight, before the note, before anything. The old Cantors said the trees could hear the breath before they heard the voice.

Lyra stood at the shaft-head with her hand raised and conducted.

---

They went.

Not all at once. One by one, round the ring, the way Aldric Vance had said they had gone quiet on the ninth day, like lamps along a street.

She watched it. She had watched it four hundred times in the Hollow, and she watched it now, and it was the same and not the same. The first was Ennis Crowle, to the left of the arch, who had kept bees on the Flume road. Lyra saw the face in the bark change. Not move. Change. The lines of it softened. The brow went smooth. The shut eyes, which had been shut tight, like a person's eyes shut against something, eased. As if she had been holding something up for five weeks and had been told she could put it down.

And then the bark round the face went a little paler. And then a little paler still. And then it was only bark. White-grey, smooth, still. With a shape in it that had been a face.

And the next.

And the next.

Round the ring. Seraphina singing, and Wren, and Dorran, and Solenne, and Ilse breathing under them. The forty-one standing outside the ring with their lanterns, not singing, some of them. Some of them humming, very low, the tending note, which was wrong, which was the wrong song entirely, the song for growing. Lyra did not stop them. And one by one, round the ring of grey hands, the faces eased, and went pale, and were bark.

Not the twenty-five. She watched those too. Every time the song came round to a trunk with a red chalk mark low on it, the voices dropped. Seraphina's and Wren's and Dorran's and Solenne's, all together, without being told, to nothing. A held silence the length of a breath, as the song went past. And then took up again at the next. The twenty-five stood in the ring with their faces in the bark, and their eyes shut, and the song went round them like water round a stone.

She did not know how long it took. Seraphina's voice was in rags by the end. Dorran had gone hoarse. Solenne had stopped being able to stand and was kneeling in the snow outside the ring, singing the fall below the fall with her forehead on the frozen ground. Only Wren did not falter. The alto through the iron went on, too loud and exactly in tune, round and round the ring, the way the Aegis had stood at the Cloister for three years guarding the empty risers.

And then it was only Aldric Vance.

---

She knelt in front of him.

He had not gone. All round her, the ring was bark. A hundred and eighty-seven pale shapes that had been faces, and twenty-five faces that still were, with their eyes shut and their red chalk marks. And the song had come round to him, to the shaft-head, to the dark rough trunk with its roots in the iron. And the four voices had sung it to him, and Ilse had breathed it.

And he had not gone.

His eyes were open. Grey and wet. Looking at her.

"*It won't... let me,*" said Aldric Vance.

She put her hand on the bark.

She felt it. Under him. Under the iron, under the ground, four hundred feet down. The Root. Holding on to him. She did not know how she could feel it. She was not glassed. She did not have the ear for gods that Kaelen had, or Solenne. But she had sat in the vault in Harvestmonth and heard it under the grinding, losing the tune. And she felt it now, through Aldric Vance's bark, through his roots and down into the iron: the Root, holding on to the one man in the Reach who had ever sold it. The way you hold on to the last person who knows your name.

And she felt it ask.

Not her. Not the way Kaelen had described it, a vast attention turning. It did not turn to her. It did not know she was there. It was asking him. Asking Aldric Vance, through his roots, the way it had been asking him since Nullday. Over and over. Very faint. Very tired.

She heard it. She heard the shape of it come up through his bark into her hand.

*Is it you? Have you come?*

And she heard the thing Auld Bessa had heard on the Rust Coast, the thing Vane would tell them about later. The thing Kaelen would hear under the Organum. She heard where it went up at the end.

Like a child at a door.

*Have you come down the stair?*

*Is it the night?*

---

She knew the song.

She had always known it. Ilse had said so. It was the one she hummed when she was tired and thought nobody could hear. Five notes, and a turn, and the five again lower, and a fall. She had hummed it in the reed-hall at nine. She had heard it in the vault in Harvestmonth, under the grinding, and known it, and not known why she knew it. She had sung the falling fifth to the Root that day, four hundred feet down, and it had let go of the question and started the lullaby again from the beginning.

She could not sing it.

She knelt in front of Aldric Vance with her hand on his bark and the Root asking under him, *is it you, is it the night*. And she put her lips together.

Ilse's hand came down on her shoulder. She felt it. She did not stop.

She whistled it.

Five notes. Thin and breathy and wavering, through the torn place in her throat. It went into her like a knife going in, and she felt the warm come up at the back of her tongue, and she swallowed iron, and went on. The turn. The five again, lower. The fall. Into the dark in the middle of the ring, over the shaft-head, over Aldric Vance's face in the bark. The oldest song in the world, that a woman with no name had sung to a god ten thousand years ago so that it would not be afraid of being bound.

The blood was in her mouth. She went on.

She whistled it all through. From the first note to the last. The way the Cantors used to on the Longest Night, all of them, round the Root in the dark. The way nobody had for three hundred years.

And under the iron, four hundred feet down, she felt the Root stop asking.

---

It let go of him.

She felt it. Through her hand. The Root, four hundred feet down, holding on to Aldric Vance's roots with everything it had left. And then, as the last note of the lullaby went out into the dark, it let go. Not gently. Not all at once. The way an old man lets go of the rail at the top of a stair, finger by finger, because somebody has taken his arm.

*Not yet*, she thought. *Not the night. Not yet. But somebody came.*

Aldric Vance looked at her out of the bark.

"*Oh,*" he said. "*Oh. There it... goes.*"

His face eased.

She watched it. She kept her hand on the bark under it and watched. The lines round the grey eyes went smooth. The mouth, which had been shut hard in the bark for five weeks the way a man shuts his mouth on a thing he will not say, softened. The eyes stayed open. They looked at her. They were wet. They were not afraid.

That was the last thing she would think, afterward. That at the very end, for the first time since she had known him, which was all her life, Aldric Vance was not afraid. And that she would have to tell Kaelen, who was not afraid either, that his father had been afraid every day, and that it was how he would know it was him. And that it was the only thing she could not write down on the slate in a way that he would feel.

The eyes closed.

The bark went pale.

---

Afterward she sat on the iron of the shaft-head with her back against his trunk and blood on her chin. Ignatius Crane came in under the arch with a lantern and a cup of something hot and set it down beside her in the snow. He did not say anything. He looked at her mouth. Then he took off Hetty Bram's apron and folded it and pressed it into her good hand, so that she would have something to wipe her chin with. He went out again.

Outside the ring, she could hear the forty-one going back down the river street to Reedside, slowly, with the children. Some of them were still humming the tending note. The wrong song. She let them.

She sat against the trunk of Aldric Vance until the lanterns had all gone down the hill. Then she took the slate from round her neck, and wiped it, and wrote on it, slowly, in the dark, by feel:

*TELL HIM HE WAS AFRAID EVERY DAY.*

*TELL HIM HE WAVED.*

She did not wipe it. She hung it back round her neck with the words on it, against her chest, inside Hetty Bram's coat. She meant to keep them there until she saw Kaelen.

---

They went down the Root on Massday.

Not the bore. The bore was choked: four hundred feet of dead leaves and rot packed into it by the Greening and frozen solid. Dorran Sedge took them to the river gate instead, and along the outside of the Hedge in the snow. Past the North Gate, where Lyra did not look at the grey thorn and the thorn did not say anything. To a place on the east side of the town where the Hedge met the old wall of the Conservatory's orchard. There, under a fallen iron-bark oak, half-buried in dead leaves, was a slab of grey stone with a ring set in it.

*The old stair*, Ilse's slate said. *The one the Synod filled in. They filled it at the bottom, where it came into the vault. Not at the top. Engineers are lazy. My teacher showed me. I have never been down it. Nobody has, in three hundred years.*

It took Dorran and the Aegis together to lift the slab. Under it, a stair went down into the dark. Narrow, cut in the rock, turning, with a rope rail of rotted hemp. A cold breath came up out of it that smelled of stone and earth and something under those, faint and green, like cut stalks.

*It comes out above the fill*, Ilse wrote. *There is a gallery. You can look down into the vault from there. You cannot get down. But you can be near.*

Lyra looked at her.

*You are not going down that*, she wrote. *You are seventy-eight.*

Ilse Brennan looked at the slate. Then she wiped her own and wrote:

*I have been seventy-eight for some time. It has never stopped me doing anything I wanted.*

---

It was four hundred and some steps. Lyra lost count.

She went first, with a lantern. Then Ilse, on the Aegis's arm. The Aegis could not fit in the stair; it took Ilse down to the first turning and then stopped, wedged, with its helm bowed and the rosy light in it, and hummed. Then Seraphina took Ilse's other arm, and Lyra took the first, and they went down between them, one step at a time. The old woman's stick went *tap* on every step. Dorran stayed at the top. Solenne stayed with the Aegis. Ignatius had gone down to Reedside at dawn to cook, because forty-one people had to eat, and Hetty Bram would have.

It was cold. It got colder. She had thought it would get warmer, going down; the earth was warm, deep down, everybody knew that. It did not. It got colder with every turning, a still, dry, bone-deep cold, until the lantern-flame stood straight up in it without a flicker and their breath hung in front of their faces and did not move. She thought: *the Root kept the cold out of the Reach. All of it. For ten thousand years. And now it has let go, and the cold has come in, and it has come in here first. To the bottom. To where the Root is.*

It came out, at last, at a gallery.

A ledge of carved stone with a low parapet, and beyond the parapet, nothing. Dark. A vast dark, going down and down. And from far below, faint, a light.

Green. Very faint. Like the lilac under the ice of the pools in the Woods. Deep down.

Lyra went to the parapet and looked over.

---

The Root filled the vault.

She had been in it once. From the bottom, in Harvestmonth, in white, with a wire in a fold of her skin and the Church's Pure Note in her ears. She had been at the bore-head, at the Synod's iron platform, where the bit went into the side of the god. She had seen only the part of it the bore had bitten. A wall of pale glistening root as wide as a house, weeping green, with the great iron bit in it, grinding.

She had not seen the whole of it.

From the gallery she saw it. It filled the vault from wall to wall, as far as the lantern reached and beyond, down into the dark. It was not one root. It was ten thousand, a hundred thousand, grown together into one. They twisted and folded over each other like the muscles of an arm, like the strands of a great rope, going down. And it was pale. It had been green in Harvestmonth, weeping green, luminous. It was grey now. Grey like the Grove. Grey like the Hedge. The green was only in the deepest places, far down, faint, the way the last coal glows in a fire that has gone out.

The bore-head was still in it. She could see the iron bit, far below, rusted, sunk in the grey. And round the bit, the place where the Root had cracked in Harvestmonth when it drank the purge-water and screamed. A wound. Black, and long, and open. It had never closed.

And she could hear it.

Faint. From very far down. Not through the air. Through the stone of the parapet under her hands, through the soles of her feet. A sound like a voice humming to itself in another room.

Five notes. A turn.

And stop.

And a silence. A long one.

And the five notes again. From the beginning.

---

Ilse Brennan sat down on the stone floor of the gallery with her back against the parapet.

She did it slowly, with Seraphina's arm and her stick, and when she was down she sat for a long time with her eyes shut, breathing. Four counts in, and a long fall out. In the lantern-light her face was grey. Her breath hung in front of her and did not move.

Then she opened her eyes and took up her slate.

*I AM GOING TO STAY.*

Lyra looked at it.

She had known. She thought she had known since the reed-hall, since *I have been seventy-eight for some time*. She had known on the stair, with the old woman's stick going *tap* on every step and the cold coming up. She had not let herself know.

*NO*, she wrote.

Ilse Brennan looked at it. She did not wipe her own slate. She held it up again, the same words, and waited, the way she had waited at the front of the reed-hall for eight years for Lyra to get an interval right.

*NO*, wrote Lyra again. She pressed so hard the chalk broke. *IT IS NINE DAYS. IT IS COLD. YOU WILL DIE.*

Ilse looked at that for a long time. Then she wiped her slate and wrote. Slowly. The small upright hand, gone shaky with the cold.

*Yes. Probably before the ninth. I am old and I have not been warm since Ashfall. I know.*

*Listen to me, Lyra. It has had nobody for three hundred years. On the Longest Night it is going to listen for the stair, the way it always has. And somebody is going to come down. Not us. Him. And he is going to ask it if it wants to put the weight down, and it is going to be so tired, and so alone, and it has been waiting so long for somebody, anybody, that it will say yes to the first voice it hears.*

*I do not want the first voice it hears in three hundred years to be his.*

She stopped. She breathed. Four in, and a fall.

*I cannot sing it the lullaby. I have no voice. But I have breath. And I know the song. And it can hear breath before it hears voice; that is the first thing my teacher taught me. So I am going to sit here and breathe it the song. All through. Over and over. For as long as I last. So that when it listens for the stair, there is somebody already here.*

*So that it is not alone when he comes.*

*So that it knows it has been asked by someone who did not want anything from it.*

---

Lyra did not write anything for a long time.

She knelt on the stone floor of the gallery in front of the old woman, with the broken chalk in her hand. Below them, far down, the Root went through its five notes and lost its place and began again.

Then Ilse Brennan reached into the pocket of her heavy skirt and took something out and held it out to her.

A reed.

A short length of wind-reed, a hand long, cut and bored and polished smooth with handling. Not green. Sugar-glass. Pale gold and clear, like amber, the way the wind-reeds of the Reach had gone in the Cascade before they blew away. There was a single hole in the side of it, near one end. Lyra knew what it was. Every Cantor-Master of the Reedside Conservatory had carried one. The pitch-reed. You blew across it, and it gave the tending note, a hundred and seventy-four, true. You gave it to your students so they would know where to begin.

It was the last one. All the others had blown away.

*The breath is the instrument*, Ilse's slate said. *The reed is only so you know where to start.*

*Take it up with you. Take it to the Organum. When he goes down the stair on the Longest Night, find whatever god you can reach and give it the note. So that it knows where to begin.*

Lyra took it.

She held it in her good hand. It was warm from the old woman's pocket. It was the only warm thing in the vault.

Then she put down the broken chalk and leaned forward and put her arms round Ilse Brennan. She held her for a long time on the stone floor of the gallery, in the cold, with the lantern burning straight up beside them and the god humming to itself far below.

She felt the old woman breathing against her. Four in. A long fall out.

---

They left her the lantern, and all the blankets, and the cup Ignatius had sent, and the soup. Seraphina knelt and took off her own white cloak, the one from the Spire, and wrapped it round the old woman's knees. She stood up with her face wet and did not say anything at all, and went to the stair.

Lyra was the last.

She stood at the top of the first step with the pitch-reed in her hand and looked back. Ilse Brennan was sitting against the parapet in the circle of lantern-light, in Seraphina's white cloak and four blankets, with her stick across her knees and her slate beside her. Her eyes were shut. Her breath went out in front of her, slowly, and hung, and did not move. Four counts in. A long fall out.

And then a different shape. Lyra watched it. The breath going out of the old woman's mouth, not in the tending rhythm, but in another. Five breaths. Short, and long, and short. A turn. Five again. A fall.

Below, far down, she heard the Root go through its five notes.

And not stop.

It went on. Through the turn. Into the five again, lower. It had not got that far in Harvestmonth. It had not got that far in three hundred years without somebody to remind it. It went into the fall. And stopped there, at the last note, the way a child stops at the top of the stairs, waiting to be told goodnight.

Ilse Brennan breathed the last note.

And the Root went back to the beginning, and went through it again, all the way to the end, with the old woman's breath going out beside it in the cold, note for note.

Lyra went up the stair.

---

She stood on the slab at the top in the grey afternoon, with the snow coming down on the bare iron-bark oaks and the pitch-reed in her fist. Seraphina stood beside her with her arms wrapped round herself, in her shirt, with no cloak. The Aegis stood behind them with its helm bowed.

Solenne was on the fallen oak, sitting, with her broken wings round her like a coat. She looked up as Lyra came out of the stair.

"She's staying," said Solenne. It was not a question.

Lyra nodded.

"She'll die."

Lyra did not move.

Solenne looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked down at the slab, and the dark of the stair under it, and the cold coming up out of it.

"She asked it," said Solenne slowly. "Didn't she. She went down and sat beside it and asked it. Not *do you want to put it down.* Something else. Something before that." The copper half of her voice was very quiet. "I don't know what. I can't hear it from here. But it's not asking any more. Not the way it was. It's—" She stopped. "It's just singing."

Lyra did not answer. She did not have to. She lifted the slate on its cord, that still had the two lines on it for Kaelen, and turned it over to the clean side, and wrote:

*SHE ASKED IT IF IT WAS ALL RIGHT.*

*NOBODY HAS ASKED IT THAT FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS.*

---

They left Oakhaven on Massday evening, going north. Lyra went to the Grove once more before they went. She stood under the low arch of hands on the south side and looked at the ring. A hundred and eighty-seven pale shapes in the bark that had been faces, and his within them. And twenty-five that still were, with their eyes shut, and their red chalk marks low on the trunks, holding hands with the dead.

She put her hand on the nearest of the twenty-five. A woman called Garra Thorne, who had taught at the orchard-school. She stood with her hand on the bark and her eyes shut until she felt her, faint and slow in the wood, still there.

She did not ask her anything. She had already asked.

She took the pitch-reed out of her coat, and put it to her lips, and blew across it. Once. Softly.

A hundred and seventy-four. The tending note. True. Thin and clear and pale gold in the snow, out of the last wind-reed in the Reach. The song for a growing thing. The wrong song entirely, for a woman in dying wood who would never grow again.

She blew it anyway. For the twenty-five, who had been asked, and had said no. So that they would know somebody had heard.

Then she put the reed away and went out under the arch and north along the Greenroot road with the others, in the dark, in the snow. Behind them, at the North Gate, as they passed it, the grey Hedge said her name once more in her father's voice, *Lyra-love*, with the little fall at the end, like a question. She did not turn round. She did not need to. She had learned what the question was, and who it was for, and that it was not hers to answer.

Under her feet, four hundred feet down, faint, so faint she could not be sure she felt it, a god sang itself a lullaby all the way to the end. An old woman breathed the last note beside it in the dark. And then they began again.

*End of Part One.*
