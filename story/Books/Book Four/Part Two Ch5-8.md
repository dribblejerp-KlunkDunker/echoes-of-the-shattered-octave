# BOOK FOUR: THE RELIEF SHIFT

## PART TWO: THE BLEACH FALLS

## CHAPTER FIVE
### Dawnday

> *Are you in the shade? — I'm in yours.*
>
> — The greeting of the Umbral Tiers of Sol-Luminary, since 412. Before that the city said *Light keep you*, and some of the old still say it, and are not answered

*Sol-Luminary. Stasisday the twenty-second of Frostmonth, to Dawnday the twenty-third.*

---

The Blade had been asking for four days, and she had not told anyone.

She knew the night it began. Nullday, the eighteenth, on the Greenroot road, in the snow, the night every leaf in Oakhaven came down. She had been asleep in a hay-barn at the fourth milestone with the Blade beside her in its white leather. She woke at the dead hour because her hand was ringing. Not the hand. The bones of it. She had gone to sleep with her fingers on the scabbard, as she had every night since she was sixteen, and something had come up out of the scabbard into her fingers and on into the bones of her wrist, very faint, the way a struck glass goes on sounding in the hand that holds it after the ear has stopped hearing it.

She had lain in the hay with her eyes open and listened, and heard nothing.

Then she had felt it go up at the end.

She did not know what that meant, that first night. She knew now. She had stood outside the ring of grey hands at Oakhaven for a whole afternoon and watched Lyra go round it with her palm flat on the bark, and she had seen Lyra's face at the shaft-head at the end, with the blood on her chin, listening to something under the iron. She had heard Solenne, afterward, on the fallen oak: *it's not asking any more. It's just singing.* She knew what asking was.

She had not told them. She had not told Lyra, who had enough to carry. She had not told her sister, who would know what it meant before Seraphina did, and say so, in two voices. She had not told the priest, because she did not tell the priest anything any more.

She had carried it north up the Greenroot road, and on to the Flume crossing, and onto the deck of a coal-freighter out of the Keel Docks. She carried it now, on her back, in the white leather, under a borrowed coat. Asking, very faintly, into the bones of her spine. The way it had asked the forty on the risers, in the Foundry, on the three hundredth morning. The way it had asked her sister.

She had been taught that the Blade was a gift. A splinter of the god's own body, given to House Aubade by the Light in the First Cycle, so that the light would know its own and not eat it. She had been taught that it was the proof of her House's grace.

She understood now that she had been taught wrong about that too. It was not a gift. It was a piece of somebody. And the somebody wanted to know if she was the one it had been waiting for.

---

The freighter was called the *Small Hours*, and she smelled of coal and lamp-oil and the wet wool of the eleven men who crewed her. She had come down from the Heights to the Flume crossing on a chime out of Null-Watch, eight days before, and waited at her mast in a field of frozen reed for five people nobody would name to her. Her master was a Tiers man called Hap Tolland. He wore a black veil from the brim of his hat to his collarbones even at the wheel, even at night, even four hundred miles from the Spire, and he had not once lifted it.

"Habit," he said, when the priest asked him. "You stop taking it off, you stop forgetting to put it on."

He did not ask what the Aegis was. He did not ask about the wings. He looked at Seraphina once, at the Flume crossing, at the shape of the thing on her back under the coat, and then he looked at the deck and did not look at her again for three days. She knew why. Everybody in the Tiers knew who carried the Light's bone. Everybody in the Tiers knew that she had stood in the gallery at the Foundry for three hundred sunrises in white plate and had not looked at the risers, and that the light had parted round her on the last morning, and that eleven thousand and forty people had not been so lucky.

She did not blame him. She had not looked at herself either, for seven years. There were no mirrors in the chains.

They raised the Heights on Stasisday at noon.

She had come home this way once before, in Ashfall, on the *Kestrel*, and she had stood at the rail then the way she stood now, with her hands folded, and watched the white peaks come up over the bow. They had been white then the way they had always been white: snow, and ice in the high corries, and bare rock gone pale with seven years of the wrong light.

They were not that white now.

She could not have said at first what was different. She stood at the rail with the snow coming down on the deck, the ordinary grey snow of the lowlands, and watched the Heights come up, and there was something wrong with the colour of them. Then the *Small Hours* came over the last of the foothills into the long glacial valley under the Spire, and the snow round the ship began to change.

It went paler as it fell.

She watched it. A flake came down past her face, grey, a lowland flake, and as it fell past the rail it went cream, and then white, and then it was not white but something past white: thin, and clear at the edges, like the flake had been drawn on the air in chalk and somebody was rubbing it out. It reached the deck. It was gone before it touched. There was no wet mark on the planking where it should have landed.

All round the ship, the snow was doing it. Coming down grey out of the cloud, and going pale, and going out, before it reached anything.

"*Light*," said one of Hap Tolland's men, at the halyards, very low.

"Don't," said Hap Tolland, without turning from the wheel. "Not that word. Not here. Get the canvas up."

---

They put up the canvas over the deck, black, on poles, the way the Tiers did, so that the whole ship ran in its own shade. They put Solenne under it first. They put the Aegis under it, where it stood in the middle of the deck with its helm bowed and the rosy light in its slot and the snow going out round it. The priest stood under it at the stern without being told.

Lyra did not go under. She came to the rail beside Seraphina in Hetty Bram's coat, with her burned hand inside it against her chest and the slate round her neck, and stood there, looking at the snow.

Seraphina looked at her. The snow was going pale round Lyra's face. Round her lashes, her mouth, the cord of the slate. One flake touched her cheek and went out on it, and left nothing, and Lyra did not move.

"Cantor," said Seraphina. "Under the canvas."

Lyra looked at her. Then she looked at Seraphina's hands, folded on the rail. At the snow falling on them. The snow did not go pale on Seraphina's hands. It fell on the backs of her fingers grey, and wet, and melted, and ran. It did that on her hair, and on the shoulders of her borrowed coat, and on the rail in front of her for a hand's breadth on either side. It went out everywhere else.

*The light will not eat its own bone.*

Lyra turned the slate round. The side with the words for Kaelen on it was against her chest, under the coat, where it always was now. She wrote on the back.

*IT IS WORSE THAN ASHFALL.*

"Yes," said Seraphina.

*HOW MUCH WORSE.*

Seraphina did not answer. She looked up the valley at the Spire.

It stood up out of the peaks at the head of the valley, a mile high, white, with the crack down its side from the top a third of the way to its root, and the crack was black. That had not changed. What had changed was round it. In Ashfall the wrong light had lain round the Spire for a mile in every direction, overexposed, edgeless, so bright the eye slid off it. It still did. But the mile was not a mile. She tried to find its edge and could not. It went down the valley toward them. It went out over the peaks to east and west. It had no edge any more. It simply got less, very slowly, the further you were from the Spire, the way the warmth of a fire gets less, and there was no line in the whole white valley where you could say: *here, it stops*.

And hanging from the Spire on its nine chains, the city.

She looked at the city for a long time.

"Get under the canvas, Cantor," she said at last. "Please."

Lyra looked at her face. Then she went.

---

Sol-Luminary hung from the Spire's flank a thousand feet below the crack, as it always had, a great inverted cone of tiers and domes on nine chains, like a wasps' nest under an eave. The broad top of it lay level with the Spire's waist. The narrow bottom dangled over the gulf. Seraphina had been born on the top of it, in the house of her mother's family on the south rim, with a window that looked straight across at the Spire. She had learned her catechism on the ninth tier, in the Basilica school, on a bench under the great east window. She had gone down to the Keel Docks, the very bottom, only twice in her life before the Fracture, and both times in a closed chair.

In Ashfall, the line had stopped below the seventh tier.

That was where it had stopped on the morning of the Restoration. She had watched it stop, from the walkway of the Pilgrim Chain, with the Blade bare in her hand and her sister walking behind her. A hard straight edge across the city's flank like a tide-mark. Above it, the white. Below it, the Umbral Tiers: black-painted, black-awninged, nineteen thousand people living on the underside of a city whose top had been eaten by the light. Seven years they had lived there. They had learned it. They had built houses that hung down off the underside like bats from the roof of a cave, and they had painted every window black, and they had taught their children silence before letters and to walk in the shade of the person ahead.

She found the line.

It was at the fourth tier.

---

She did not say anything. There was nobody to say it to who would not already have seen. She stood at the rail with the snow melting on her hands and looked at it, and counted, the way Vane would have counted, the way the scribe would have written it down in a ruled column. Seventh. Sixth. Fifth. Three tiers in nine days, and the fourth half gone.

The Basilica's black tower was above the line. It had been above the line since Ashfall; it had been built on the ninth tier, straddling the old line with an iron wall through its nave, and in Ashfall the line had come down past it. She had not seen it since. She saw it now. It stood up out of the white on the city's south-west flank, the one black thing above the line, black stone, black slate, with its bell-chamber open. And round its foot, very faint, she could see a pale gold shimmer like heat over a road.

Glass-fire. The Church had ringed its tower with censers. It was holding the Basilica in the white the way the Flambeaux held their bubbles of ordinary shade on the walkway of the Pilgrim Chain: with fire, and faith, in shifts.

She watched the gold shimmer for a long time. She could not hear the Pure Note from here. She knew it was being held. Nine choristers in three rows in the dark of the choir-stalls, the shade half of the nave, taking the note one from another without a breath between them, day and night. It had been held without a break since the morning of the Fracture. It had been held through Ashfall. It would be held, she knew, until the last chorister fell off the end of the stall.

Somebody would be standing at the Altar of the nave at the dawn service tomorrow, the Dawnday service, in white, with his old hands raised.

The Blade asked, on her back, into the bones of her spine. Very faint.

She did not answer it.

---

The city's shadow was narrower than it had been in Ashfall. Hap Tolland had to bring the *Small Hours* up the inside of it toward the Keel Docks the way Vane had brought the *Kestrel*, and he was not Vane. He did it slowly. He did it with three men on the canvas poles and one at the stern calling the edge of the shade, and when the edge came in close on the windward side he did not try anything clever. He put her nose straight down into the gulf and dropped two hundred feet, and let the shadow find them again further down where it was wider, and came up under the city from below like a fish coming up under a boat.

It was ugly flying. It worked.

The Keel Docks were full.

In Ashfall there had been eleven cradles and three ships. There were still eleven cradles, great iron baskets hanging on chains off the very bottom of the city, swinging over two thousand feet of nothing. Every one had a ship in it. Freighters, packets, a Church tender with its sun-disc painted out, two Free Captains' brigs with their envelopes stitched black. And beyond the cradles, moored to the cradles, moored to each other, moored to anything that would take a line, more. Fishing-skiffs from the glacial lakes with little patched bags. A barge that had no business in the air at all, hung under a cluster of six balloons like grapes. Rafts. The whole underside of the city was a raft of hulls and envelopes, all in the shade, all very still, all lashed together, the way boats crowd into a harbour before a storm.

Nobody on any of them made a sound.

Hap Tolland brought the *Small Hours* in alongside a coal-barge, three hulls out from the nearest cradle, and his men passed lines across without a word, and made fast, and he let go of the wheel.

"Well," he said, under his veil. "That's that, then. They've all come home."

Seraphina looked at him.

"When the Tiers want a ship," said Hap Tolland, "they ring for it. There's a chime-house on the third tier. It's been ringing since Nullday." He turned and looked up, through his veil, at the black underside of the city hanging over them, the hanging houses, the black-painted windows, level on level, dark. "Every ship the Tiers have got a share in. Every ship with a Tiers master. All back. All in the shade." He was quiet for a moment. "There's not enough of us. There was never going to be enough of us. They know that. They rang anyway."

"For what?"

He did not answer that. He was looking up at the city.

"Brenna'll tell you," he said. "She's been waiting for you. She's been waiting for somebody."

---

The harbourmaster came down the ladder from the tier above in her black veil and her black gloves, with her shuttered lantern, as she had in Ashfall. The same woman. Seraphina knew her by the way she put her feet: always in shade, always exactly, as if the deck were a floor of black and white tiles and the white ones would take her foot off. The two men with hooked poles came behind her in her shadow, step for step.

She did not ask for glass. She looked at the Aegis, nine feet of iron under the black canvas with *DEFEND THE SINGERS* on its chest, and at the wings, and at Lyra's slate, and at the shape on Seraphina's back. Then she looked at the priest.

Ignatius Crane stood at the stern of the *Small Hours* in the shade of the canvas, in a plain brown coat that had belonged to a Watch trooper, with his white hand bare at his side. He had stopped wearing the glove at Oakhaven. He had not said why.

The harbourmaster looked at the white hand for a long time.

"Inquisitor," she said.

"Not any more," said Ignatius.

"No," said the harbourmaster. "I heard." She turned her veiled face to Seraphina. "Knight. Brenna Corliss is at the chandlery. She says you're to come up. All of you. She says to bring the iron one, if it'll climb." A pause. "She says to bring the priest as well. I don't know why. I'd have left him on the boat."

She turned to go. Then she stopped, and turned back, and took something out of the breast of her black coat. A bag. Black velvet, drawn shut with a cord, the size of two fists. She held it out, not to Seraphina, but to Lyra.

"The *Kestrel*'s," she said. "From Ashfall. You left in a hurry. I keep what I take. I give it back when you leave the chains." Under the veil her tired face did not change. "You're not leaving. I know that. But I heard who's dead. I thought you'd want them before there's no shade left to give them back in."

Lyra took the bag. She did not open it. She held it against Hetty Bram's coat with her burned hand, and Seraphina knew what was in it, because she had stood on the deck in Ashfall and watched them go in. A sextant's index-mirror. A shaving-glass. A tin looking-glass with a dent in its rim. A girl's brass compact with a sprig of something engraved on the lid.

Pennick's. Vane's. Hetty's. Bettany's.

Three of the four were dead.

Lyra put the bag inside her coat, next to the slate, and did not look at it again.

---

They climbed.

The ladders went up from the Keel Docks through the hanging houses on iron rungs bolted into black stone, and walkways ran between them on chains, swaying. It was the dead of the afternoon and the Tiers were dark. Seraphina had expected that. What she had not expected was the people.

They were everywhere. On every walkway, on every ladder-head, in every doorway, in the shade. Sitting. Standing. Waiting. Not talking. Families on the walkways with bundles at their feet, tied up in black cloth, a bundle to a person, each the same size. Old men with their backs to the black stone and their eyes shut. Children, a great many children, sitting in rows along the walkways with their knees drawn up, in the shade of the adults beside them, very still. Every one of them had a bundle. Every one of them was facing the same way: down. Toward the Keel Docks. Toward the gulf.

Somewhere on a walkway below them, as they climbed, a child was humming. Very low, under its breath, two notes: a high one, and under it, a hair off, a low one that did not match. Seraphina knew it. Every soul in the Tiers knew it. It was what Lyra had sung on Lumina under two hundred throats, the second note, the one that sent the Light back to sleep. The Tiers had been humming it since Ashfall. Somebody hushed the child. It stopped.

Lyra, on the ladder above, did not look down. Seraphina saw her hand go still on the rung for a moment, and then go on.

Nobody looked at the Aegis as it climbed past them, grinding at the knee, with the ladder-rungs groaning under it. Nobody looked at the wings. They looked at the priest. All of them. Every face on every walkway turned to follow Ignatius Crane up the ladders through the Tiers in his brown coat with his white hand bare, and turned back again when he had passed, and nobody said anything at all.

Seraphina climbed behind him. She watched him feel it. She watched his back.

He did not stop. He did not look round. He climbed.

On the second tier, at the head of the last ladder, a woman was sitting on a bundle in a doorway with a baby asleep against her chest. She was young, and her face was white down one side, the left, from brow to chin, the way Vane's was, the way Vesper's was. Leaned into the light, once. Seraphina saw her see the priest. She saw the woman's arms tighten round the baby.

Ignatius Crane stopped at the ladder-head.

He stood there, a yard from her, in the shade of the walkway. He looked down at her and the baby. He did not say anything. Seraphina, on the ladder below him, saw his good hand close on the rail.

Then the woman said, very quietly, in the murmur of the Narrow, "*Are you in the shade?*"

The priest did not move.

Seraphina knew what the answer was. She had learned it in Ashfall, in a black room on the Keel Docks, listening to the Tiers through the wall. It was the oldest thing the Tiers had, and it was seven years old. You said it at a door, in a street, at a ladder-head, to anyone. You said it the way the Reach said *does it ring*. *Are you in the shade.* Are you well. Are you safe. Are you one of us. And the answer was the same every time, for everyone, the only answer, because in the Tiers it was always true.

*I'm in yours.*

Ignatius Crane opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

The woman looked up at him for a long moment with her white half-face and her dark half-face. Then she looked down at the baby against her chest, and moved her arm a little, so that its sleeping head was in her shadow and not in his, and did not ask again.

He went on up.

---

Brenna Corliss's chandlery hung off the underside of the second tier on four chains, with a sign over the door that said ROPE in black on black, so that you had to know it was there to read it. Inside it smelled of tar and hemp and the black bitter drink the Tiers brewed out of something that was not coffee. The shutters were closed. One lamp burned on the counter, turned down to a bead.

There was no rope. That was the first thing Seraphina saw. In Ashfall the walls had been hung from floor to ceiling with coils of it, every weight and lay, and bundles of oakum, and spools of tarred twine. The walls were bare. There were pale shapes on the black paint where the coils had hung.

Brenna Corliss was sitting at the counter with a slate.

She was a big woman of sixty, older than Seraphina remembered, with one good eye and one milk-white one. The right half of her face was white and thin as candle-wax, from a morning on the fourth tier in 412 when she had turned her head the wrong way, and she kept that side turned always to the wall. She did not get up. She looked at them as they came in under the low door, one at a time, the Aegis last, on its knees, crawling, because it would not fit any other way. She looked at Lyra. At the slate. At Lyra's throat.

"I heard," she said. "Cantor. About the voice. I'm sorry."

Lyra nodded.

"I heard you broke it on Lumina," said Brenna Corliss, "under two hundred of them, and stopped the Light coming home." She looked at Lyra a moment longer with her good eye. "There's people in the Tiers who'll say that's why. All this. That you sent it back to sleep and it didn't like being sent. I'll tell you that now, so it doesn't come at you sideways." She turned her slate face down on the counter. "I don't say it. I've done the sums. It's nothing to do with you. It started on Nullday, the eighteenth, at the dead hour, and you were four hundred miles off."

"What started," said Seraphina.

Brenna Corliss looked at her. At the shape on her back.

"You'll know better than me," she said. "Knight. It'll have told you."

---

She did not answer. Brenna Corliss did not wait for her to.

"Nullday night," she said. "The dead hour. I was up. I'm always up. And the Basilica's bell rang. Not the hour. It rang once. On its own, the Altar sisters said after, with no hand on the rope. The whole Tiers woke up. And then the line came down." She turned the slate face up again and pushed it across the counter. "I've kept it. Every bell since. Look."

Seraphina looked. It was a column of figures in a big square hand, a tier-mark and an hour against each.

*Nullday, dead hr — 7th to 6th.*
*Rootday, 3rd bell — 6th half.*
*Hearthday, dawn — 5th.*
*Massday, noon — 5th half.*
*Massday, dead hr — 4th.*
*Stasisday, dawn — 4th half.*

"It drops at dawn," said Brenna Corliss. "Mostly. A step at a time. And it drops at the dead hour. Like something breathing. In, out. Every time it breathes out the line comes down." She put a big finger on the last figure. "That was this morning. Half the fourth. There were four hundred people living on the fourth when Ashfall ended, Knight. We'd moved three hundred and sixty down by sunrise. We'd drilled it. We've drilled it seven years. The other forty—" She stopped. "Some wouldn't. Old ones. And some were asleep, and you can't drill being asleep."

Nobody said anything.

"At that rate," said the priest, from the door, very quietly.

Brenna Corliss looked at him for the first time. She looked at him for a long while, with the good eye and the white one.

"At that rate," she said, "the line passes the Keel Docks on Nullday. The dead hour. Four days." She turned the slate face down again. "And then there's no shade on the city at all, Inquisitor. None. Not under it, not inside it, not anywhere. There's the city's shadow on the gulf, going away from the Spire, and that's all the shade there is, and it's two thousand feet down."

"And the Church," said Seraphina.

Brenna Corliss laughed. It was not a laugh. It was a short hard breath out through the nose.

"Read the ladder-head," she said.

---

It was pasted on the black iron of the lamp-post opposite the chandlery door, where every proclamation in the Tiers was pasted. It was printed in red. Red was the one colour that still showed in the shade. Seraphina read it through the crack of the door with the priest at her shoulder, and Lyra under her arm, and Solenne behind them all in the dark of the shop, not reading it, listening to them read.

*BY THE LIGHT'S GRACE, IN COUNCIL, AT THE BASILICA OF THE PURE NOTE, MASSDAY THE TWENTY-FIRST OF FROSTMONTH.*

*My children.*

*You will have seen the Light coming down to us these four nights, and you will be afraid. Do not be. I have asked it every night, on my knees, what it wants of us, and it has answered me. It is not a judgement. It is a homecoming. The Light is coming down the chains to find its own city, as a father comes down the stair to find his children in the dark. Those who are in its city when it comes will be held.*

*Therefore let no one go down.*

*Removal from the chains is not required. The descent of the Light is to be received. Any vessel conveying souls from the chains is to be taken in the Light's name. Discomfort is not anticipated.*

*Severin, by the Light's grace.*

---

She read it twice.

The first part was his. She would have known it anywhere. She had heard him say *my children* from the Altar of the nave every Dawnday of her life, with his old hands raised and his face shining. She had heard him say *do not be afraid* to a girl of sixteen in white plate on the morning before the three hundredth sunrise, sitting on the floor of the knights' hall at the top of House Aubade with his legs crossed like a boy. He had said it so kindly that she had believed him. *As a father comes down the stair to find his children in the dark.* That was his. That was exactly how he talked.

The second part was not.

She did not know how she knew it. She read it again. *Removal from the chains is not required. The descent of the Light is to be received.* There was nothing wrong with it. It was the kind of sentence a proclamation was supposed to have in it. And she read it a third time, in the crack of the door, with the red letters dark on the black iron, and felt it the way you feel a step that is not there.

There was nobody in it.

Severin Ashe had never written a sentence in his life without a person in it. *My children. My son. My dear. You mustn't get up.* He put people into sentences the way he sat on the floor, so as not to stand over them. And the second half of the proclamation had nobody in it at all. Nobody removed anybody. Nobody received anything. Nobody would be hurt, because nobody was there to hurt or be hurt. *Discomfort is not anticipated.* By whom? In whom?

"That's not his," said the priest.

She turned her head.

Ignatius Crane was reading it over her shoulder with his white hand flat on the doorframe. He read it the way he had read heretics' letters once, in the Inquisition's black rooms, with his lips very slightly moving.

"The end," he said. "That isn't Severin. That's the Office." He did not look away from it. "I was at the Organum for two years before I took orders. A clerk. In the Arbiterium's copying-room. They train it into you there. You learn it before you learn where the privy is. No verb with a person at the end of it. Nobody is ever *done to*. Things are processed. Things are received." He was quiet for a moment. "*Discomfort is not anticipated.* I've written that sentence. On a writ. I don't remember whose."

"Then who—"

"I don't know." He took his hand off the doorframe. "But somebody in that house writes like a clerk of the Organum, Knight, and puts it in the Patriarch's mouth, and he doesn't hear the difference." He looked down at his white hand. "He never did. He never heard the difference in anything."

She looked at him. He did not look at her. He went back into the shop.

She stood in the door a moment longer, reading the red words on the iron. *As a father comes down the stair.* Then she shut it.

---

"Every ship that takes a soul off the chains," said Brenna Corliss, "is to be taken in the Light's name. You'll know what that means better than me, Inquisitor." She did not wait for the priest to answer. "The second chapter came home on Hearthday. From the Coast. Two hundred of them, and their censers, and the smell of Port Cantabile still on them. They're at the Basilica now, round the tower, holding it in the white with glass-fire in shifts. And on Massday the Basilica sent word down to the chime-house that any ship that drops a hawser off the Keel Docks with a soul aboard gets a censer through its envelope."

"Can they reach the docks?" said Seraphina. "From the ninth?"

"In glass-fire they can reach anywhere," said Brenna Corliss. "You know that. You saw them come down the Pilgrim Chain in their little bubbles of shade." She turned her white side to the wall. "And they don't need to reach the docks. They only need to reach the chime-house. You burn the chime-house, nobody rings. Nobody rings, nobody goes."

"So the ships can't go."

"Some can. Some will. At the end, when it doesn't matter what the Church says because there's no Church left to say it." Brenna Corliss pulled her slate back to her across the counter. "But it doesn't matter, Knight. That's the thing I need you to understand before you go up those ladders tonight and do whatever you're going to do. The ships were never the plan. There's eleven cradles and forty hulls lashed to them and the whole lot of it can lift two thousand souls if they stand on each other's shoulders. There's eighteen thousand six hundred and forty of us. We counted Nullday." She looked at Lyra. "The ships are for the old and the sick and the ones too small to climb. That's all. That was always all."

"Then how," said Seraphina.

Brenna Corliss looked at her for a long time with her one good eye.

"Come and look at the rope," she said.

---

There was no rope in the chandlery because the rope had gone down.

She took them out through the back of the shop, through a door Seraphina would not have seen, into a passage cut through the black stone of the second tier, and down. Iron rungs. A shaft, like a well, going down through the bottom of the city, with the cold coming up it and the sound of the gulf, the long hollow sigh of two thousand feet of nothing. The Aegis could not follow. It knelt at the top of the shaft with its helm bowed and hummed. Solenne came down after them without a rung, her talons on the stone, her broken wings folded tight.

The shaft came out under the Keel Docks.

Below the cradles. Below everything. On a narrow iron gallery bolted to the very tip of the city's cone, the last thing Sol-Luminary had, the point of the wasps' nest, with the black underside of the docks over their heads and the swinging iron baskets and the hulls. And below the gallery, the gulf.

And going down into the gulf, chains.

Seraphina counted them. Nine. They hung from great iron eyes set into the stone of the city's tip, in a ring, nine of them, a yard apart. And they went down. Straight down. Not slanting, like the city's chains, up to the Spire. Plumb. Black links each a little longer than a man's forearm, the iron of them old and scabbed and mended and mended again, going down and down into the shade under the city until the eye lost them in the grey. And knotted to every link, on either side, a rope. Tarred hemp. A hand-line. So that the chain was a ladder, and a long one, the longest ladder in the world, with rope rungs knotted to iron.

"Two thousand and eleven feet," said Brenna Corliss. "To the scree at the bottom. Each one." She put her big hand on the iron eye of the nearest. "We started the first in the spring of '13. Out of the links off the dead tiers. The Bleach doesn't take iron, not quickly. We'd go up to the line at night and break it out of the gratings and the stair-rails and the gutted houses, and carry it down, and weld it. A link a night, the first year." She was quiet for a moment. "My husband did the first forty feet. He was a smith. He went up to the line for a grating on the fifth in '14 and leaned out too far."

Nobody said anything.

"Nine," said Brenna Corliss. "One under each of the city's nine. So that the city would have as many ways down as it had ways of hanging. Somebody said that, the first year. I don't remember who. It stuck." She looked down the nearest chain into the grey. "We finished the ninth in Harvestmonth. Just before you came. We drilled it on Driftdays. Every Driftday for seven years, the children, a tier at a time, down the first hundred feet and back. In the dark. In each other's shade." She turned and looked at Seraphina. "They're good at it. You'll see. You've never seen anything like it."

"Then why haven't they gone?"

Brenna Corliss's face did not change.

"Because the shade at the bottom is the city's shadow," she said. "On the gulf. Going away from the Spire, swinging with the sun, like you saw in Ashfall. That's the only shade there is once you're off the chains. A wedge of it, across the scree and up the north side of the gulf to the pass, and it moves. Eighteen thousand people going down nine ladders, a man a yard, take the whole of a night and most of a day, and at the bottom they've to walk out in that wedge, in a long line, in each other's shade, keeping to it as it swings." She shut her good eye. "We've drilled the chains. You can't drill the bottom. You only get to do the bottom once."

"And the Church—"

"The Church wants us held," said Brenna Corliss. "The Light's coming home. And half the Tiers believe it, Knight. That's what you're not hearing. Half of them are sitting on their bundles on the walkways because they're waiting for me to say go. And the other half are sitting on their bundles because they've read the ladder-head and they're waiting for him to say *stay*." She opened her eye. "He's never once been wrong about the Light, as far as they're concerned. He's the only man in Sol-Luminary who can walk in the white."

"He can't," said Seraphina. "He's never—"

She stopped.

"No," said Brenna Corliss. "I know. Glass-fire and choirs. But they don't know that." She looked at the shape on Seraphina's back. "They know the Patriarch's house walks in the white, and they know the Patriarch's knight does, and they've seen you do it. Ashfall. On the Pilgrim Chain. Coming down out of the white in front of the Cantor with your face set like a coin." She was quiet. "I don't want you to fight the Church, Knight. I've no use for that. I want you to go up to the Basilica, before Nullday, and tell him to say *go*."

---

Seraphina did not answer for a long time.

She stood on the iron gallery at the tip of the city, under the Keel Docks, with the nine chains going down beside her into the grey and the Blade on her back asking, very faintly, into her spine. She thought of the knights' hall at the top of House Aubade. A boy's legs crossed on the boards. *Do not be afraid.* She thought of the white stone of the Altar of Lumina in Ashfall, and an old man on his knees beside the Blade where she had dropped it, his hands held out over it, not quite touching. *Why, child? It was answering.*

She had told him why. She had said it standing over him, with her sister's wrist in her hand and her sister's broken wing dragging on the stone.

*Because it was tired, Father. And so was she.*

She had not seen him since.

"I'll go," she said.

Brenna Corliss nodded. She did not say thank you. In the Tiers, Seraphina remembered, you did not thank anybody for going into the light. It was considered unlucky. It was considered the kind of thing you said to someone you did not expect to see again.

"Not tonight," said a voice behind them.

---

Solenne was crouched on the rail of the iron gallery with her talons hooked over it and her broken wings half-open for balance, over the drop, the way she had crouched in the nest in Ashfall. She had not spoken since the chandlery. She had not spoken, Seraphina realised, since Oakhaven, since the fallen oak and the slab. *It's just singing.* She had come north on the Greenroot road and onto the *Small Hours* in silence and stood under the black canvas for three days with her arms wrapped round herself and her face turned toward the Heights.

She was looking up now. Not at the city. Past it. At the top of the cone, a thousand feet above them, where the white began and the rim was, and the anchor-houses, and the chains going up into the glare.

"Not tonight," said Solenne again, in her two voices, the copper and the well. "Tonight I'm going up to the sixth."

Brenna Corliss looked at her.

"Your angels," she said. Not unkindly. "Your ten."

Solenne did not answer.

"We fed them," said Brenna Corliss. "After you went. Every night. The Tiers did. We'd carry it up to the line, like we always had, and leave it on the stone, and in the morning it'd be gone, and we'd know they'd come down for it in the dark." She was quiet for a moment. "And then the line started coming down. And every night we'd leave it lower. A tier lower. Further from them. And they'd have to come further down through the white to get it." She looked at the chains. "We've not been up since Rootday. There's no up any more, Lady. The line's at the fourth. Last night we left it on the fourth, half-way across, on a step. This morning it was still there."

Solenne's talons tightened on the rail.

"Then I'm late," she said.

She looked down. Not at Brenna. At her sister. For the first time since Oakhaven, Seraphina saw her sister look at her, straight, with the two lines cut down from her eyes to her jaw and the copper glinting at her throat.

"I left them," said Solenne. "In Ashfall. I went down the chain with you, Sera, and I left ten of them in the chain's shadow at the top of the world, and I told myself I'd come back for them. And I went to the Shelf, and the Organum, and Oakhaven, and I never came back." Her voice was very quiet. "They're mine. They sang under me for seven years. They held the note because I held it. They're mine the way he's mine." She did not look round at the priest. She did not need to. "I'm going up tonight. I'd like you to come. I can't carry them down on my own with one wing. You've got the bone. The light goes round you."

Seraphina looked at her.

"Yes," she said.

Solenne nodded. Then, without turning her head, without changing the tone of her two voices at all, she said, "And you."

Behind them, at the foot of the iron rungs, Ignatius Crane went still.

"You'll come," said Solenne Aubade. Not to him. To the gulf. To the nine chains going down into the grey. "You'll carry the food. And when we get there you'll look at them." A pause. "I want you to look at them, Ignatius. That's all. That's all I want from you. I want you to stand where they can see you and look."

It was the first time she had said his name since Lumina.

The priest did not answer. Seraphina saw his white hand close, slowly, at his side, and open again. Then he nodded, once, to her sister's back.

---

Lyra wrote it on the back of the slate, in the chandlery, while they were getting the food together. She held it up to Seraphina in the light of the one lamp.

*I SHOULD COME.*

"No," said Seraphina.

*I CAN WHISTLE. IF THEY ARE WIRED, A NOTE A HAIR FLAT—*

"No." She put her hand over the slate, gently, so that she would not have to read the rest. "Cantor. Look at your hand."

Lyra looked at it. The burned one, in its linen, against Hetty Bram's coat.

"Look at your mouth," said Seraphina.

Lyra did not move.

"There's a woman on the second tier," said Seraphina, "with a baby, who moved her arm so the priest's shadow wouldn't fall on it. And there's eighteen thousand six hundred and forty people sitting on bundles on the walkways waiting for somebody to say *go*. And when somebody does, Brenna Corliss is going to need someone at the top of those chains who can make eighteen thousand people move in time without saying a word." She took her hand away. "I watched you do it at Oakhaven. With your hand. With four voices that weren't yours. You're the only one of us who can do it, and you can't do it if you've whistled your throat out at the top of the world tonight for seven people who'll be just as safe with my shadow."

Lyra looked at her a long time.

Then she wiped the slate with her sleeve and wrote something else, and held it up.

*BRING THEM DOWN.*

"I will."

*ALL OF THEM.*

Seraphina did not answer that. She did not know how many there were.

Lyra seemed to understand. She looked at Seraphina's face, and then she reached out with her good hand and touched, very lightly, the hilt of the Blade where it came up over Seraphina's shoulder under the borrowed coat. As if she knew. As if she could feel it too, through her fingertips, going up at the end.

Then she took her hand away and went to help Ignatius with the bread.

---

They went up at the dead hour, the three of them, with a sack of black bread and a skin of water and four black blankets, and the Blade.

The Tiers watched them go. Seraphina felt it the whole way up, through the hanging houses, on the ladders, on the swaying walkways, in the dark: the faces in the doorways turning to follow. Not the priest, this time. Her. The shape on her back. Nobody said anything. On the third tier, at a ladder-head, an old man in a black blanket reached out as she passed and touched the hem of her borrowed coat, the way you touch the hem of a priest's robe in a procession. She did not stop.

The line was at the fourth.

She had seen it in Ashfall from the inside, from the Glassmarket, from the white streets of the top tier at the dead hour. She had never come up to it from below in the dark, the way the Tiers did, with offerings. She did now. The ladder came up through the underside of the fourth tier into a street. A real street, the right way up, the first right-way-up street she had seen since the Keel Docks. Cobbled. With the backs of houses on one side, black-shuttered, and on the other a parapet, and the gulf. And half-way along it, across it, slanting, from the parapet to the house-walls, straight as a ruled line, the edge of the white.

On this side, dark. The ordinary dark of a winter night at the top of the world, with frost on the cobbles and the stars out over the gulf.

On that side, light.

Flat. Sourceless. White. Coming from nowhere, from the stone itself and the air and the sky, with no shadows in it that pointed down. Every shadow on that side of the line lay long and thin and black across the cobbles, pointing the same way: away from the Spire, out and down the city's flank. As if the Spire were a sun that had never set.

On the step of a house, a yard inside the white, there was a cloth bundle. Black, once. Seraphina could see what it had been. It had gone grey. It had gone thin. Beside it, a tin cup, white. A candle-end, white. A pear, white all through, translucent, so that she could see the shadow of the core in it, like the bones in the priest's hand.

Last night's. Left for the ten. Not taken.

Beside the pear, a child's drawing, weighted with a stone. A black shape with wings. A sun, crossed out. It had gone so thin she could see the cobbles through it.

Solenne stopped at the line and looked at it for a long time.

Then she stepped over.

---

The light did not take her. Seraphina had known it would not. She had watched it fail to, in Ashfall, on the Altar, on the chain. *The light doesn't know what I am any more.* Solenne went into the white with her broken wings half-open and her bare feet on the frosted cobbles, and the light bent round the edges of the black plates of her wings, gold, the way water bends round a stone, and could not get in. Her shadow lay out behind her long and black toward the gulf.

Seraphina drew the Blade.

She had not drawn it since the Organum. She had not needed to. It came out of the white leather into her hand with a long soft sound like a breath drawn in, and it was not metal and not glass. Translucent, and gold, and warm, a splinter of something alive. It had been warm in her hand every time she had held it since she was sixteen.

It was asking.

She felt it go into her hand. Not faint, now. Not the ghost of a ring in the bones of her wrist. Strong, the way the sound of a bell is strong when you stand under it. Out of the Blade into her palm, into her arm, into her teeth. Not words. A shape. Going up at the end.

She stepped over the line.

The white came at her and went round her. She felt it go. She had felt it on the Altar and on the chain, and it was the same, and it was not the same. The Blade drank. The light in the street, the sourceless flat white of the Bleach, bent in toward the blade from every side and went into it, the way water goes into sand. And it parted round her as it went, so that she stood in the white street in a small moving space of ordinary dark, with her shadow falling the wrong way, forward, toward the Spire, long and black on the cobbles in front of her.

Behind her, the priest stood at the line with the sack on his shoulder.

He did not step over. He stood on the dark side, in the frost, with his white hand at his side, and looked at the white street, and at the grey bundle on the step, and at the pear.

"Walk in my shadow," said Seraphina.

He looked at her.

"It falls forward," she said. "You'll have to go ahead of me. Not behind." She held the Blade up a little. The shadow on the cobbles moved. "Go in front, Father. I'll keep it on you."

He had not been her father since she was sixteen. He had been her confessor. She had called him Father on her knees in a black box in the Basilica every Memoryday for ten years and told him every sin she could think of, and he had told her, in his deep nave voice, that the Light forgave it. She had not called him that since the Foundry. She did not know why she had now.

Ignatius Crane stepped over the line, into her shadow, in front of her. And they went up.

---

She would not remember most of the climb.

She would remember the stair. The great public stair of the city, that went up the south-west flank from the fourth tier to the rim in long turning flights, between walls, under arches, white. All of it white. White stone, white rails, the white dust of whatever had been carried down it and dropped. She had gone down it once in a procession at eleven years old, in a white dress, singing. She went up it now in the dead of a winter night with a god's bone in her hand and an Inquisitor in her shadow, three steps ahead of her, never looking back, and her sister ahead of them both, going up the stair on her talons and her hands like an animal, with her broken wing dragging.

She would remember the Basilica.

It stood off the stair on the ninth tier, across a square. The whole of it was in the white now, nave and dome and tower, the shade half and the light half alike. She could see the iron wall down the middle of it through the great west door, which stood open. And round the foot of the black tower and along the west front, in a ring, standing a yard apart, men in crimson, with censers.

The glass-fire burned white-gold in their cages, high, so bright she could not look at it. Each man stood in his own small bubble of ordinary shade. Between the bubbles, the white. And from inside the nave, through the open door, faint, steady, unbroken, a single note.

Five hundred and twenty-eight. The Pure Note. Held.

She did not stop. She did not look. *The guard looks at the Spire.* She went up the stair past the end of the square with the Blade drinking and the priest in her shadow and her face set, and she heard the note go on behind her, held, held, without a breath in it, until the turn of the stair took it away.

And she would remember that the priest did not look either. That he went past the end of the square three steps ahead of her, past the men in crimson and the fire he had carried since he was a boy, and did not turn his head.

She would remember thinking that it was the first time she had ever seen him not look at fire.

---

The sixth anchor-house stood on the very rim of the city, on the south-west side, nearest the Spire.

A roofless ring of broken wall forty feet across, built out over the drop on stone corbels. The wall stood twenty feet high and jagged along its top. In the floor, a ring of iron ten feet across, and coming down into it through the open top of the tower, at its long slant, out of the glare above, the sixth chain. A kilometre of black link going up to the Spire.

It was the only black thing at the top of the world. The light had not taken it. It lay across the white like a line drawn in ink. And under it, on the floor of the anchor-house, its shadow. A long black band across the stone, from the iron ring to the wall, the width of a link, pointing away from the Spire.

The nest was in the shadow.

Seraphina saw it from the doorway, as she had seen it in Ashfall. Choir-robes, white, folded and piled. Scraps of gold leaf peeled off the dead domes. Black blankets, a great many of them, the Tiers' offerings, carried up night after night from the line and laid down here in the chain's shadow. A nest built the way a bird builds, out of whatever could be carried.

It had been full, in Ashfall. Solenne in the middle of it with her wings folded. Ten bleached people round her, kneeling, in the shadow of the chain that held up the city.

There were people in it now. She counted. She could count. The Blade held the light off her, and in the space it made she could think.

Seven.

---

They were lying down. All seven. In the chain's shadow, close together, on the black blankets and the white robes, the way the Tiers' children slept in their hammocks under the walkways, in rows. Thin. So thin. Seven years thin, and seven weeks more, and the light coming through them a little at the edges the way it came through the priest's hand. Copper glinting at their throats.

And they were singing.

She had not heard it from the doorway. She heard it now. Very soft. Lying on their backs in the chain's shadow with their eyes open, all seven, singing. One note. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Not the Pure Note. Not held, not flat and unbroken like the note in the nave. It rose. Slowly, from the bottom of the breath, the way the deck had risen under Mags's palm on the Rust Coast, the way Vane had told them on the Shelf. And at the top, at the end of the breath, it lifted. A small tilt upward.

A question.

And the Blade in her hand answered it. Not answered. *Joined* it. She felt it go up through her arm. The same shape. The same lift at the end. The Blade, and the seven, and somewhere above them at the end of a kilometre of chain, the thing the Blade had been cut from, all asking the same thing in the same voice at once.

Solenne went into the shadow on her knees.

---

The thin one was at the end of the row. Seraphina knew her. The translucent one, the one who could speak a word to a note. She had come across the floor of this anchor-house in Ashfall to the edge of the nest and fought the wire for three notes. *We are sorry.*

She turned her head on the blanket. Slowly. She looked at Solenne kneeling over her.

She stopped singing.

The other six did not. The question went on, soft, rising, lifting at the top. But the thin one at the end of the row lay with her mouth shut and her eyes on Solenne Aubade's face and fought the wire. Seraphina saw it. She saw the cords stand out in the thin white neck, round the copper. She saw it take everything the woman had.

A note.

*Lead.*

"Yes," said Solenne. Her copper voice was in pieces. "Yes. I'm here. I'm late. I'm sorry. Marit, I'm sorry, I'm so—"

Another note. Higher. Thinner.

*Three —*

*— sat —*

*— down.*

"I know," said Solenne. "I counted. I know."

Seraphina had not seen the three. She saw them now. At the far end of the nest, in the shadow, laid out side by side under a white robe, with their hands folded. Somebody had folded them. Somebody in this nest, with no voice and copper in her throat and seven weeks of the cold, had laid three of her choir out under a robe in the chain's shadow at the top of the world and folded their hands. And then lain down beside the others and gone on singing.

The thin woman fought the wire again.

*It —*

*— asks.*

Solenne put her hand on the thin white forehead.

"I know," she said. "I hear it. I always heard it."

*Since —*

*— Null —*

*— day.*

*We —*

She stopped. She could not find the next note. Seraphina watched her try. Watched the cords in her neck, the copper, the breath. Watched the woman's eyes go to the chain overhead, going up into the white, and back to Solenne.

*— can't —*

*— not —*

The last note did not come. Her mouth stayed open on it. And then she shut it, and her face did a thing that Seraphina would carry for the rest of her life: it went apologetic. Like a child who has been told to say something to a grown-up and cannot remember the words.

And she opened her mouth and began to sing the question again, with the others. Rising. Lifting at the end. Because the wire would not let her do anything else for long, and because, Seraphina understood, it was the only thing left in the whole of the white city that anybody was saying to the god, and somebody had to.

---

Solenne turned her head.

The priest was standing in the doorway of the anchor-house. He had stopped there when Seraphina went in, and he had not come further. He stood in the doorway with the sack of bread on his shoulder, on the line between the doorway's shadow and the white, with the long black shadow of the doorframe lying across his boots. He was looking at the floor.

"Ignatius," said Solenne.

He did not look up.

"Come here."

He came. Seraphina moved with the Blade so that her shadow was on him, and he came across the floor of the anchor-house in it, into the chain's shadow, to the edge of the nest. He stood there with the sack on his shoulder. He looked at the floor.

"No," said Solenne. "Look."

He looked.

Seraphina watched him do it. She stood at the edge of the chain's shadow with the Blade up and the light going into it and parting round her, and watched Ignatius Crane look at seven people lying on their backs in a nest of robes and blankets with copper in their throats, singing a god's question to a chain. Thin as paper. Light coming through them at the edges. And three more laid out under a white robe at the far end with their hands folded.

He had been in the Foundry on the three hundredth morning. In the gallery, with the censer, behind her. She had never looked round. She had stood in the gallery in white plate and looked at the Spire, because the guard looks at the Spire. And behind her, a man of thirty-three with a voice built for a nave had seen the light turn and the throats go and had shouted *Purify the Rite*, and lifted his fire, and brought it down on the risers.

On her sister.

She had never seen his face when he did it. She had never looked.

She saw his face now.

It did not do anything. That was what she would remember. She had thought it would break. She had seen men's faces break, in the Inquisition's black rooms, when she stood guard at the door. It did not. It stayed exactly as it was, thin and dark under the eyes, with the white streak up one side of it from the Pilgrim Chain. And he looked. He did not look away. He looked at each of them. One at a time. Down the row. All seven. And then at the robe at the far end, and the three shapes under it, and the folded hands.

He looked for a very long time.

"Seraphina," said her sister, without taking her eyes off him. "Is his fire coming back?"

Seraphina looked at him. At the sack on his shoulder. At the white hand. There was nothing in it. Not a flicker. Not the smell of honey and struck flint that had come off him all her childhood, in the confessional, through the grille.

"No," she said.

"Good," said Solenne. Very quietly. In the void half of her voice, the half that was like wind over the mouth of a well. "Then he can do it again tomorrow."

The priest looked.

After a while, he took the sack off his shoulder and knelt down at the edge of the nest and began, with his one good hand, to break the bread.

---

The thin woman would not eat. None of them would. They could not stop singing long enough to eat. Ignatius broke the bread into pieces the size of a thumbnail and held them, one at a time, at the edge of each mouth, at the bottom of each breath, in the space between one question and the next, and waited. Sometimes it went in. Mostly it did not. He did not stop. He went down the row on his knees with the bread and the water-skin and did it, each of them, over and over, the way he had stood over Hetty Bram's stove on the *Equity* with a spoon. He did not speak. He did not look away from them while he did it. Not once.

Solenne sat in the middle of the nest with her broken wings round her and watched him.

Seraphina stood at the edge of the shadow with the Blade.

She could not put it down. She had understood that as soon as she came through the doorway. The chain's shadow was not what it had been in Ashfall. It was a band of black across the floor the width of a link, and in Ashfall that had been enough; ten of them had lived in it for seven weeks. But it was not black now. She could see that, with the Blade drinking beside her. It was grey. A dark grey, like ash, like a winter afternoon. And at its edges, very slowly, the grey was going paler.

The chain's shadow was thinning.

*Since Nullday.*

She watched it. She watched the edge of the grey on the stone by the thin woman's shoulder go a shade lighter while she watched, the way a coal goes lighter when the fire goes out of it. The Light was coming down. Not only the line on the city's flank. Everywhere. Into the shadow of the chain that held it up. Into everything. Breathing out.

She held the Blade, and her own shadow lay across the nest from her feet, black, the only black thing on the floor of the anchor-house. Black, and falling the wrong way. Toward the Spire. Across the row of the singing seven, and her sister, and the priest on his knees with the bread.

She did not put it down.

---

The dawn came at the seventh hour.

She knew it before she saw it, because she had been taught to. Every child in the Basilica school knew the dawn. You knew it in the dark by the bells. And at the seventh hour, on the morning of Dawnday, the twenty-third of Frostmonth, the Basilica's bell rang the dawn office down through the white city from the black tower on the ninth tier, as it had rung it every Dawnday morning for four hundred years. One deep slow bronze note. And under it, through the stone, faint, she heard the nine in the choir-stalls change. Not break. Change. The note in the nave lifted, very slightly, as it had always lifted at the dawn office, to begin the morning hymn.

*Light of the first note and the last.*

She knew every word. She had sung it at six.

And the Blade in her hand went up at the end so hard that she cried out.

She had not cried out in seven years. She heard herself do it, a short sound, in her own voice, the warm low mezzo that carried in a city of whisperers. She did not care. She could not have stopped it. It was not a shape any more. It was not a ring in the bones. It came up out of the Blade through her hand and her arm into the middle of her and it was *words*, the way the old man's voice was words to Kaelen through the glass. The first words she had ever heard from the god whose bone she had carried since she was sixteen. The god she had guarded for three hundred sunrises without once looking at the people it was eating.

*Is it you?*

*Have you come?*

And it went up at the end. Like a child at a door. Like a child at the top of the stair who has heard the bell for the morning office ring every Dawnday for four hundred years and come down every time to find one note, held, and nobody singing it the rest of the song.

*Is it the night?*

---

She understood it then. All of it. Standing in the white at the top of the world with the god's bone in her hand and the Basilica's bell dying away below her.

The dawn office. Every Dawnday since the Church was the Church. The Sky Choirs going up the Pilgrim Chain in the dark, singing, and the best treble of the Basilica school on the Vessel's stone at Lumina with the Eye behind her, singing the first line alone. She had thought it was worship. Everyone had. It was a visit. Somebody coming every eighth day to the god of Light to sing to it, so that it would know it was heard. And the Church had kept the day, and kept the hour, and kept the bell, and dropped every note of the song but one.

And held that one, every morning, for four hundred years, at a god that was waiting for somebody to come down the stair. And never once let it hear anything else.

*The Light wants to be held.* She had learned it at six. *Q. What does the Light want?*

It did not want to be held. It wanted to know who was there.

She stood with the Blade up and wept, and did not lower it.

---

And then the light breathed out.

She felt it go. Brenna Corliss had said it, on the slate, in the chandlery, in her big square hand. *It drops at dawn. Like something breathing. Every time it breathes out the line comes down.* She felt it go down through the white city under her feet, a long slow outward breath through every stone, and she felt the chain's shadow go with it. She saw it. The grey band across the floor of the anchor-house went pale. Paler. Ash, and then the colour of an old bone, and then the colour of the white around it, and then it was not there. The chain was still there, black, overhead, going up. It threw no shadow at all.

There was no shade on the floor of the sixth anchor-house except hers.

She saw the thin woman at the end of the row, Marit, lying on her back, singing, go paler. Saw the edges of her hand on the blanket go clear. Saw the next one. Saw the light come down onto the nest out of nowhere and everywhere, flat and white and sourceless, with nothing in its way.

"*Sera*—"

She moved.

She did not decide to. She was a knight of the Sun-Altar and she had been trained since she was fifteen by a one-eyed old woman who had guarded the Altar for forty years, and the first thing she had been taught, before the Blade, before the plate, before anything, was where to stand. *The guard stands between the Spire and the Choir. The guard looks at the Spire. The guard does not look at the Choir.*

She stepped into the nest. Over the priest's legs, over the bread. She stood on the white robes in the middle of it, with the Blade up, and turned.

Her shadow fell toward the light. It always had. That was the thing about the bone: the light went round you, and your shadow went the wrong way, forward, toward the Spire. So to put it over the nest, over the seven, over her sister and the priest, she had to stand with them between her and the Spire. She had to stand with her back to the gulf and her face to the Spire, and the choir between.

She had to look at them.

She did. She stood in the middle of the nest with the Blade held up in both hands, drinking, and the white going into it and parting round her, and her shadow lying out in front of her, black, long, across seven people on their backs in the robes, and her sister on her knees, and the priest with the bread. And beyond them, through the jagged top of the anchor-house wall, the Spire. White. A mile high. Cracked. Asking.

The guard looks at the Spire.

She looked at the Choir.

She looked at them, in her shadow, and the Spire stood up behind them where she could not help but see it, and she did not look away from either.

---

It went on for a long time. The breath out. Longer than any she had felt. The Blade burned in her hands. It drank and drank, and she felt the light going round her, and she felt something else, under it. The bones of her own hands, going a little lighter under the skin. Like hands that had been in water too long. She did not lower it.

Solenne was looking up at her.

From her knees, in the middle of the nest, in her sister's shadow, with the copper at her throat and her face wet. And she said, in the copper half of her voice, the half that had sung the lead on the riser for three hundred sunrises while her little sister stood in the gallery and did not look:

"*Are you in the shade?*"

The oldest thing in the Tiers. Seven years old.

Seraphina had never once said the answer. You did not, in House Aubade. The high houses did not stand in anyone's shadow; they had not had to. And after the Fracture she had walked in the white with the bone, and nobody's shade had ever fallen on her, and she had never needed to say it.

"I'm in yours," she said.

It was not true. It was the wrong way round. Solenne was in hers.

Her sister knew that. Seraphina saw her know it. Saw the copper half and the dark half of her face move, not quite together.

"Yes," said Solenne Aubade. "You are."

---

The breath ended.

She felt it end. The long outward sigh through the stones of the city stopped, the way a breath stops at the bottom, and held. Overhead, very faint, the chain began to throw a shadow again. Grey. Thin. A band of ash across the floor of the anchor-house, not as wide as it had been. Thinner by a hand's breadth. The seven lay in it, and in hers, and sang. All seven. Paler than they had been. All seven.

She did not lower the Blade yet. She was not sure she could. Her arms had gone past aching into something else.

The priest got up off his knees. He came round the edge of the nest in her shadow and stood beside her and looked at what she was looking at: the seven, and Solenne. And then, after a while, past them. Over the jagged wall of the anchor-house, down. At the city's flank, going away below them in the dawn, white, tier on tier, to the edge of the white.

She saw him look. She looked too.

The line was at the third tier.

It lay across the city's flank far below them, hard and straight as a ruled line, slanting from left to right. Below it, the black: the hanging houses, the awnings, the ladders, two tiers and the Keel Docks and the raft of hulls and the iron tip of the cone with nine chains going down from it into the gulf. Above it, all the rest. The whole white city. The fourth tier, where she had stepped over a grey bundle and a white pear four hours ago, was in the white.

Somebody had been asleep on the fourth. Somebody always was. You cannot drill being asleep.

The priest was counting. She could see his lips move. He had been a clerk at the Organum before he took orders, he had said; he had been taught to count before he was taught where the privy was.

"Not Nullday," said Ignatius Crane. "Brenna's wrong. It's faster than that. It's not a tier a day any more. Yesterday at dawn it came down half a tier. This morning it came down more than a whole one." He looked at the line for a long time. "Two more breaths. Three. It'll be past the Keel Docks at the dead hour of Driftday night."

"Tomorrow night," said Seraphina.

"Tomorrow night."

She looked down at the city on its nine chains. At the black underside of it, so small now, with eighteen thousand people sitting on their bundles in it, waiting for somebody to say *go*. And at the Basilica's black tower on the ninth tier, white now, in its ring of gold fire, where somebody was waiting, she supposed, on his knees at the Altar of the nave, to be told what the Light wanted.

She would have to go and tell him.

Below her, across the city, faint, steady, the Pure Note went on. Held. Without a breath in it. And all round her, from the seven in the nest, from the Blade in her two hands, from the white Spire at the end of a kilometre of chain, the other thing. Rising. Going up at the end.

*Is it you?*

"No," said Seraphina Aubade, to the god whose bone she held. Gently. The first word she had ever said to it. "Not me. I'm sorry. But I'm here. I'm looking."

She did not know whether it heard her.

She held the Blade up until the sun came over the peaks, with her shadow lying across her sister and the priest and the seven, and did not look away.

## CHAPTER SIX
### The Correspondence

> *Light keep you.*
>
> — The greeting of the Choir-Church of the Pure Note. It has no answer. The faithful are taught that the Light gives the answer, and to wait for it

*The Basilica of the Pure Note, Sol-Luminary. Dawnday the twenty-third of Frostmonth, to Driftday the twenty-fourth.*

---

She heard the sun come up.

Nobody else did. She knew that. She had known it for seven years, since the dark, since the morning she came up out of the trench of nothing on her hands with the copper still in her throat and found that everything had a sound now. The sun had one. It came over the peaks to the east of the Spire at the eighth hour of Dawnday like a held breath let out through the teeth, a long thin hiss on the edge of hearing, and it went into the white city and the white city rang with it, very faint, every stone, the way a glass rings when you run a wet finger round its rim.

She heard her sister's arms shaking.

Seraphina stood in the middle of the nest with the Blade held up in both hands and her shadow lying over the seven, and she had stood like that since the dawn breath, and her arms were shaking. Not so that you could see. So that you could hear. A faint dry tremor in the joints of the elbow, a creak in the tendons of the wrist, under the sound of the Blade drinking. Solenne heard it the way she heard everything now: without wanting to, without being able to stop.

"Sera," she said. "Put it down."

"No."

"The chain's shadow is back. Listen to it."

Her sister did not listen. Her sister had never been able to. That was one of the things about Seraphina that Solenne had loved most, once, when they were girls on the rim, in their mother's house with the window that looked straight across at the Spire: that Sera could not hear the things Solenne heard, and so she was never afraid of them. But Solenne listened, and the chain overhead was humming again. The long low note of a thing that has been holding a weight for a hundred years and is tired. And its shadow on the floor of the anchor-house had come back with the hum, grey, thin, a band of ash a hand's breadth narrower than it had been at midnight.

"It's enough for them," said Solenne. "For the day. If they lie close. Put it down before your hands go."

Seraphina looked at her own hands on the hilt.

They were a shade lighter than they had been. Solenne could see it. Like hands that had been too long in water. The Light had taken the colour out of the knuckles, a little, while the bone drank.

Seraphina lowered the Blade.

It went down very slowly. Her arms did not want to bend. The light came back into the space round her as the Blade came down, not all at once, the way it had come back on the Pilgrim Chain in Ashfall. Gently. Grudgingly. And the seven lay in the grey of the chain's shadow, close, the way Solenne had told them to, and sang, and did not go any paler.

Seraphina sat down where she stood. In the middle of the nest, on the white robes, with the Blade across her knees and her shoulder against the thin woman's shoulder, Marit's. She shut her eyes.

Solenne listened to her breathe until she slept.

---

They spent the day in the chain's shadow.

There was nothing else to do. Nothing walked in the white by day. Not even the Church, not even in glass-fire. The Tiers had a saying for it, Brenna Corliss had told them in Ashfall: *the Light by night is a fire in the next room; the Light by day is the fire.* So they lay in the nest under the sixth chain, the eleven of them, on the robes and the blankets and the gold leaf, in a band of ash-grey shade a yard wide across the floor of a roofless tower at the top of the world, and waited for it to be night.

The seven sang.

They did not stop. Solenne had not expected them to. She lay among them with her broken wings half-open over as many of them as she could cover, and listened to them sing it, over and over, a breath at a time. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Rising from the bottom of the breath. Lifting at the top. The question. It came out of the copper in their throats as clearly as if the Spire itself were speaking through them, and she supposed it was. That was what the wire was for. That was what the Church had put it in them for, in the year after the Fracture, choir by choir, once the forty were gone: so that the Light would have throats that could not do anything but give it back its own note. The Church had meant them to hold it. Pure. Flat. *Yes.*

They had held it for seven years. And then on Nullday, at the dead hour, the god had begun to ask again, and the copper in them had done what copper does. It had given back what it was given.

She did not sing with them. She could have. The copper in her throat was the same copper, the first copper, the wire the Synod's surgeons had threaded through the vocal fold of a girl of eighteen so that she could hold a note for three hundred mornings. She could feel it in her throat now, under the skin, warm, wanting to. It had wanted to since Nullday. She had felt it on the Greenroot road, and in the ring of grey hands at Oakhaven, and on the deck of the *Small Hours* under the black canvas. It wanted to lift at the end.

She did not let it.

She lay in the nest and listened to the question, and to her sister breathing, and to the priest.

---

He sat at the edge of the chain's shadow with his back against the jagged wall and did not sleep.

She could hear him not sleeping. It had a sound. She had been listening to it since Lumina: on the *Kestrel*, on the Shelf, on the *Equity*, in the reed-hall at Reedside. A man lying in the dark with his eyes open, breathing slowly on purpose, so that nobody would know. She had held him by the collar for two months, and she had never once let him go far enough away that she could not hear it.

He had looked at them. She had made him. He had stood at the edge of the nest in the dark and looked at each of them, one at a time, all seven, and the three under the robe, for as long as she had made him look, and he had not turned his head away once. And then he had knelt down and broken the bread.

She had not told him to do that.

She lay with her wings over the seven and listened to him sit at the edge of the shadow in the white heat of the day, not sleeping. His good hand was in his lap. His white hand, the one the Light had taken on the morning of the Fracture, lay on the stone beside him. She could hear the white hand. It made a very small sound, so small she did not think even he could hear it: a faint high ring, like a fingernail on a glass, the sound everything taken by the Bleach made if you were close enough. It had made it for seven years. She had heard it through the grille of the confessional, on the morning before the three hundredth sunrise, when she went to him to be shriven before the Rite, and he had told her the Light forgave her everything.

She had been nineteen. She had told him she was afraid. He had said, in his deep nave voice, *the Light does not ask us to be unafraid, child, only to hold.*

She had held.

---

At the fourth hour of the afternoon the thin woman stopped singing and turned her head on the blanket and looked at her.

Solenne turned her head and looked back.

Marit Ferrand had sung second soprano in the Altar choir before the Fracture. Solenne remembered her. Not well. A thin girl of fifteen at the end of the second row on the riser, the row behind Solenne's, with a long neck and bitten nails, who had been too frightened to look at the Patriarch when he came along the riser on the first morning to bless them, and had looked instead at her own feet. She had not been one of the forty. She had been too young for the forty. The Church had wired her in the autumn of 413 with the cathedral choir, when the forty were gone, to give the Light throats.

She was twenty-two now. She looked fifty. She looked like a drawing of a woman in chalk on dark paper, that somebody had started to rub out.

She opened her mouth.

*Lead.*

"I'm here."

*Where —*

*— are —*

*— we —*

*— going.*

Solenne looked at her for a long time.

"Down," she said.

The thin woman's eyes went to the chain overhead, going up into the white, and came back.

*Can't.*

"You can. I'll lead. You'll follow. You always followed." Solenne put her hand on the thin white forehead. It was cool, and dry, and she could feel the copper humming under the skin of the throat, the question, wanting to go on. "You don't have to stop asking. You can ask all the way down. I don't mind. I'll be in front."

Marit looked at her.

*Will —*

*— it —*

*— hear?*

Solenne did not know what she meant. Then she did.

*Will it hear, if we go down. If we stop being up here, at the top, in the white, at the end of its chain. Will it hear anyone asking, if we go.*

She did not answer. She did not know.

She lay with her hand on Marit Ferrand's forehead and listened to the six beside her sing the question to the chain, and to the god at the top of the chain asking it back, and to her sister asleep with a piece of the god across her knees, and to the white hand on the stone, ringing. And she thought, for the first time since Oakhaven, the thing she had not let herself think on the fallen oak, or on the deck of the *Small Hours*, or anywhere.

*He's going to answer it. On the Longest Night. And when he does, it will stop asking, and nobody will ever have heard it but us.*

She had wanted him to be right. Since Lumina. Since the morning she heard the question and held the note and the god let go. She had wanted it so much that she could not sleep. Because if he was right, then she had done it a kindness, on the riser, that morning. Then holding had been mercy. Then eleven thousand and forty people had died of a mercy, and she had been its voice.

And she had stood in the ring of grey hands at Oakhaven in the snow and sung the fall below the fall for a hundred and eighty-seven people who had been asked, and watched the song go round twenty-five red chalk marks like water round a stone.

*He won't ask, will he.*

The thin woman's eyes had closed. She had gone back to singing.

Solenne took her hand away.

---

The sun went down at the fourth hour after noon, behind the peaks to the west, and she heard it go: the long hiss on the edge of hearing drawing back through the teeth, and the white city's faint glass-ring sinking with it to something lower, steadier, the note of the Bleach by night. The fire in the next room.

Seraphina woke when it went. Solenne heard her wake. She did not move for a while. Then she sat up, with the Blade across her knees, and looked at her hands.

"How long," she said.

"Till the dead hour, Brenna says. That's when it breathes. We want to be off the top before then."

"And then?"

"The Basilica," said Solenne. "It's on the way. It's in the white, and it's held. There's shade in there. Glass-fire and a choir." She paused. "And there's him. You said you'd go."

Seraphina did not answer for a moment.

"With the seven?" she said.

"With the seven." Solenne looked at the six on the blankets, singing, and the thin one at the end. "They were his. The Church's. He had them wired. He blessed every one of them on the riser before the first morning." The copper half of her voice was very steady. "I'd like him to see them."

Her sister looked at her for a long time.

Then she looked past her, at the priest, sitting against the wall at the edge of the shadow with his white hand on the stone.

"And him?" said Seraphina.

"Him too," said Solenne. "Especially him."

---

They could not carry the seven. Solenne had known that since the morning. She had one wing that would hold and one that would not, and a priest with one good hand, and a sister who would need both of hers for the Blade. And the seven could not walk. They had lain in the chain's shadow singing for six days, Marit had told them in nine notes, since the line began to fall and the bread stopped coming. They had not stood up since Nullday.

But they had walked once. Solenne remembered it. Ashfall. The white streets of the top tier at the dead hour, forty of them in four ranks of ten, coming down toward the plaza the way a procession comes, step and step and step, and singing. They had walked like that for seven years, all over the white city, in the dark, hunting.

Hunting her. Their lead. The one they took the note from.

She stood up in the middle of the nest, in the chain's shadow, with her broken wings half-open and her talons gripping the blankets. She did not look at anyone. She shut her eyes. And she let the copper do what it had wanted to do since Nullday.

She sang.

Not the question. She would not give them that. Not the Pure Note either, flat and unbroken, *yes*. She sang the lead. The first line of the morning hymn, the one the best treble of the Basilica school used to sing alone from the Vessel's stone at Lumina every Dawnday while the Eye flamed gold behind her. Solenne had sung it there herself, at eleven. *Light of the first note and the last.* It went up out of the copper half of her voice into the white air over the anchor-house, clear and high and true.

And the seven turned their heads.

She heard it. Seven breaths caught at once, in the middle of the question. Seven copper throats going still. And then, one after another, the way they had gone quiet on the riser and come back, they took the note from her. Not the question. The hymn. The line under hers, the second line, the one the Altar choir had sung behind the treble every Dawnday for four hundred years. Thin. Ragged. In tune.

And the thin woman at the end of the row put her bleached hands flat on the blanket, and pushed, and sat up.

Solenne did not stop singing. She did not open her eyes. She heard them come up off the blankets one by one, slowly, the way the old come up out of chairs, a hand on the stone, a hand on the shoulder of the one beside. She heard them stand. She heard Seraphina on her feet beside her with the Blade drawn again and drinking, and the priest at the wall, not moving, not breathing.

She turned, singing, and walked out of the chain's shadow toward the door.

They followed her.

---

She led them down the great stair of the city in the dark, singing.

She would remember it, afterward, as the strangest procession Sol-Luminary had ever seen, in a city that had once been nothing but processions. A woman with broken black-gold wings going down the white stair barefoot, on her talons, singing the first line of the morning hymn in a voice like copper. Behind her, seven people in white choir-robes that the light came through at the edges, walking slowly, in a line, each with a hand on the shoulder of the one in front, singing the second line. Beside them, going down the stair a step at a time with a god's bone held up in both hands, a knight in a borrowed coat, with her shadow falling the wrong way, forward, over the line of them. And at the back, in the last of the shadow, a priest with a white hand, carrying a sack with nothing left in it.

Nothing else moved in the white city.

She could hear that it did not. She listened all the way down, the way she had listened in Ashfall from the nest: to the empty streets, to the dead domes, to the glass-ring of the stone. Once, on the eleventh tier, she heard something far off along the rim. A ragged note, a single voice, copper, singing the question to itself in a street she could not see. One of the twenty-six, who had run in Ashfall when the Unison broke. Alone. She heard it falter. She heard it stop.

She did not stop.

Once, on the tenth, she heard the Tiers. Not near. Far below, under the line, through the stone of the whole city, very faint: eighteen thousand six hundred and forty people in the dark, not talking. A silence that had a shape. A silence with bundles in it.

And all the way down, under everything, she heard the god.

---

The Basilica's square opened off the stair on the ninth tier, as it had in Ashfall, and she heard the Flambeaux before she saw them.

Glass-fire had a sound. Nobody else seemed to know that either. A low steady roar, like a furnace heard through a wall, with a high crackle in it like frost breaking. Two hundred censers, burning white-gold, in a ring round the black tower and along the west front of the Basilica, a yard apart. She had heard that sound on the riser on the three hundredth morning, coming down from the gallery behind her, the moment before the fire came. She had heard it on Lumina, on the Altar, in a ring round two hundred throats. She heard it now across the white square, and the copper in her throat went tight, and her voice faltered on the hymn.

Behind her, all seven faltered with it.

She heard her sister's step check. She heard the priest stop.

She went on singing.

She led them out of the mouth of the stair into the square, into the open, singing, with the line of the seven behind her and the knight's shadow over them. And across the square, in the ring of gold fire, two hundred men in crimson turned their heads.

Nobody moved.

She walked toward them. She did not hurry. You do not hurry a procession; that was the first thing the Sky Choir taught you, at six, before the scales. You walk at the pace of the slowest throat. She walked at the pace of the thin woman behind her, step and step, across the white square of the ninth tier toward the west front of the Basilica of the Pure Note and the open door and the ring of fire.

A man came out of the ring to meet her.

---

He was tall and grey and very straight, in the crimson of a Flambeau-Preceptor, with the gold chain of the office across his chest and his censer held out on its chain in front of him so that the glass-fire made a little room of ordinary shade round him as he came. She knew him. Everybody in the Church knew him. Lucan Ostrel had preached the going-out of the Flambeaux at the Basilica every autumn for twenty years. *The Only Song.* Every Flambeau who had ever gone up the Greenroot road carried a printed copy in the breast of his crimson.

He had been in the gallery on the three hundredth morning. Beside Ignatius. She had heard his voice in the moment before the fire.

He stopped ten yards from her, in his room of shade, and looked at her. At the wings. At the seven behind her in a line with their hands on each other's shoulders, singing. At the knight with the Blade. And past them, to the back of the line, to the priest.

She heard his breath go in.

"Crane," said Lucan Ostrel.

Ignatius did not answer.

"I heard you were dead." The Preceptor's voice was the voice of a man who had preached in a nave for twenty years. It did not shake. "I heard the Abomination took you on Lumina. I said a mass for you." He looked at the empty sack. At the white hand, bare. "Where is your fire?"

"Out," said Ignatius Crane.

The Preceptor looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked at Solenne, and she saw him decide what she was, and what to do about it, the way she had seen Ignatius decide on the riser. She heard the glass-fire in his censer climb. White-gold. Roaring.

"Abomination," he said. Quite gently. "You'll not bring those into the Light's house."

She stopped singing.

---

She did not decide to. She had been taught, at six, never to stop in the middle of a line. She stopped in the middle of the line. And behind her the seven stopped too, all at once, and the white square of the ninth tier went silent except for the roar of two hundred censers. And into that silence, without turning round, without raising it, she spoke with the other half of her voice.

The void half. The half she had brought back up out of the dark. Like wind over the mouth of a well.

"*Look at them*," said Solenne.

She heard it go out of her across the square. She had used it on the Organum's marines in the willows at the edge of the Woods, *sit down*, and watched a man sit down in the mud with his musket in his lap and his face quite empty. It was not a command. She had never known what it was. It was the sound of the place she had climbed out of, and men heard it, and did what it said, the way you do what a dream says.

Lucan Ostrel looked.

She watched him do it. She watched him look past her at the seven, at the thin bleached faces and the light coming through the robes, at the copper glinting at seven throats. She watched him look at the thin woman, Marit Ferrand, who had been too frightened to look at the Patriarch on the riser on the first morning, and who was looking now, straight, at him.

"*They're yours*," said Solenne's void. "*You wired them. The autumn of '13. The cathedral choir. You stood at the end of the table in the Basilica undercroft with your censer while the surgeons did it, so that the fire would be in the room. I've heard about it. They told me. Marit told me. In notes, one word a note, over seven weeks, in the chain's shadow at the top of the world.*"

That was not true. Marit had not told her. But she had heard the Preceptor's breath go in again, and she had known.

"*Look at them*," said Solenne. "*All of them. One at a time.*"

He looked.

She stood in the white square with her broken wings half-open and listened to two hundred men in crimson hold their breath, and to the glass-fire roaring, and to the Preceptor's heart. It was going very fast. And she heard the moment it changed. Not slowed. Changed. The way the god's note changed at the end.

Lucan Ostrel lowered his censer.

He did not step aside. He did not say anything. He stood in his room of shade with his censer hanging from its chain at his side, and the glass-fire in it sank. Not out. Down. And he looked at the seven, one after another, down the line, the way she had made Ignatius look in the anchor-house. And she understood that he would go on looking for as long as she let him, and that she could let him go on for the rest of his life.

She did not have time for that.

She took up the hymn again at the place she had stopped, in the middle of the line, and walked past him.

The seven followed her. Through the ring of gold fire, between the Flambeaux, who did not move. Up the steps of the west front. To the great door of the Basilica of the Pure Note, which stood open on the dark, and the one note, held.

---

She had never been inside it since the Fracture. She knew it the way she knew her own throat.

The nave went away from the door under the vault in the dark, long and high, built like a blade through a loaf across the old line, which was not there any more. The west half had been the shade half: black stone, black doors, black glass in the high lancets. It was still black. But the light was in it now. The flat sourceless white of the Bleach lay in the nave the way water lies in a cistern, and the only shade was round the censers.

There were censers everywhere. Hung on iron stands along both aisles, a yard apart, burning white-gold, each one making its little room of ordinary dark in the white, so that the nave was a corridor of overlapping shadows down the middle with the light pressing in at the sides. And at the end of the corridor, the iron wall. Black iron, floor to vault, that the Church had built across the nave on the morning of the Fracture to keep out the light. With a door in it. And beyond the wall, she knew, the Lightward Nave, sealed seven years, where the congregation of the eighth tier had been at the dawn office when the Spire let go, and still were, in the pews, seven years thin.

And in the shade-side stalls, under the censers, nine people in white.

Holding the note.

She heard them as she came through the door. She had heard them from the square. She had heard them every hour of her life since she was six. Nine choristers in three rows of three, taking the note one from another without a breath between, so that the note was never not being sung. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Flat. Unbroken. *Yes.* They had held it through the Fracture and through Ashfall and through the line coming down past the Basilica on the morning of the Restoration, and they were holding it now, in the white, in their little rooms of fire, with the Light coming down the chains to find its city.

They were old. She saw that as she came down the nave. All nine. Grey, and white, and thin, the youngest of them sixty. They had been the old men and women of the cathedral choir in 412, too old to be wired, too old for the riser. Kept back. And they had held the note for seven years in shifts in the shade-side stalls of the Basilica while the young ones were taken.

They did not stop when the seven came down the nave behind her, singing the second line of the morning hymn. They could not. You did not stop the Pure Note. But she watched their eyes. Nine pairs of old eyes over nine open mouths, following the seven down the nave. Following Marit Ferrand, who had sung second soprano beside some of them in the Altar choir before the Fracture, when she was fifteen.

She stopped at the end of the corridor of shadows, before the iron wall. The seven stopped behind her. She let the hymn go, at the end of a line, the way you are supposed to.

The seven let it go with her.

And in the quiet after it, with the Pure Note going on from the stalls, flat, unbroken, a voice she had not heard since Lumina said, from the floor at the foot of the iron wall:

"Oh. Oh, who's that? Who's singing the morning office? It's not the hour. Is it Dawnday? I've lost the days. Who's there?"

---

Severin Ashe was sitting on the floor.

He was sitting cross-legged on the bare stone at the foot of the iron wall, in the room of shade of a censer on a stand beside him, in a white cassock that was too big for him, with his white thistledown hair standing up round his head. He had a lamp beside him on the floor that was not lit. He had his hands in his lap, palms up, the way you hold your hands to receive something.

He was looking up at her. At the sound of her.

His eyes were white.

Not pale. White. The colour of a pear left a night in the Bleach. She had seen him on Lumina in Ashfall, with his face streaming and his soft clear eyes full of the light, reaching for the Blade. They had been blue then, a little faded, an old man's. They were not any colour now. The Light had taken them. Slowly, she supposed, over seven years, in a room of shade that was never quite shade enough, while he sat at the foot of the iron wall and asked it what it wanted. And he had not noticed until it was done, because he had never been looking at anything but the Light.

"Who's there?" said Severin Ashe again. Softly. Delighted. "That was lovely. I haven't heard the morning office sung since Lumina. Not properly. Not with the treble." He held out one hand, a little, toward the sound of her, toward nothing. "You mustn't let me keep you. But who are you?"

She did not answer.

Her sister did.

---

"Father," said Seraphina.

The old man's face did a thing that Solenne had heard described and never seen. It lit. All of it, at once. The way a child's face lights.

"*Seraphina*," he said. "Oh. Oh, child. Oh, you've come home." He put both hands out now, toward the sound of her, reaching, his white eyes going past her shoulder. "Come here. Let me— no, I can't see you, come here, come down where I can—"

Seraphina went down on one knee on the stone in front of him, with the Blade across her other knee, bare. He found her face with his hands. Solenne watched him do it. His old fingers on her sister's cropped head, her brow, her cheek, the line of her jaw. Learning it. The way you learn a face in the dark.

"You've cut your hair," said Severin Ashe. "Oh, I'm so glad. I'm so glad you've come. I've prayed for you. Every night. Since Lumina." His thumbs moved on her cheekbones. "I wasn't angry. Did you think I was? You mustn't. I was frightened, on the Altar. That's all. I said terrible things. I'm old and I was frightened and I said things." He held her face. "*Because it was tired, Father.* That's what you said. I've thought about it every night. I've asked the Light about it. And do you know, it told me you were right." He smiled. His white eyes shone. "It told me it *was* tired. And that it was coming home to rest. And that we're to stay and receive it." He leaned in, close, as if it were a secret. "It's coming down the chains, Seraphina. You'll have seen. It's coming to find us. Isn't it wonderful?"

Seraphina did not answer.

Solenne heard her sister's breath. She heard it go in, and stop, and not come out.

"Father," said Seraphina at last. "The Tiers."

"Yes?"

"Eighteen thousand six hundred and forty people. On the walkways. Under the line. With their bundles." Her voice was very low, the warm mezzo, gone rough. "They're waiting for you to say *go*. They've built nine chains to the gulf floor, link by link, out of the dead tiers. For seven years. They've drilled it every Driftday. The line will be past the Keel Docks tomorrow night." She took his hands from her face, gently, and held them. "Please. Tell them to go down."

The old man's face did not change.

"But they mustn't," said Severin Ashe. "Child. Don't you see? That's what I've been shown. *Removal from the chains is not required.* It came to me on Massday, very clearly, at the night office. *Discomfort is not anticipated.* It doesn't hurt. The Light doesn't hurt. It's only coming home." He patted her hands. "And those who are in its city when it comes will be held. That's what it said. Held. Isn't that what we've always wanted for them?"

Solenne heard it then.

Not in what he said. In how he said it. *Removal from the chains is not required.* He said it the way you say a line you have learned by heart. A different voice from his own. Flatter. No person in it. He said it the way she had heard the nine in the choir-stalls say the Pure Note: something given to him, held, repeated, without a breath in it.

*Somebody in that house writes like a clerk of the Organum.*

She looked at the priest.

Ignatius Crane was standing at the back of the corridor of shadows, behind the seven, in the room of a censer. He was looking at the Patriarch. And she saw him hear it too.

---

"Who reads to you, Father?" said Ignatius.

The old man turned his white eyes toward the voice.

"*Ignatius*," he said. "Oh, Ignatius. Oh, I said a mass for you. Lucan said you were— oh, come here, come here, let me—"

"Who reads to you?" said Ignatius Crane again. He did not come. "Your eyes have gone. Since Ashfall. Since before. Who reads you your correspondence?"

"Why, Placidus," said Severin Ashe, surprised. "He always has. Since I came to the Patriarchate. He's been my reader for thirty years. He reads me the letters in the morning and the Office in the evening, and he sits with me at the night office while I pray." He smiled toward the sound of the priest. "You know Placidus. He taught you to write a writ, at the Organum, when you were a boy. He told me so. He was so proud of you."

Solenne heard the priest's breath stop.

"Where is he?" said Ignatius.

"Oh, he'll be in the chapel. Getting it ready. It's nearly the night office." The old man turned his face back to Seraphina, and his hands found hers again, and held them. "Stay for it. All of you. Please. I'd so like you to. It's been so quiet since Lumina. And the Light always answers at the night office. It always has. Every night for seven years." His white eyes shone. "You'll hear it, Seraphina. I'd so like you to hear it. I've always wanted to share it with someone."

---

The chapel of the night office was a black room off the north aisle, the shade side, the size of a ship's cabin, with a censer on a stand in each corner and no window. A kneeler, worn hollow in the middle by seven years of one old man's knees. An altar the size of a table, with a sun-disc on it in black enamel. And behind the altar, from wall to wall, a screen.

Solenne knew the screen. Every chapel in the Heights had one. A lattice of black iron, fine as lace, from the floor to the height of a man's head, worked in the shape of a sun with its rays going out. In the old Church, before the Fracture, the Altar sisters had knelt behind it at the night office and sung the response to the Patriarch's prayer, so that he would hear it come from the place where the Light was, and not from a woman's mouth. *The Light gives the answer.* It had been beautiful. She had knelt behind a screen like it herself, at nine, in her first year in the Sky Choir, and sung the response at the Patriarch's night office in the old Basilica, and seen through the iron lace the back of his white head bowed over the kneeler.

There were no Altar sisters behind it now. She could hear that there was nobody behind it at all.

Then she heard somebody breathing.

---

They stood in the doorway of the chapel, the three of them. Severin had asked them to. *Stand at the back, where you won't distract me. It doesn't mind company. It just likes me to ask properly.* The seven were in the nave, in the corridor of shadows, lying down in the room of a censer at the foot of the iron wall, singing the question very softly to the vault. Lucan Ostrel stood over them in his crimson with his censer, looking at them, and had not moved since the square.

The old man knelt.

He did it slowly, with a hand on the altar's edge, the way he had sat down on the floor of Lyra's black room in Ashfall. An old man's grunt. When he was down he stayed for a long time with his white head bowed and his hands together on the edge of the kneeler, breathing.

Then he lifted his face toward the screen. Toward the sun of black iron lace, with his white eyes open.

"It's Severin," he said. "Good evening."

Nobody answered.

"I've brought people tonight. I hope you don't mind. Seraphina's come home. You'll remember Seraphina. And her sister. And Ignatius, who isn't dead after all. I'm so glad." He smiled at the screen. "I'm sorry I'm late. I lost the hour. Somebody was singing the morning office in the square and I thought it was Dawnday." A little laugh, an old man's, at himself. "It *is* Dawnday. I'd lost the days. Placidus will have to tell me the days now."

He was quiet for a moment.

"The Tiers are frightened," he said. "Seraphina says. They're on the walkways with their bundles. Eighteen thousand of them. They've built chains down to the gulf and they're waiting for me to tell them to go." He bowed his head. "I've told them what you told me. On Massday. To stay, and receive you. But they're frightened. And Seraphina's frightened, and she's never been frightened of anything." He lifted his face again. "So I'm asking. Again. I'm sorry to ask again. What do you want of us?"

Silence.

The nine in the choir-stalls held the note, faint, through the wall. The censers roared in the corners. The old man knelt with his face lifted to the screen and his hands together and waited, the way he had waited, Solenne understood, every night for seven years. And she listened, the way she always listened. To the room. To the stone. To the god, very far off, at the end of its chain, asking.

To the breath behind the screen.

It drew in.

And a voice came through the iron lace. Low. Level. Unhurried. No higher and no lower than a man reading aloud. It came from the place where the Light was supposed to be, through the sun of black iron, and it said:

*"The descent is to be received. Removal is not required. What is held in the city at the time of the descent will remain held. Concern for the Tiers is noted. Discomfort is not anticipated."*

The old man's face lit.

"*Thank you*," he whispered. "Oh, thank you. Thank you."

Behind the screen, very faintly, so faintly that she was certain nobody else in the room could hear it, Solenne heard paper move.

A page. Turned.

---

The Light did not turn pages.

She did not think. She went. Past her sister in the doorway, past the kneeler, past the old man with his face lifted, round the end of the black altar to the place where the screen met the wall, where there had always been a narrow gap for the sisters to go in and out. She had gone through it at nine. She went through it now with her broken wing scraping the iron and her talons loud on the stone, and nobody stopped her.

There was a man behind the screen.

He was kneeling on a cushion on the floor in the narrow space between the iron lace and the black wall, in the room of the censer in the corner. A small old man in the black cassock of a canon of the Basilica, with a fringe of white hair and ink on his fingers. He had a shuttered lamp on the floor beside him, open a slit, so that a thin blade of yellow light lay across his knees. And on his knees, in the blade of light, a letter.

Heavy cream paper. Folded in three. A seal of plain grey wax, broken, with no device on it at all.

He looked up at her.

She had seen him before. Seven years ago. On the riser, the first morning, behind the Patriarch, carrying the book while the old man went along the rows to bless them. She had not known his name then. She knew it now, because the old man had said it in the nave. *Placidus. He's been my reader for thirty years.* He had written a book once, before the Fracture. *On the Constancy of the Light.* There had been a copy of it in her mother's house.

He did not get up. He did not cover the letter. He knelt on his cushion behind the screen with the blade of lamplight on his knees and looked at her, at the wings and the copper and the two lines cut down her face. And she watched him understand that it was over. She heard it. His breath went out of him, all of it, the way a breath goes out at the end of a very long phrase. And did not come back in for a long time.

"Lady," said Canon Placidus Sorell. Very quietly. In his own voice, which was not low or level at all. It was an old man's voice, frightened, with a tremor in it. "Please. Not in front of him."

On the other side of the screen, the Patriarch said, "Is somebody there? Seraphina? Is somebody behind the screen?"

---

She would think, afterward, that she could have let it go.

She had the letter in front of her, on the canon's knees, in the slit of lamplight. She could see the first line of it, in a small old very clear hand. She could have turned round, and gone back through the gap, and said *nobody, Father, nobody's there*, and taken the canon by the arm on the way out of the chapel and made him write the other proclamation, the one with *go* in it, in the voice the old man would believe. She could have done the kind thing. The thing the canon had done for seven years.

She did not.

She had held a note once, because it was the kind thing. Because the god was asking so gently, *is it you*, and the Rite would fail if she stopped, and the Patriarch was in the gallery, and her sister. She had held it, and that was yes.

She bent down and took the letter off the canon's knees. He did not stop her. She went back round the end of the altar with it, out of the gap in the screen, into the chapel. Seraphina was standing in the doorway with her face gone white. The priest was behind her. And the old man was kneeling at the kneeler with his white eyes turned toward the sound of her talons on the stone, puzzled, smiling a little, the way you smile at a child who has come into the room during prayers.

"Solenne?" he said. "Is that you? Was there somebody there?"

"Placidus," said Solenne Aubade.

The old man's face went still.

"He's behind the screen, Father. He has been every night. He has a letter." She heard her own voice, the copper and the void, and it was not steady. "He's been reading them to you. At the night office. In the Light's voice. For seven years."

---

Severin Ashe did not say anything for a long time.

He knelt at the kneeler with his hands still together on its edge. He did not turn his head toward the screen. He kept his white eyes on the place where her voice had come from. And she listened to him, because she could not help it: to his breath, which had gone very slow, and his heart, which had not changed at all, and the small dry sound of his old hands pressing together on the wood.

"Placidus," he said at last. Not loud. The way you say the name of somebody in the next room. "Is that right?"

Behind the screen, the canon made a sound.

"Come out, Placidus. Please. I can't see you. Come where I can hear you properly."

The canon came out through the gap in the screen. He came slowly, an old man getting up off his knees, with the shuttered lamp in his hand. He stood at the end of the altar in the black chapel with the lamp and did not come any further. Solenne could hear him shaking.

"Is it right?" said Severin Ashe.

"Yes," said the canon.

"Every night?"

"Yes."

"Since when?"

The canon did not answer at once. When he did, his voice had gone very small.

"The ninth night after the Fracture," he said. "You'd stopped eating, Severin. You'd stopped sleeping. You sat at the foot of the wall, there, in the nave, day and night, with your hands like that, and asked it. What do you want. How do we bring you home. Over and over. And it never answered. And on the ninth night you got up and went to the door in the wall, the door into the Lightward Nave. And you put your hand on the bolt." He stopped. "I thought you were going to go through. To them. To the eighth tier, in the pews. I thought you were going to go into the white and sit down with them because it wouldn't answer you."

The old man did not say anything.

"And that morning a letter had come," said Placidus Sorell. "From the Organum. From him. The first one. He'd written to you about the Fracture. What he'd heard. He'd been listening, the morning it let go. He wrote that it wasn't angry. That it had let go because it was told it could rest, and that it had felt like rest, and that it wasn't in pain." His voice shook. "It was the only thing anybody had written that sounded as if it *knew*. Everybody else wrote that it was a judgement. He wrote as if he'd been in the room. And you were at the door, with your hand on the bolt, and I thought—" He stopped. "I took you back to the night office. And I went behind the screen, where the sisters used to kneel. And when you asked it what it wanted, I read you the letter. Not all of it. Only the part about the Light. I read it the way the sisters used to sing the response. I put it in the Light's voice. The Office's voice. The one they taught me at the Organum when I was a boy. Nobody in it." His breath caught. "And you wept, Severin. You said *it answered*. You went to bed. You ate in the morning."

"And the next night," said Severin Ashe.

"There was another letter. He wrote every week. Sometimes twice." The canon's voice had gone almost to nothing. "I couldn't stop. You'd have known. You'd have gone back to the door."

The old man was quiet.

"And the score," he said. "The living chord. The note in the margin, in my pencil. *Hold until it answers.*"

"You sent him the score. To share it with him. You were so happy." The canon swallowed. "He sent it back with that on it. In his hand. I put it in your papers. You couldn't see it well enough by then to know it wasn't yours."

---

Solenne stood in the black chapel with the letter in her hands and listened to the old man not move.

She had thought he would break. She had braced for it, the way she had braced on the riser. She had heard men break. She had heard her sisters' throats go, on the three hundredth morning, one after another, every one, as the light came back and ate them. She had heard Ignatius Crane's fire go out on Lumina, a sound like a door closing on a furnace.

Severin Ashe did not break. He knelt at the kneeler with his hands together and his white eyes on nothing, and she heard his heart go on at exactly the same pace. And after a long time he said, very gently, the way he had spoken to Lyra on the floor of her cell:

"Oh, Placidus. Oh, my dear. Seven years. You must have been so tired."

The canon made a sound she would not forget. It was not a word.

"Come here," said the old man. "Please. I can't get up. My knees."

The canon came. He came round the end of the altar and knelt on the stone beside the kneeler, an old man beside an old man. And Severin Ashe reached out without looking, toward the sound of him, and found his inky hand, and held it.

"Was it all his?" he said. "All of it? Every night?"

"Yes."

"None of it was—" He stopped. He did not finish it.

"No," said the canon.

"No." The old man nodded slowly. "No. I didn't think so. Not really." He was quiet. "I think I knew. Do you know, I think I've always known I couldn't hear it. I used to stand in the gallery at the Rite and watch the girls on the riser, and watch their faces when it sang, and I couldn't hear anything. Not a thing. Seventy years in the Church and I've never once heard it." He held the canon's hand. "I thought if I asked properly. If I asked every night. If I kept asking. One night it would." A small laugh. "And one night it did. And it was you."

---

"Read it to me," he said.

Solenne looked down at the letter in her hands.

"The one tonight. All of it. The way he wrote it." The old man turned his white eyes toward the sound of her. "Solenne, would you? I'd rather it was you. Not Placidus. Placidus has done enough reading."

She did not want to. She stood with the cream paper in her hands and did not want to more than she had ever not wanted anything, more than she had not wanted to sing the fall below the fall at Oakhaven. She looked at her sister in the doorway. Seraphina was looking at the old man with her face like a coin that has been struck too hard.

She unfolded the letter.

The hand was small and old and very clear.

*Hearthday, the twentieth of Frostmonth. The Organum.*

*My dear Severin,*

She stopped.

"Go on," said the old man.

*My dear Severin,*

*I am told the line is coming down on your city, and I am sorry. I have heard it asking since Nullday. I expect you have not. I do not say that unkindly. You have always been the one person I could write to who did not hear them, and I have always been grateful to you for it. You cannot imagine what it is to write to someone who does not hear them.*

*You will ask me what to do. You always do. So I will tell you what I think, which is all I have ever told you.*

*I should not move them, if I were you. It will not hurt them. I have listened to the Light for eighty years, and it has never once hurt anyone who did not fight it. The Bleach is not cruel. It is only very tired, and it is letting go of things, and they go with it. I have heard a great many people go. Nobody screams.*

*On the thirtieth, on the Longest Night, I am going to go down under the Organum and give all eight of them what they have asked me for. I have told you this before. After that, I think, it will not matter very much where anybody is standing. I would rather your people spent their last week at home, in the shade they have made for themselves, than on a ladder in the dark over a two-thousand-foot drop. That is all. That is the whole of my advice.*

*I know you will not hear this as I mean it. You never have. Placidus will see to that, as he always has, and I have never discouraged him, because I think you would not have lived through the first winter without it, and I find I am selfish enough to want you to have lived. I hope you will forgive me that. I hope you will forgive him.*

*Eat something. Sleep. I am sorry about your eyes. Placidus wrote to me in Ashfall.*

*With my love, as ever,*

*Casimir*

---

Nobody said anything.

The censers roared in the corners. The nine held the note through the wall. In the nave, faint, the seven sang the question to the vault. Solenne stood with the letter in her hands and listened to the old man at the kneeler, and to the canon beside him with his inky hand in the old man's hand, and to her sister in the doorway, who had stopped breathing again.

"*My dear Severin*," said Severin Ashe.

He said it very softly. As if he were trying it.

"Every one?" he said. "Placidus. Every letter. Did they all—"

"Every one," said the canon. "*My dear Severin.* Every one. I left it out." His voice cracked. "You'd have known."

"Seven years," said the old man.

He said it to nobody. To the screen. To the sun of black iron lace with nobody behind it.

"Somebody wrote to me every week for seven years," said Severin Ashe, "and called me by my name. And I never heard it once." He was quiet for a long time. "And the Light never said anything at all."

He did not weep. Solenne had thought he would. She had watched him weep on Lumina, on his knees by the Blade, with his hands held out over it. He knelt at the kneeler now with his white eyes dry and his head a little on one side, the way a man sits when he is listening for something he has been told is there. And after a while she understood what he was doing.

He was listening. Properly. For the first time. Now that nobody was going to answer.

"Seraphina," he said. "Is it saying anything? Now? You'd know. You've got the bone."

Her sister did not answer at once.

"Yes, Father," said Seraphina.

"What?"

Seraphina looked at Solenne. Solenne looked back. And she saw her sister decide to tell him the truth, the way she herself had decided to bring the letter out from behind the screen. Not because it was kind.

"It's asking if it's you," said Seraphina. "It's asking if anyone has come."

The old man's face did not change. Not for a long time. Then, very slowly, it did. Not the lighting. The other thing. The thing she had seen on the face of the thin woman in the anchor-house, Marit, when she could not find the next note.

"Oh," said Severin Ashe. "Oh. Is it." He bowed his head. "No. It's only me. I'm so sorry."

---

He dictated it to the canon on the floor of the chapel, with his back against the kneeler and the canon's lamp opened all the way.

He did not write it himself. He could not see the page. But he made the canon read every line back to him, slowly, twice, and he changed words, and he would not let the canon change any. Solenne stood in the doorway and listened to them do it, the two old men on the floor of the black room, one saying and one writing, the inky hand moving across the paper in the lamplight. It took the best part of an hour. It was not long when it was done.

*My children.*

*I have never heard the Light. I have told you for seven years what it wanted, and I never once heard it say anything. I am sorry. I was wrong.*

*Go down.*

*Take the children first, and the old, and the sick. Go in each other's shade, the way you have taught each other. Go carefully. Do not wait for me.*

*No one in the Church is to stop you. Any man in crimson who stands at a ladder-head is to stand there with his fire to keep the light off you, and for nothing else.*

*I am so sorry.*

*Severin.*

"Is that all of it?" said the canon.

"Read it again."

The canon read it again.

"Yes," said the old man. "That's all." He held out his hand toward the sound of the paper. "Give it here. I'd like to sign it. I can still do that. I've done it so many times." The canon put the pen in his fingers and guided his hand to the bottom of the page, and Severin Ashe wrote his name, by feel, in an old man's copperplate that went uphill at the end. Then he gave it back.

"Ignatius," he said.

The priest had not moved from behind Seraphina in the doorway. He had not said a word since the nave.

"Father."

"Take it down. To the chime-house. You and Lucan, with the fire, so they'll know it's the Church that sent it and not some trick." The white eyes turned toward the sound of him. "They'll believe you. They're frightened of you. Everybody in the Tiers is frightened of you. That's useful now. Let it be useful."

Ignatius Crane looked at the old man on the floor for a long time.

Then he came into the chapel and knelt and took the proclamation out of the canon's hand, and folded it, and put it in the breast of his brown coat, where the Flambeaux carried *The Only Song*.

"Seraphina," said the old man. "Go with him. The Cantor's down there. She'll need you. You're the only one of them who can walk in the white in daylight." He smiled, a little, toward her. "You always were."

"Father—"

"And Solenne." He turned his face toward her. "Would you take me to the Altar?"

---

Seraphina said no. She said it three times. She said it in the warm mezzo that carried in a city of whisperers, and the third time it did not carry at all, because she had gone down on her knees on the chapel floor in front of him and was holding both his hands.

He let her. He held her hands and listened to her say it, and when she had stopped he said, gently:

"Child. I'd like to be somewhere it can find me. That's all. I've spent seventy years telling people what it wanted. I should like to sit where it can tell me itself, if it wants to." He was quiet. "And if it doesn't want to, I should like to be the last thing in this city it takes, and not the first thing it was told to."

She did not answer.

"You can't take me. You've got the bone. It would go round me. I'd be sitting in your shade and it would never find me." He took one hand out of hers and touched her cheek, where he had learned her face in the nave. "Solenne can. It doesn't know what she is any more. She said so on the Altar. I heard her." He turned his white eyes toward the doorway. "Will you? I'm not heavy. I've never been heavy. Lucan says the Flambeaux call me the feather."

Solenne looked at him.

She thought of the riser. Of the Rite. Of the old man in the gallery on the three hundredth morning with his hands raised, while the god asked her, so gently, *is it you*. Of her own throat, holding. *Yes.* She thought of the ring of grey hands at Oakhaven. Of Lyra going round it with her palm on the bark, a whole day in the snow, asking every one. Of twenty-five red chalk marks.

"Do you want to go?" she said.

The old man was surprised. She saw it. His white eyes went wide.

"Nobody's asked me that," he said. "Do you know, in seventy years, nobody's ever asked me that." He thought about it. Properly. She watched him do it. "Yes. Yes, I want to go. I'd like to be asked something, at the end. Even if it's only *is it you*." He smiled. "And I'd like to be able to tell it the truth."

"Then I'll take you," said Solenne.

---

She took him across the Pilgrim Chain at the dead hour.

She carried him. He was right; he weighed nothing. A bundle of white cassock and thistledown and old bones, in her arms, against her chest, with his arms round her neck like a child's. She carried him out of the seventh anchor-house on the south-east rim onto the walkway of the chain, a kilometre of black iron decking six feet wide over the gulf, polished by four hundred years of pilgrims' feet, going out level through the white to Lumina.

He was not in any shade. She had none to give him. The light came down on both of them out of nowhere and everywhere, flat and white, at the dead hour, and went round her wings, gold, and did not go round him.

She heard it start.

It did not hurt him. She listened for that, the whole way out. She listened to his breath and his heart and the small dry sounds of his old body in her arms, and there was nothing in them that was pain. Only, after the first hundred yards, a very faint high ring, like a fingernail on a glass, the sound the priest's hand made. Coming up out of him. Getting a little louder with every yard.

"Is it now?" he said, into her neck. "I can't tell. I can't feel anything."

"It's started."

"Oh." He was quiet. "It doesn't hurt."

"No."

"He was right about that, then. Casimir." A breath. "He usually is. That's the trouble with him."

Halfway across, the city breathed out.

She felt it go, behind her, through the iron of the chain and the stone of the rim: the long slow outward sigh through every stone of Sol-Luminary, the dead-hour breath. She did not look back. She knew what it was. The line, coming down. The second tier. The Tiers below it pulling their shutters, and her sister and the priest and the Preceptor going down the ladders in their bubbles of fire with a folded paper in a brown coat, toward a chime-house that had been ringing since Nullday for ships that could not come.

She walked on.

---

The Altar of Lumina was a disc of white stone two hundred feet across, on a peak half a mile from the Spire's flank, and it was empty.

The canopies were gone. The choir was gone. The Eye stood on its frame at the far edge, forty feet of bronze gone white as a shell, with the long black crack across its face from Ashfall. And in the middle of the disc, the Vessel's stone. A dais of white stone ten feet across, where the best treble of the Basilica school had stood every Dawnday for four hundred years and sung the first line of the morning hymn alone.

She had stood on it herself, at eleven.

She set the old man down on it. He wanted to sit on the floor. She sat him cross-legged in the middle of the stone, the way he sat, like a boy at a story, with his white cassock round him and his hands in his lap, palms up. He was lighter than he had been in the chapel. She could see the shape of his hands through the skin of them, faintly, the way you see the shape of a coin through a cloth.

"Is it facing me?" he said. "The Spire. Am I facing it?"

She turned him a little. "Now you are."

"Thank you." He lifted his face. His white eyes were open. "Is it still asking?"

She listened.

It was. Louder here than anywhere in the city, half a mile from the Spire's flank, on the stone where the dawn office had been sung for four hundred years. It came up out of the Altar through the soles of her feet. It came down the air. It came out of the copper in her own throat, which wanted, so badly, to give it back. *Is it you? Have you come?* Going up at the end. Like a child at a door.

"Yes," she said.

"What shall I say to it?" said Severin Ashe. He sounded, for the first time since she had known him, which was all her life, uncertain. "I've never— I don't know how to talk to it. I only ever asked it things."

She thought of Ilse Brennan, in the gallery over the Root, breathing the song in the cold. *She asked it if it was all right. Nobody has asked it that for three hundred years.*

She thought of Lyra's slate.

"Tell it who you are," said Solenne. "It's never been told. Nobody ever comes up here and says who they are. They just sing at it."

The old man thought about that.

Then he turned his face up toward the Spire, toward the white needle a mile high with the black crack down its side, that he had never once heard. And he said, quite clearly, in his own voice, warm and soft and a little out of breath, the way he had said it to Lyra Ashgrove on the floor of a black cell in Ashfall:

"Hello. I'm Severin. I don't use the rest of it, mostly."

---

She left him there.

He did not want her to stay. He said so. *It'll be easier for it if it's only me. It's never had only me.*

So she stood up, and stepped down off the Vessel's stone, and went back across the white disc of the Altar toward the chain, with her broken wing dragging and her talons loud on the stone.

At the rim she stopped. She did not turn round. She listened.

He was talking.

Not praying. Talking. The way you talk to somebody at a bedside. She could hear it all the way across the Altar, because she could hear everything. He was telling it about himself. About his mother, who had kept bees on the eleventh tier. About the Basilica school, and the bench under the great east window, and the Cantor with the tuning-rod and the voice like a bell who had told him the soul had a sound. About seventy years of not hearing it. About Placidus, who had been so tired. About a man at the Organum who had written to him every week and called him by his name. About Seraphina, who had cut her hair.

And under it, all the time, coming down out of the white, the question. Going up at the end.

She did not know whether it was listening. She did not know whether a god that had been asked one note for four hundred years could hear an old man telling it about bees. She thought, standing at the rim of the Altar of Lumina with her back to him and the copper aching in her throat, that nobody knew. That nobody had ever tried.

She went onto the chain and started back.

She heard him talking all the way across. Half a mile of black iron through the white, and she heard every word. He was still talking when the sky went grey over the peaks to the east. He was still talking when the Basilica's bell rang the hour before dawn on Driftday, down in the city, the drill day, and under it, very faint, from the chime-house on the third tier, in the white now, held in a ring of glass-fire, a different bell: high, quick, over and over, the way a bell rings that has been waiting seven years to ring.

He was still talking when the sun came up.

Then he was not.

---

She stood on the walkway of the Pilgrim Chain, a hundred yards from the seventh anchor-house, in the full white of the morning, with the light going round her wings gold, and listened to the place where he had been.

Nothing.

And then, out of the white, from the Spire, the question again. *Is it you?* The same. Going up at the end. The god had not stopped asking. She had not thought it would.

But it was asking a little more slowly.

She was almost sure of that. She would never be sure. She would tell Lyra, later, on the slate, and Lyra would look at her for a long time and not write anything back. She would tell herself, for the rest of her life, that she could have imagined it. But she stood on the chain in the morning and listened to the god of Light ask its question, and between one asking and the next there was a space. A pause. The length of a breath. The length of the space you leave in a conversation when someone has told you something about bees, and you are thinking about it.

She had wanted Casimir Vaelen to be right since Lumina. She had wanted it so much that she could not sleep.

She found, standing on the chain in the white, that she did not any more. Not because he was wrong about the gods. He was not wrong. They were tired. They were asking to put it down. She had heard it on the riser and she heard it now.

Because he had listened to them for eighty years and written to an old man every week and called him by his name, and had never once, in all that time, gone up to one of them and told it his.

The copper in her throat ached.

She did not let it lift. Not yet. She went back along the chain to the city, toward the bell that was ringing *go*, to find her sister and the seven and the priest, and Lyra, and the nine chains going down into the shade.

Behind her, very faintly, the god asked again. And paused.

---

## CHAPTER SEVEN
### The Fall-Chains

> *Hand, foot, hand. Breathe on the foot. If the one above you stops, put your hand on their boot and hum. If the one below you stops, wait.*
>
> — The fall-chain drill, taught on Driftdays since 413 to every child in the Umbral Tiers tall enough to reach the first rung

*The Keel Docks and the gulf, Sol-Luminary. Driftday the twenty-fourth of Frostmonth, to Memoryday the twenty-fifth.*

---

The bell did not stop.

Lyra had heard bells in her life. Every Cantor of the Reach had. The Chapel bell in the square at Oakhaven, the hour-bell at Reedside, the funeral bell, the fire bell, the little brass hand-bell the midwives rang at a door to say *come quickly*. She had never heard a bell like the one that began to ring in the chime-house on the third tier in the hour before dawn on Driftday. High, and quick, and thin, the same two notes over and over, the way a child says the same two words when it has been waiting a long time to say them and is afraid that if it stops someone will tell it to be quiet.

*Go. Go. Go. Go.*

She was on the iron gallery at the tip of the city, under the Keel Docks, with the nine chains going down beside her into the grey. She had been there since midnight. She had not slept. Brenna Corliss was beside her on an upturned crate with the slate on her knees and the white half of her face turned to the stone, and when the bell began Brenna did not move for a long time. She sat with her big hands flat on the slate and listened to it.

"That's the Church," she said at last. "That's not us. We'd not ring it that fast."

Lyra looked at her.

"It's the priest," said Brenna. "Your priest. He's up there in the chime-house in the white with a ring of fire round him, ringing it himself." She shut her good eye. "Seven years I've waited to hear that. I thought it'd be me ringing it."

---

It came down the ladders a quarter of an hour later. Not the priest. A paper.

A Flambeau brought it. A young man, red-haired, in crimson, with his censer burning white-gold on its chain, coming down the last ladder from the second tier to the Keel Docks in his little room of shade. Every face on every walkway turned to follow him. Lyra watched the Tiers watch him. She watched the woman with the baby, on the walkway over the cradles, pull the baby's head into her shadow and not his.

He came along the gallery to Brenna Corliss and held out the paper. His hand was shaking.

"From the High Patriarch," he said. "By the Preceptor's order. It's to be read at every ladder-head." He swallowed. "I've read it at eleven. I was told to bring it to the woman with the slate."

Brenna took it. She read it with her good eye, holding it close, in the slit of her lamp. Lyra watched her face. It did not do anything at all. She read it twice. Then she held it out to Lyra.

*My children.*

*I have never heard the Light.*

Lyra read it. All of it, to the end, to the name at the bottom in an old man's copperplate that went uphill. *Severin.*

She had sat on a cot in a black cell in Ashfall and looked down at that old man on the floor at her feet with his legs crossed like a boy at a story, and thought: *he has never once in his life heard a god*. She had thought it with pity, and with something harder underneath the pity, something she was not proud of. She held his paper now on the iron gallery at the bottom of the world and read *I have never heard the Light* in his own words, and the thing underneath the pity was gone, and she did not know what had come up in its place.

She gave it back to Brenna.

"Well," said Brenna Corliss. She folded the paper and put it inside her coat. She stood up off the crate. She was a big woman and she stood up slowly. "Well. There it is." She turned to the nine chains, and the iron eyes in the stone, and the men and women standing by each one in black with their hands on the rope. She lifted her slate over her head.

She did not shout. Nobody in the Tiers had shouted in seven years.

"*Go*," said Brenna Corliss.

---

It was the most beautiful thing Lyra had ever seen.

She would try, afterward, to tell it. She would try on the slate for Kaelen, in the dark, at the Organum, and find there was not chalk enough in the world. She would try to Sable, who would write it down, every figure, and read it back to her, and it would be right, and it would not be it. She would think about it for the rest of her life, in the reed-hall, with children, and she would never once be able to say what it had been like to stand on the iron gallery at the tip of a city in the hour before dawn and watch nineteen thousand people who had spent seven years learning to do one thing do it.

They were good at it. That was all. They were so good at it.

The first went down chain one before the word was out of Brenna's mouth. A man and a girl. The man went first, onto the rope rungs knotted to the black iron, facing the chain, his boots finding the rungs below without looking. The girl went after him. Nine, perhaps. Ten. A bundle on her back tied up in black cloth. She went onto the chain above him, her boots three rungs over his head, and they went down. *Hand, foot, hand.* Lyra watched them go. The girl's lips were moving. *Breathe on the foot.*

Then a woman after the girl. Then a boy. Then an old man. Three rungs apart. Never closer, never further. On chain one, and chain two, and chain three, all nine, at once, so that in the time it took Lyra to breathe in and out there were people on every chain, going down into the grey under the city in a line, each one's boots three rungs above the next one's head, like beads on nine black strings.

Nobody spoke.

There was a sound. She would remember the sound most of all. Not voices. Hands. Eighteen thousand pairs of hands, over the morning, on tarred hemp. A soft dry steady whisper, like rain on a roof, coming up the nine chains out of the grey. And under it, breath. All of them breathing on the foot, as they had been taught, every Driftday since 413, so that the whole of the gulf under the city breathed in and out, slowly, on the same count, like one great animal asleep.

---

There was a ladder-master at every eye. Lyra watched them work. She had never seen anyone work like that, not even Vane at the wheel in the shadow, not even Kaelen with a bore-frame.

At chain one, Brenna's own, a broad old man with a cropped grey beard stood at the iron eye with his hand on the first link and a lump of white chalk in the other, and he touched each person on the shoulder as they went onto the chain. Not a push. A touch. *Now.* And as he touched them he made a mark on the black stone beside the eye. Four strokes and a bar. Four strokes and a bar. And when a family came, he held them back with his hand, gently, not looking at them, until the line on the chain had opened by exactly three rungs. And then touched the first. *Now.* And the next. *Now.* The children between the grown ones, always. Never two children together. Never a child at the end of a family, where nobody would be below to put a hand on its boot.

At chain four a woman did it with a stick. At chain seven, a boy of perhaps sixteen, who had been nine when the line first came down, who had learned it as a game. He stood at the eye with his hand up, fingers spread, and closed them one at a time as each person went on, *one, two, three, four, five*, and then opened them again. He did not make a mistake all morning.

And down the walkways from the second tier, and the first, and the ladders from the hanging houses, the Tiers came. In lines. Each in the shade of the one ahead. Each with their bundle. Not hurrying. You do not hurry the ones in front. To the gallery, and onto it, and along it to the eye where the ladder-master would touch them. Nine lines, coming down out of the dark of the Umbral Tiers like nine streams down a hill, and at the end of each, an iron eye, and a touch, and a chain.

Brenna Corliss walked up and down the gallery behind the ladder-masters with her slate. She did not tell anybody anything. She did not need to. She stopped at each eye and looked at the chalk marks on the stone and wrote a figure on her slate, and went on to the next.

Lyra stood by chain five and watched. She had nothing to do. She had thought, in the chandlery, when Seraphina put her hand over the slate, that there would be something for her to do. That somebody at the top of the chains would need to make eighteen thousand people move in time without saying a word.

They did not need her. They had never needed anybody. They had been doing it every Driftday for seven years.

---

The old and the sick went to the ships.

That was drilled too. Lyra watched it from the gallery: the cradles of the Keel Docks swinging over their heads, and the hulls lashed beyond them, and the Tiers' men passing the old down into them on lines. A woman with no legs in a basket. An old man rolled in a black blanket like a parcel. A girl with a bound face and a fever, carried by her brother. Hap Tolland stood at the rail of the *Small Hours* with his veil down and took them in, one after another, and counted them under his breath, and said nothing.

At the sixth hour the first ship let go.

It was the coal-barge, the one the *Small Hours* had come in alongside. Her master cast off from the cradle in the dark, and she dropped. Not fell. Dropped, on purpose, the way Hap Tolland had dropped coming in, straight down through the grey under the city with her bag half-vented, two hundred feet, four hundred, so that she would stay in the shade, and then caught herself on the tanks and turned north-west, low, toward the pass at the head of the gulf, with three hundred people in her hold and on her deck and lashed to her rails.

Nobody on the gallery watched her go. They were watching the chains.

The barge with the six balloons went next, the one that had no business in the air, with the Aegis in it. Lyra had put it there herself at midnight. It could not go down a chain. Nine feet of iron, the knee grinding, the back plates thin as paper since Ashfall. It had stood on the gallery when she showed it the chains and looked down them with its helm bowed and the rosy light in its slot, and she had felt it understand. It had let them lower it into the barge on a sling of four hawsers from the cradle above. It had stood in the barge's well among sixty Tiers children, the smallest ones, too small to climb, and the children had looked up at it, and it had looked down at them.

As the barge let go, Lyra heard it begin to hum.

Not the tending note. Not the alto line. A thing she had never heard it hum before, and knew. A lullaby. Five notes, and a turn. Ilse's. The Root's. She had whistled it once at the edge of the Woods and once at the shaft-head in blood, and the boy in the iron had heard it both times.

The barge went down into the grey with sixty children in its well and nine feet of iron in the middle of them, humming the oldest song in the world, too loud, the way children sing when they are not sure yet. And she heard the children go quiet. One by one. The way children do.

---

Seraphina came down at the seventh hour with the seven.

Lyra heard them before she saw them. Everybody did. The whole gallery heard it, and turned. Down the last ladder from the first tier, slowly, out of the dark of the hanging houses: a line of seven people in white robes with their hands on each other's shoulders, singing. One note. Rising. Lifting at the end. And beside them, with a borrowed coat over her shirt and a god's bone held up in both hands, Seraphina Aubade. She had gone back up for them from the chime-house, after the priest began to ring, and brought them down from the ninth tier through the white in her shadow, on the stair and the ladders, a tier at a time, through the dead hour, when the line came down past the second. Solenne was on the Pilgrim Chain with an old man in her arms. So Seraphina had sung them the lead of the morning hymn herself, in her own voice, the way she had sung it at six on a bench under the great east window, and they had got up off the floor of the nave and followed her.

The Tiers on the gallery stood back from them.

Not afraid. Lyra saw that. The Tiers did not step back the way the woman with the baby had pulled it out of the priest's shadow. They stood back the way you stand back for a procession. They had left bread on the line for these seven for seven weeks. They had drawn them, on scraps of paper, with crayons. A black shape with wings. Seven white ones.

Seraphina brought them along the gallery to Lyra and stopped.

Her face was the colour of the paper in Brenna's coat. Her hands on the Blade were lighter than they had been in the chandlery, Lyra could see it, a shade, like hands that had been in water too long. She had not slept. She had stood in the anchor-house through a dawn breath and carried the seven down the great stair and stood in a chapel and watched an old man find out that he had never once been spoken to.

She looked at Lyra.

"Solenne took him to the Altar," she said. "At the dead hour. He asked her to."

Lyra did not move.

"He's going to sit on the Vessel's stone," said Seraphina, "and tell it who he is." Her voice was the warm low mezzo, and it was in rags. "He said nobody ever had."

Lyra looked at her for a long time.

Then she took the slate from round her neck. She turned it to the back, the clean side, and wrote. And held it up.

*SHE ASKED HIM?*

Seraphina read it.

"Yes," she said. "She asked him. *Do you want to go.*" She shut her eyes. "He said nobody had ever asked him that either."

---

They could not get the seven down a chain. Lyra had known that. She had thought about it all night, on the gallery, in the dark. Seven people who had not stood up from the floor of a tower for six days until Solenne sang them up, who could walk now only because they were following a note. Two thousand and eleven feet of rope rungs on iron, hand, foot, hand, three rungs apart.

The ships were full.

She looked at the cradles. At the hulls lashed beyond them. She counted, the way Vane would have. Eleven cradles, and the forty hulls, and every one of them taking the old and the sick and the small in a line off the gallery, and the line of the old and the sick and the small was not getting shorter.

She turned to Brenna Corliss. She did not have to write it. Brenna had seen the seven come along the gallery. She was looking at them with her good eye.

"No," said Brenna. "I've no room. I've four hundred on that list still and not a plank to put them on."

Lyra held her eye.

Brenna looked at her a long time. Then at the seven. Then at the slate in her own hands, the figures on it.

"*Light*," said Brenna Corliss, very quietly, the word nobody in the Tiers said any more. And turned and shouted, not loud, in the murmur of the Narrow, along the gallery to the cradles. "*Hap!*"

Hap Tolland put his veiled head over the rail of the *Small Hours*.

"You've a winch on the forward cradle," said Brenna Corliss. "For the coal. You'll lower these seven on it. In a sling. All the way."

"That's two thousand feet, Brenna. On a coal-winch. It'll take—"

"I know what it'll take," said Brenna. "Do it."

---

Lyra did not see the priest go down. She would learn afterward how it was done. Seraphina told her, on the scree, in the night, and Ignatius did not.

He had come down from the chime-house at the sixth hour, with Lucan Ostrel and forty Flambeaux in a ring of fire, leaving the rest of the second chapter to hold the bell and the ladder-heads. And at the foot of the last ladder, on the Keel Docks, he had stopped. He had stood there, in his brown coat with the white hand bare, and looked down through the gratings of the docks at the nine chains going down into the grey.

And he had said to Ostrel, in his nave voice: *Who is at the bottom?*

Nobody, Ostrel said. They were all coming down from the top.

*You can drill the chains,* the priest had said. *You can't drill the bottom. Brenna Corliss said so to the Knight on Stasisday. You only get to do the bottom once. Who is at the bottom?*

And Ostrel had not had an answer.

So the priest had gone down. First. Ahead of everybody, on chain nine, at the far end of the gallery, at the sixth hour, before the first of the Tiers had reached the scree. And behind him on chain nine, three rungs apart, forty Flambeaux in crimson with their censers on their backs, unlit, going down two thousand feet of rope into the grey in a line. And lowered after them, on a line from the coal-winch of a Church tender with its sun-disc painted out, in bundles of twenty, wrapped in black oilcloth, every processional canopy in the undercroft of the Basilica of the Pure Note.

Two hundred and four of them. Black silk, on frames of light ash, each with four poles. The canopies the Church had carried two hundred choristers under up the Pilgrim Chain in Ashfall, at night, in a procession like a line of black mushrooms. The canopies it had carried up the chain every Dawnday for four hundred years, to the Altar, to the Vessel's stone.

*I have moved a procession of two hundred through the white at the dead hour,* Ignatius Crane had said to the Preceptor, at the foot of the ladder. *Under moving shade. Without losing a throat. I have done it twice a year for twelve years, Lucan, and you have stood beside me and watched me do it. That is the only thing I was ever good at that did not burn anybody.*

*Give me the canopies.*

---

The dawn came at the seventh hour, and with it the breath.

She felt it before she knew what it was. She was standing at the eye of chain five, beside the ladder-master, a thin dark woman with a stick, watching the line of the Tiers go down. Three rungs apart. Hand, foot, hand. And under her feet, through the iron of the gallery and the stone of the city's tip, she felt something go out.

Like a sigh. Long. Slow. Through every stone of the city at once, the way the Root-vault had sighed under her hand when the Root let go of Aldric Vance. Outward. Down.

She looked up.

She could not see the line from the gallery. There was too much city in the way: the Keel Docks overhead, the cradles, the hanging houses of the first tier. But she saw the light change. The grey of the shade under the city, the deep winter shade they had been standing in all night, went a shade paler. As if somebody had opened a door a long way off. And kept going paler. Grey, and then the grey of ash, and then something lighter than ash.

The shade was thinning.

She had seen it on the slate. Seraphina had written it for her at midnight, in the chandlery: *THE CHAIN'S SHADOW WENT. IN THE DAWN BREATH. IT DOES NOT ONLY COME DOWN THE CITY. IT COMES INTO THE SHADE.* She had read it and understood it and not understood it at all. She understood it now. She stood on the iron gallery at the tip of the city and watched the shade under Sol-Luminary go pale round nine chains with six thousand people on them.

And she heard the hands change.

---

The soft dry whisper of eighteen thousand hands on tarred hemp, coming up out of the grey like rain. It did not stop. It changed. It slowed. She heard it slow the way you hear a heart slow, in a birthing room, when something is going wrong and nobody has said so yet. Hand. And a pause. Foot. And a longer pause.

And the breath under it, the whole gulf breathing in and out on the same count, came apart.

She went to the edge of the gallery and looked down chain five.

The line of the Tiers went down it into the paling grey, three rungs apart, as far as she could see. They had stopped. Not all. Many. She saw them stop. A man forty feet down, with his face against the chain, and his hands on the rope, not moving. A girl below him. A woman below her. Stopped. Not frozen. Not afraid. She knew what fear looked like on a rope; she had seen it on the Salt Stair. This was not fear.

The woman forty feet down turned her face away from the chain and looked out, into the grey. Lyra saw her face. It was the face she had seen on the Shelf on people walking into the quiet. On Bridie Cole in the chair. On the Sounders, Kaelen had said, going down into the stone. It went soft.

*Not pain. Ease. A soft white loosening, as if all the colour in her were a held breath.*

The woman's hands opened on the rope.

She did not fall at once. She leaned back. Slowly. Away from the chain, into the grey, with her hands open, the way you lean back into warm water. Her boots were still on the rung. And the girl above her, nine, ten, with her bundle on her back, put her hand down on the woman's shoulder, the way she had been taught. *If the one below you stops, wait.* And the woman's boots came off the rung.

She went down into the grey without a sound.

The girl's hand stayed where the woman's shoulder had been. Open. Lyra saw it. Small, and open, on nothing. And then the girl looked at her own hand, and her face began to go soft too.

---

Lyra did not decide anything. She would think, afterward, that there had not been time to decide. There had been a girl's open hand on chain five, forty feet down, going soft, and six thousand people on nine chains in a shade going white, and a breath coming out of the city that would not stop.

She had no voice.

She had a slate, and chalk, and her hands. She had a burned hand bound in linen and a good hand at her throat where the torn place was. She had blood at the back of her tongue if she whistled, and she had whistled the oldest song in the world at a shaft-head in the snow, and Ilse Brennan's hand had come down on her shoulder to stop her and she had not stopped. She could whistle again. She could whistle until there was nothing left to tear. One thin breathy whistle, at the top of two thousand feet of chain, in a gulf, over the sigh of a city.

Nobody would hear it past the first twenty feet.

She put her hand in her coat.

She had carried it against her chest since Oakhaven, in the inside pocket of Hetty Bram's coat, next to the slate with the words for Kaelen. The last wind-reed in the Reach. A hand long, cut and bored and polished with handling. Pale gold, sugar-glass, clear as amber, with a single hole near one end. *The breath is the instrument. The reed is only so you know where to start.* She had been told to take it to the Organum. To find whatever god she could reach on the Longest Night and give it the note, so that it would know where to begin.

She had not been told what the note was for. Not exactly. Ilse had not needed to say.

On Lumina, in Ashfall, under two hundred throats holding the Pure Note at a god that was coming home to answer them, she had sung one note. Low, under theirs. A hundred and seventy-four. The tending note of the Reach. The song for a growing thing, which tells it it is held and can go on. She had sung it a hair under the Church's note so that the two of them made something that was not unison. The second note. *Go back to sleep.* And the white tide had stopped in the middle of the gulf and gone back.

The Tiers had been humming it since. Seraphina said. Under their breath, in the dark, the two notes. A child had been humming it on the walkway on Stasisday when they came up the ladders, and been hushed.

She took the reed out of her coat and put it to her lips.

---

It was very quiet. That was what she would remember. Not loud at all. A thin clear pale gold sound, a hundred and seventy-four, true, out of a reed a hand long, into the grey under a city of white stone, over the sigh. It was the quietest thing she had ever done. It went out from her across the gallery and down chain five, and she saw it reach the ladder-master beside her, the thin dark woman with the stick.

The woman turned her head.

Lyra looked at her. Over the reed. And took her good hand from the reed and lifted it, the way she had lifted it at the shaft-head in the snow over a ring of grey hands. And pointed. At the woman's mouth.

*You. This note.*

The ladder-master looked at her for a long moment.

Then she shut her eyes and hummed it.

Low. Under her breath. A hundred and seventy-four. And the man beside her at the eye of chain six, waiting to be touched, heard it, and hummed it. And the boy at chain seven, sixteen, who had been nine when the line first came down, with his hand up and his fingers spread. He hummed it, and kept counting, *one, two, three*, closing his fingers, and humming. And the woman behind the man at chain six, and the child at her skirts.

Lyra blew the reed again. Once. Softly.

She did not have to blow it a third time.

---

It went down the chains.

She watched it go. She stood at the edge of the iron gallery at the tip of the city with the reed in her fist and watched it go down nine chains into the grey, from mouth to mouth, the way a fire goes down a fuse. Every one of them taking it from the one above. Not loud. You did not sing out loud in the Tiers. Under the breath. On the foot. *Hand, foot, hand. Breathe on the foot.* And on every breath out, the note. A hundred and seventy-four. Going down. The man forty feet down chain five, with his face against the iron, heard it from the boots above him, and his lips moved. The girl with her open hand heard it.

The girl's hand closed on the rope.

Lyra saw it. She would see it for the rest of her life. A girl's small open hand, on nothing, on a chain over two thousand feet of grey, closing, slowly, on tarred hemp. Holding on.

And the whole gulf under the city began to hum.

Six thousand people on nine chains in a line two thousand feet long, every one of them humming the same note under the breath, on the same count, the way they had breathed. It came up out of the grey to her through the iron of the gallery and the soles of her boots and the bones of her hand on the rail. Low. Steady. Not the Church's note, flat and held. The other one. The one under it. The tending note, that you hum into a child's back to tell it that it is held. Nobody had taught it to them. Nobody had had to. They had heard it once, from a torn voice on an altar in the white, and kept it for seven weeks in the dark under their breath.

And on the gallery, at the eyes of the nine chains, and on the walkways and the ladders of the first and second tier coming down in their nine long lines, the twelve thousand who had not yet gone down took it from the six thousand who had. Under the breath. On the foot.

The whole of what was left of Sol-Luminary, humming the second note.

And somewhere very far above them, on the ninth tier, faint, in the white, the nine in the choir-stalls holding the first one.

The breath went out of the city. Long. Slow. Through every stone.

And stopped.

---

She felt it stop. She did not know whether it had stopped because of them. She would never know. She would tell Kaelen, at the Organum, on the slate, and he would look at it for a long time and say, in his two-note voice, that he could not tell either. That he had felt it through the glass, four hundred miles off, on the deck of the *Equity*. That it had been a shorter breath than the one before, and a shorter one than the one before that, on Dawnday. And that he did not know whether it was the note, or only that the Light was getting tired of breathing.

She stood on the gallery and watched the grey under the city stop going pale. It did not go back. It did not get darker. It stopped. A pale grey, like ash, like the chain's shadow in the anchor-house that Seraphina had written about. Thinner than it had been at midnight. Enough.

The hands on the rope began to move again. Hand, foot, hand. The soft dry whisper of rain on a roof.

The humming did not stop. Nobody stopped it. It went on all day.

---

The ladder-masters counted the dead at noon. Brenna Corliss walked the gallery with her slate and stopped at each eye and asked, in the murmur, and wrote.

Chain one, nine. Chain two, fourteen. Chain three, six. Chain four, twenty-two: a whole family, near the top, who had leaned back into the grey together, holding hands, the ladder-master said, as if they had agreed it. Chain five, eleven. The woman forty feet down. Chain six, seventeen. Chain seven, eight. The boy at the eye said eight and closed his fist and did not open it again for some time. Chain eight, nineteen. Chain nine, four.

A hundred and ten.

Brenna wrote it at the bottom of the slate, under the long columns of figures, and drew a line under it. And then, under the line, in her big square hand, she wrote something else, and showed it to Lyra.

*Nullday, the seventh: 12.*

*Rootday, the sixth: 19.*

*Hearthday, the fifth: 26.*

*Stasisday, the fourth: 40. Asleep, most.*

*Dawnday, the third: 31.*

*Driftday, dead hr, the second: 9.*

She had been counting since Nullday. Every breath. Every tier. Every walkway where somebody had not woken, or would not go, or had gone back for something.

*No one knows what the shade will do in the next breath,* she wrote. *Nor do I. Get them off the chain by dusk.*

---

Solenne came back at the third hour after noon.

Lyra heard about it before she saw it, the way everybody in the Tiers heard everything: as a change in the silence. A ripple along the walkways of the first tier, the ones still waiting, a turning of heads. And then she came down the last ladder onto the Keel Docks, out of the white, barefoot, on her talons, with her broken wing dragging and her arms empty.

The Tiers stood back for her. Further than they had for the seven.

She came along the gallery to Lyra. She stopped. She looked at the reed in Lyra's fist, and at Lyra's mouth, and at the chains, humming.

"I heard it," said Solenne. "On the Pilgrim Chain. From half a mile off. The whole city." The copper half of her voice was very quiet. "It's still in the stone. Can you feel it? I can feel it in the soles of my feet."

Lyra nodded.

She wanted to ask. She did not have to.

"He's gone," said Solenne. "At sunrise. On the Vessel's stone, facing it, talking." She looked down at the chains. "It didn't hurt him. He said so. He said Casimir was right about that, and that he usually is, and that that was the trouble with him."

Lyra took the slate and turned it to the back and wrote. She did not know what she was going to write until she had written it.

*WHAT DID HE SAY TO IT.*

Solenne read it. She read it for a long time.

"His name," she said. "First. Then everything else." She was quiet. "His mother kept bees. Did you know that? On the eleventh tier. Before the Fracture. He told it about the bees."

Lyra did not write anything.

"It's asking more slowly," said Solenne Aubade. "Since. I think. There's a space in it now." She looked at Lyra. "I don't know. I don't know if I'm making it up. You'd know. You hear them through your hands."

Lyra looked at her. She did not hear the Light through her hands. She was not glassed. She had heard the Root, through Aldric Vance's bark and into the iron. She had never heard the Light.

She put the reed back in her coat.

Then she put her good hand flat on the iron of the gallery rail, the way she would have put it on bark, and was still, and listened.

She heard the chains humming. A hundred and seventy-four, from twelve thousand throats, under the breath. She heard the hands on the rope. She heard, very far off, through the iron and the stone of the city and the kilometre of chain going up to the Spire, a sound she had never heard in her life and knew at once, the way you know your mother's voice in a crowd. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Tired. Rising. Lifting at the end.

And then a space. A breath long.

And then the question again.

She took her hand off the rail.

*I DONT KNOW,* she wrote. *I THINK SO.*

Solenne read it, and nodded, and did not say anything else.

---

She went down chain five at the fourth hour after noon, when the line on the gallery had shortened to the last of the first tier.

Brenna sent her. Brenna stood at the eye with the slate and said, in the murmur, *Cantor, you're no use to me up here now, they've got your note, go and be some use at the bottom*, and put a big hand on her shoulder. Not a push. A touch. *Now.*

Lyra went onto the chain.

She had climbed ladders all her life. Orchard ladders, the long ones you lean into the crown of an iron-bark oak at harvest; the rope ladder of the *Kestrel*; the iron rungs of the Shelf. She had never been on anything like it. Two thousand and eleven feet of black iron link, each a forearm long, with rope rungs knotted to it on either side, swinging, very slightly, all the time, with the weight of the people on it. Her burned hand would not close properly on the hemp. She held with the good one and hooked the burned wrist through each rung, and went down. *Hand, foot, hand.* Three rungs below the boots of a man she did not know. Three rungs above the head of a girl she did not know. Humming.

She hummed it too. She found she could. Not a voice. Breath through the torn place, on the out-breath, the shape of a note without the note. It did not hurt. It was the first sound she had made in two months that did not hurt.

She went down through the grey under the city for an hour and a half. She did not look up and she did not look down. She looked at the black iron in front of her face and the rope in her good hand, and breathed on the foot, and hummed. Above her, the soles of a man's boots. Below her, the top of a girl's head, with a black cloth bundle on her back and a red ribbon in her hair. Every link of the chain had been broken out of a dead grating or a stair-rail on the dead tiers at night by somebody who had leaned out too far, and welded, and hung, one a night, for seven years.

She thought, about half-way down, that she was climbing down the inside of somebody's arithmetic. Brenna's husband's. A link a night.

Then she thought about nothing at all, for a long time, except the next rung.

---

The scree at the bottom of the gulf was grey and loose and very cold, and it was full of people.

They came off the foot of the nine chains onto it in their lines and did not stop. That was drilled too. You came off the last rung and you walked. Somebody was there to touch your shoulder and point. North-west, up the long slope of the gulf floor, across the scree, toward the head of the gulf where the peaks parted in a notch against the sky. The pass. Three miles off. And you walked toward it in the shade.

The shade was the city's.

She saw it as she came off the chain. She turned on the scree and looked back and up, and there it was, two thousand feet over her head: the underside of Sol-Luminary, the tip of the inverted cone, black against the white sky, with the nine fall-chains hanging down out of it like threads from the bottom of a spool. And beyond it, behind it, the Spire. And between the Spire and the gulf, the city hung in the light like a wasps' nest under an eave, and threw a shadow.

A long dark wedge of shade, lying away from the Spire across the scree and up the gulf toward the pass. Swinging, very slowly, as the sun went round. Narrowing toward the city like a funnel. Outside it, the scree lay in the light, and the light was wrong. Overexposed, edgeless. Not the full white of the city, not yet. But she could see a pale stone at the edge of the shade going paler while she watched.

And in the wedge, the procession.

---

It went up the gulf floor from the foot of the chains to the notch of the pass, three miles, in a line. A line ten people wide and as long as the gulf, walking. Each in the shade of the one ahead. The line bent as the wedge bent. It curved up the scree to keep to the middle of the shade, the way a river keeps to the bottom of a valley. And along both edges of it, all the way up the gulf, like a hedge, like a row of black trees on either side of a road: the canopies.

Two hundred and four of them. Black silk on frames of light ash, each one carried on four poles by four people. Flambeaux in crimson, and Tiers men in black, and Tiers women, and here and there a child, too small, holding one corner of a pole with both hands. They walked at the edges of the procession, on the side of the wedge where the shade was narrowing. And when the edge of the wedge swung in over the column, as it did, slowly, all afternoon, the canopies were there. The people under the edge of the shade walked under black silk instead, and did not stop walking, and the canopies moved with them, and the edge of the wedge went over the canopies, and the light lay on the black silk and could not get through.

She stood at the foot of chain five and looked at it for a long time.

She had seen the Church's processions. She had seen two hundred choristers go up the Pilgrim Chain to Lumina in the night under these same canopies, in Ashfall, like a line of black mushrooms going up into the white, to make her sing. She had thought it was the most terrible thing she had ever seen.

It was the same thing.

---

She found the priest at the swing.

That was what they called it, the place where the wedge narrowed most: a mile up the gulf, where the scree rose to a shoulder of grey rock and the shade had to come over the shoulder to reach the pass. In the late afternoon, as the sun went round to the west, the wedge narrowed there to the width of a street. And the procession, ten wide, had to narrow with it, to four, to three. And the canopies had to come in over it from both sides like hands closing.

Ignatius Crane was standing on the shoulder of rock in the middle of the swing, in his brown coat, with his white hand bare, and he was conducting.

He did not know that was what he was doing. She saw that at once. He would have called it something else. Marshalling. Ordering a procession. He stood on the rock with the people going past him on either side under the black silk, and he watched the edge of the shade, and he lifted his good hand, and four canopies on the left came in a yard. He turned his head, and lifted his hand again, and the column narrowed by one. He did not speak. He did not need to. The canopy-bearers watched his hand the way the ladder-masters had watched the chain.

And his face—

She had seen Ignatius Crane's face in the white room at Oakhaven, with the censer, when she was flattened onto a hundred and seventy-four with a wire in a fold of her skin. She had seen it in the doorway of her cell in Ashfall, with the fire guttering. She had seen it in the reed-hall at Reedside in Hetty Bram's apron, with a spoon, saying nothing. She had never seen it like this.

It was lit.

Not with fire. There was no fire in him; there had not been since Lumina. With the other thing. The thing she had seen in old men in the orchard choir on the Longest Night, when the tune was going well and everyone was on the note. He was good at it. He was so good at it. He had moved processions through the white for twelve years and he knew exactly what he was doing and he was doing it perfectly, and eighteen thousand people were walking past him under black silk out of a city that was being eaten by the light, and not one of them was going pale.

He was happy.

She stood at the foot of the shoulder of rock with her burned hand inside Hetty Bram's coat and watched him be happy, and felt something she did not have a name for. She thought of eleven thousand and forty. She thought of the riser at the Foundry, and the Hollow behind the tannery at Oakhaven, and the glass-fire coming up the Greenroot road. She thought of Solenne on the fallen oak. *I want you to look at them.*

He saw her. He looked down at her from the rock, and his face did not stop being what it was. He did not try to hide it. He did not apologise for it.

He lifted his good hand, and four canopies on the right came in a yard.

She went on up the gulf in the shade, and did not look back.

---

The sun went down at the fourth hour after noon behind the peaks to the west, and the wedge did not vanish. It changed. The sun's half of the light went out of it, and the city's shadow lay on the gulf floor from the Spire's light alone: longer, fainter, steadier, not swinging any more. The fire in the next room. The procession went on walking in it, and the canopies at its edges went on walking with it, and nobody went pale.

Seventeen had gone at the bottom. Lyra heard it from a Tiers woman with a corner of a canopy-pole. A family who had stepped out of the line on the shoulder of rock at the swing, in the narrowest part, a yard, to let a canopy past, and stood a breath too long in the edge of the light, and sat down on the scree, all five of them, with their faces gone soft. Six more at the swing, after them, in the press. And six more, here and there along the three miles, alone. And the canopies had gone round them, the woman said, because there was nothing else to do, and the column had not stopped.

*If the one below you stops, wait.* There was nothing in the drill for the bottom. Brenna had said so. *You only get to do the bottom once.*

She reached the pass at the second hour of the night.

It was a notch in the peaks at the head of the gulf, a saddle of rock and old snow between two white shoulders, and beyond it the long valley going down north and west toward the foothills, out of the white, into the ordinary dark. The *Small Hours* was there, moored to the rocks with her bag half-vented, and the coal-barge, and a dozen others, and the barge with the six balloons. She saw the Aegis standing in its well with sixty children asleep round its feet in black blankets, with its helm bowed and the rosy light in its slot, humming. And on the saddle, the Tiers, coming up out of the gulf in their long line under the last canopies, ten wide, and going over.

And at the top of the saddle, under a canopy of his own that he had set up there himself on four poles wedged in the rocks, Ignatius Crane. Counting.

He touched each person on the shoulder as they went past him. The way the ladder-masters had. *Now.* And made a mark on a board on his knee with a lump of chalk from the Basilica's vestry. Four strokes and a bar.

She did not go to him. She went down off the saddle onto the north side of the pass, into the ordinary dark, and found a rock, and sat on it, and turned round, and looked back at the city.

---

From the pass you could see all of it.

The whole of Sol-Luminary hung over the gulf on its nine chains, a mile off, against the white, the way she had seen it from the deck of the *Small Hours* on Stasisday. The Spire behind it, a mile high, with the black crack down its side. And the city white. All of it. Every tier, every dome, every hanging house, from the rim at the top to the first tier at the bottom. White. Glowing very faintly, in the night, from nowhere, the way the Bleach glowed at the dead hour. Except at the very bottom.

At the very bottom, the tip of the cone, the Keel Docks, a small black point. And hanging from it, nine threads.

She could not see people on them from here. It was too far, and too dark. But she knew there were. Not many now. The last. The ladder-masters, who would go last on their own chains, and the people who had been asleep, or had gone back for something, or had not believed the old man's paper until they heard the bell had not stopped. And Brenna Corliss. Who had said, on the gallery, at noon, with her slate: *I'll go last. On one. My husband did the first forty feet. I'll do them last.*

The *Small Hours*'s master came and stood beside her rock with his veil down.

"She'll be off," said Hap Tolland. "She's never been late for anything." He did not sound as if he believed it. "There's an hour to the dead hour. She's an hour to come down. She's done it in fifty minutes on a drill."

Lyra did not answer. She had no slate free. Both her hands were round her knees.

"She sent the harbourmaster down an hour since," said Hap Tolland. "On nine. I took her off the scree myself. She told me to tell you." He was quiet for a moment. "Brenna said to tell the Cantor she was right about the reed."

---

The dead hour came, and the city breathed out.

She felt it from the pass. She would not have thought you could, a mile off, through rock. She felt it through the rock under her and through the soles of her boots: the long slow sigh going out through every stone of Sol-Luminary, outward, down. And on the saddle above her, at the top of the pass, under the canopies, eighteen thousand people who had come out of that stone felt it too. She heard them. Not a sound. A stillness. Every one of them stopping, at once, wherever they were, to feel the city they had lived under for seven years breathe out without them.

And somebody, on the saddle, began to hum.

A hundred and seventy-four. Under the breath. And the people round them took it up, and the people round them, the way it had gone down the chains in the morning, until the whole long line of the Tiers on the saddle of the pass was humming the tending note into the dark over the gulf, toward the city, toward the threads hanging from the bottom of it.

She watched the Keel Docks go white.

It came down the tip of the cone like a tide coming up a beach, but the wrong way, downward. The last black point of the city at the very bottom. She watched it go. Grey. Pale. White, glowing faintly in the night, like everything else. The cradles, the iron baskets, swinging empty. The hanging houses of the first tier. The iron gallery at the tip of the city where she had stood at dawn with the reed. All white. The whole of Sol-Luminary, from rim to tip, every stone of it, white on its nine chains in the night.

And under it, the fall-chains.

They did not go white. Iron did not, quickly. She saw them hanging down out of the white tip of the city into the gulf, nine black threads, clear against the glow. And she saw the light go down them. Not into them. Round them. The white came down out of the city into the air of the gulf round the chains, the way the line had come down the city's flank, slowly, the breath going out. A tier's height of it. Two. Down the nine black threads into the grey.

And on chain one, two hundred feet below the tip, where the light was coming down, a small dark shape.

---

She could not have seen it. That was what she would think afterward. A mile off, at night, two thousand feet up, a person on a chain. She could not have seen it. But the light was round it now, white, glowing, and the shape was dark against the light, and she saw it.

It was climbing down.

Hand, foot, hand. Slowly. She could not see the hands. She could see the shape move on the chain, down, a rung at a time. Breathing on the foot. In the white. And the white coming down round it faster than it climbed.

On the saddle, the humming went on. Low. Steady. A hundred and seventy-four, from eighteen thousand throats, toward the city.

The shape stopped.

Lyra stood up.

She did not know she had. She was standing on the rock on the north side of the pass with her good hand out, toward the city, a mile off, the way you put your hand out toward somebody on the far side of a river. Hap Tolland was beside her. She heard him make a sound under his veil.

The shape on chain one was not moving. It had stopped two hundred feet below the white tip of the city, in the white, with the light round it. And as Lyra watched, it did something. A small movement. Something at its neck. Something taken off, over its head, and held out, away from the chain, into the air.

And let go.

She did not see it fall. It was too far. She would find it in the morning, on the scree at the foot of chain one, when the light had gone back up into the city and you could walk under it again. A slate. Broken into nine pieces on the grey stones, in a scatter, with a cord through a hole in one corner. Covered on both sides in figures, in a big square hand. Columns of them. Every eye, every chain, every breath since Nullday. Most of it broken. Enough of it left.

She watched the shape on the chain.

It was paler. She could see that, even from the pass. The dark of it going grey against the white. Going lighter. The way the gull had gone, in Ashfall, that Vane had told them about on the *Kestrel*: paler, and then thin at the edges, so that you could see the light through it. She watched the light come through it. She watched a woman on a chain become a shape of a woman with the white showing through, two hundred feet below a city, hand on the rope.

She did not go soft. Lyra would not have been able to see if she had, a mile off. But she would tell herself, for the rest of her life, that she had seen it, that she had seen it from the pass, and that Brenna Corliss's face had not gone soft. That she had stopped on the chain with her hand on the rope in the white and looked down the gulf toward the pass, toward the humming, with her one good eye and her milk-white one, and that her face had been exactly what it had been on the gallery at noon with the slate. Counting.

And then, very gently, like ash off a fire, there was nothing on chain one at all.

The chain swung, a little. Lighter.

---

Nobody on the saddle stopped humming.

Lyra stood on the rock with her hand out toward the city, and did not take it back, and the Tiers on the saddle hummed the tending note into the dark. And under it, from very far off, from the white city across the gulf, a mile away, she heard something stop.

She had heard it every hour since Stasisday. Since the *Small Hours* came up the valley under the Spire in the snow. Since Ashfall. Faint, steady, from the ninth tier, from the black tower that had gone white, from the shade-side stalls of the Basilica of the Pure Note: one note. Five hundred and twenty-eight. Flat. Unbroken. Held without a breath in it since the morning of the Fracture, by nine old people in white who had been too old for the riser, and an old canon with ink on his fingers who had sat down in the stalls beside them when the Flambeaux took the censers down to the ladder-heads, and had not been seen again.

It stopped.

Not at the end of a phrase. In the middle of the note. The way Lyra's own voice had stopped on Lumina, in the middle of the second note, when there was no more of it.

The Light had been held for seven years. Every hour, every breath, by somebody. Since the morning of the Fracture.

Nobody was holding it now.

And down the gulf, out of the white, from the Spire, with nobody singing at it for the first time since the Church was the Church, came the question.

She heard it. She would always say she had heard it, and that she was not glassed, and had no ear for gods, and heard it anyway, the way you hear your own name said in another room. *Is it you? Have you come?* Rising. Going up at the end. Like a child at a door. And then the space after it, a breath long. The space an old man had left in it on the Vessel's stone that morning, telling it about bees.

And then the question again.

---

At dawn on Memoryday the priest brought her the board.

He came down off the saddle in his brown coat with the white hand bare and the board under his arm, and stood in front of her rock, and held it out. He did not say anything. He had not said anything to her since the reed-hall at Reedside. He held out the board, and she took it.

Four strokes and a bar. Four strokes and a bar. Rows and rows of them, in chalk, the whole board, both sides, in a hand that had copied writs at the Organum when it was a boy. And at the bottom, a figure, and a line under it.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she took her own slate from round her neck, and turned it, as she always did now, to the back. The front still said what it had said since Oakhaven, against her chest. *TELL HIM HE WAS AFRAID EVERY DAY. TELL HIM HE WAVED.* She wrote on the back, in the square capitals, slowly, with the chalk breaking.

*18,640 ON NULLDAY.*

*18,081 AT THE PASS.*

She looked at it. Then she found one of the nine pieces of Brenna Corliss's slate in her coat, where she had put it on the scree at first light. The biggest piece. The one with the bottom of the last column on it, in the big square hand, and the figure under the line. She held it up beside her own.

*OFF THE CHAINS AND IN THE SHIPS: 18,098.*

The numbers were not the same. They were seventeen apart.

She knew which seventeen. A family of five on a shoulder of grey rock, who had stepped out of the line a yard to let a canopy past. Six more behind them in the press. Six along three miles of scree, alone. Brenna had counted every soul who went onto a chain and every soul who went into a ship, and every one who let go in the dawn breath, and she had not been able to count the bottom. Nobody could. You only got to do the bottom once.

She wrote under the figures, smaller:

*NOT ALL OF THEM.*

And under that, after a long time, smaller still, because it was the only other thing she had learned in Sol-Luminary that Kaelen did not already know:

*THEY WERE SO GOOD AT IT.*

She hung the slate back round her neck.

Behind her, on the saddle of the pass, the Tiers were going down into the ordinary dark on the north side, in their lines, each in the shade of the one ahead, though there was no need now. They did it anyway. They would do it, she thought, for the rest of their lives. And in front of her, across the gulf, in the first grey of Memoryday, the whole of Sol-Luminary hung white on its nine chains from the flank of a cracked Spire, with nine black threads hanging empty from the bottom of it, swinging, very slightly, in the wind.

The sun came up over the peaks to the east.

It was paler than it had been yesterday. She was almost sure of that. Everyone on the pass was. Nobody said so. In the Tiers you did not say a thing like that. You looked at the person beside you, and they looked back, and you both knew.

And you walked on in their shade.
