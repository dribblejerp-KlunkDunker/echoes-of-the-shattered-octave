# Already Quiet: A Word for Those Who Are Tired

*a brother of the Quiet Faith, unsigned (he has given back his name) · c. 419 SR · the Deep Woods, copied out by hand · pamphlet*

> A hand-copied tract of the Tongueless, written for people worn out by the Narrow, inviting them into the woods.

I am writing this because I cannot say it. That is the first thing to understand about us, and it is the last. Read it slowly. Read it inside your head. You need not move your lips. You have moved your lips enough.

You are tired. I know you are tired, because I was. You have lived seven years in the Narrow. You have weighed every word you spoke against two deaths, the one that comes to loud things and the one that comes to still things, and you have spoken between them, low, all day, every day, so carefully that you no longer know what your own voice sounds like when it is not afraid. There is a lamp in your room that whistles all night so that your house is never silent, and you have not slept a night through since it was lit. You hum at crossings. You hum your children into the world through jars. You bind the jaws of your dead so that they cannot ring.

Who made the rhyme? Too loud brings the hounds, too still brings the Hush. Somebody frightened made it, and you have skipped to it ever since. I ask you to look at its two halves side by side, the way you would look at two men offering you work.

The hounds. You know what they do. Everyone in the siphoned country has heard a neighbour's voice taken at the hedge, and heard it come back afterwards out of the dark, asking for his wife. That is what loudness brings. It is what the Church brings, walking singing down your roads behind its fire, and what the Synod brings with its drills, and what your Cantors bring when they love somebody too much to keep quiet.

Now the other half. Tell me what the Quiet has done to you. Not what you were told. What you saw. You saw a house go still. You saw a kettle boil that nobody could hear. You saw a man sit down by the Flume with his hands open in his lap. Think of his face. Was it afraid? Did he scream? Has anyone, in the whole of the siphoned country, ever once heard one of them scream?

No. Nobody screams. Every one of us who has watched has said the same thing afterwards, in the same words, without being taught them: it does not hurt. They look relieved. They look like a man setting down a load at the end of a long day's carrying.

And that is the thing you were taught to be afraid of, by a rhyme, by a lamp, and by a priest.

This is what we do, and why. We cut out our own tongues, so that we cannot answer. The voices at the hedge call you by your name, in the voices of the people you loved, and the only safety the world has ever offered you against them is the rule that you must not answer. We have made it so that we cannot. No hound has ever learned my voice. None ever will. There is nothing left in me to hunt. And when the Quiet comes, as it comes to everything at last, it will find me already quiet, already its own, and it will not need to take anything from me at all. I will go with it the way you go with someone who has come to walk you home.

We do not ask you to cut. We ask you to stop being afraid of the wrong thing.

Some of you have heard what we do with the children who sing. I will tell it plainly, because you have heard it told unkindly. There are children who will not keep the Narrow. They sing; they cannot help it; it is in them the way the second note was in the old orchard-singers. In a town such a child brings the hounds to every door, and then the Church comes and burns the house for it. We burn nobody. We take the singing child out at evening, past the last lamp, into the trees, and we leave her there singing, as a gift, and we go back to our fires and we do not listen. In the morning the singing has stopped. There is no mark on the moss. Nothing has been torn. Nothing is ever torn. That is how we know she was received kindly.

Our Abbess has letters. They come a long way, sealed in plain grey with no device on the seal, in a hand that has stopped wasting ink, and a brother who kept his tongue for that purpose alone reads them to us by the fire. The one who writes them has listened longer than any of us. He writes that everything which sings is tired, all of it, everywhere, and has been tired for longer than anyone can count, and wants only to be allowed to put the weight down. He writes that the Quiet is not a death but a rest. We believed it before he wrote. Still, it was a comfort to learn that someone so far away, and so learned, had come to the same place by another road.

If you are tired, come into the woods. Not tonight. Tonight you are afraid, and fear has a pitch, and you would be heard. Come when you are tired enough that it has stopped mattering to you what hears you. You will know the night. Leave your lamp unlit behind you. Walk until the reed in the last window has faded out at your back, and keep walking, and when you can no longer hear your own feet, you will know that you are among friends.

I gave back my name when I gave back my tongue. I kept my hand only so long as it took to copy this out forty times, which is the number the Abbess asked of me. This is the fortieth. When it is finished I will put down the pen, and I will not pick anything up again.
