# The Rule of the House: Of the Keeping of Names

*the Silent Veil; copied fair by the scribe styled Umber · c. 290 SR (the Rule itself is far older) · the House of the Silent Veil, in the Spectral Woods · law*

> The Veil's rule for its scribes: why they keep names, why they never lift a hand, and what they give up to do it.

THE RULE OF THE HOUSE. Of the keeping of names, and why. To be read to every scribe on the evening before she takes the pen, and again on any evening she asks for it.

Over the door of the House it is cut in our notation: What is written is not lost. Read it as a promise and you will be disappointed. Read it as a duty and you will be a scribe.

The House keeps three rules. Write what happened. Write who it happened to. Do not comfort yourself in the margin. Everything else in this book is only those three, explained to someone tired.

OF THE FIRST RULE. Write what happened: not what was meant to happen, nor what the powers say afterwards happened, nor what you would have liked. Every kingdom is rewritten by whoever outlives it, and the rewriting begins within the year. The House is the one place in the world where the first account is kept against the second. We have outlived eight crowns doing this. We expect to outlive the Synod, which is young.

OF THE SECOND RULE. Write who it happened to. The novice thinks she understands this rule, and does not. The powers will always write that a town was lost. We write the town's names: all of them, in the order we can learn them, with the age and the trade, and the last thing said, if anyone heard it. A loss without names is weather. A loss with names is a crime, or a grief, or both, and it can be answered.

WHY THE NAME. Because the name is the last thing that belongs to a person when everything else has gone to the ground or the fire or the powers. Because the world forgets on a curve, steep at first and then slow, and every name slides down that curve, and the House stands at the bottom of it with a cup. Because a name kept is a door left on the latch. We do not know who will come through it. We keep it on the latch.

A name written in the House is held. It is not to be spent, nor lent, nor sold, nor set to any work, nor counted towards any sum but the sum of the dead. A scribe who uses a name for anything but its keeping has stopped being a scribe and become a kind of merchant, and the House has a word for that which it does not write down.

OF THE THIRD RULE. Do not comfort yourself in the margin. You will want to. You will stand in a burning town with your desk on your knees and write the names of the people going past you into the fire, and you will want to write beside them that you were sorry, that you wept, that nothing could be done. Do not. The margin is not yours. Every word of comfort you write there is a word taken from them.

OF THE STILL HAND. You will ask, as every novice asks: why do we only write? Why do we not carry water? Hear the answer the House has given for a thousand years, and then decide whether you can bear it. A scribe who carries water is one more pair of hands in the burning town, and burns with it, and then nobody writes. The powers do not fear our hands. They fear the record. A scribe who takes a side is a witness for that side, and a witness for a side can be answered. A scribe who takes none cannot. So we stand still and write, and are hated by everyone in the town, and are not answered. That is the price of the record. The scribe pays it, and she does not get to stop paying it by feeling bad. If you cannot bear it, lay down the pen tonight. There is no shame in that. There is only no place for you here.

OF THE SCRIBE'S OWN NAME. On the evening you take the pen, you give your name into the House. It is written once, in the Prior's hand, and sealed, and laid in the cabinet in the scriptorium, and kept against the day you lay the pen down, when it is given back to you at the door. Until then you are styled by a colour or a creature, as every scribe before you was. This is not unkindness. A scribe who carries her own name into a burning town will write it into every page. Leave it here. It will be safe. Nothing in the House is ever lost.

OF MEMORYDAY. On Memoryday, at dusk, the duty-scribe stands in the lower hall with a lamp and reads one page of the dead aloud, unhurried, in the voice for entries, so that they are heard. Nobody has been able to tell the House why this was begun. The page is read all the same, every eighth day, and no book we hold records a Memoryday it was missed.

Here the Rule ends. Beneath it, in a smaller hand and a browner ink:

Copied fair by the scribe styled Umber, in the House, in the winter the Synod's surveyors came. They measured every room and wrote a name for the House on their plan which is not its name, and asked the Prior a great many questions about the cabinet, very politely. I have kept the third rule in every margin of my life but this one. We cleaned the desks with vinegar today for the end of the month, and the scriptorium is very cold, and I am afraid.

Across the foot of the page, in red, a later stamp:

RECEIVED INTO THE OFFICE OF THE CATALOGUE, WITH THE HOUSE AND ITS CONTENTS. RETAINED AGAINST NEED.
