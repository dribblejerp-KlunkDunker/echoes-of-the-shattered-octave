# A Digest of the Standing Law, for Magistrates of the Resonant Reach

*the Arbiterium of the Iron Synod; with marginal notes by Septimus Fallow, clerk to the bench at Oakhaven · 414 SR, annotated to Harvestmonth 419 · the Organum; the margins written at the Oakhaven watch-house · law*

> The Synod's handbook of law for Reach magistrates, and the increasingly personal notes of the clerk who has had to apply it in a town behind a hedge.

A DIGEST OF THE STANDING LAW OF THE IRON SYNOD, abridged for the use of Governors, Reeves and Magistrates in the Resonant Reach. Issued under the Scales by the Arbiterium, at the Organum. Third issue, 414. One Note. One Law. One Hand.

On the flyleaf, in a small brown hand: Property of the bench, Oakhaven watch-house. Annotated by S. Fallow, clerk, who has read it more often than the men who wrote it. The notes are mine and the law is theirs. I have tried to keep the two apart and have not always managed.

PREFACE. The magistrate is not required to understand the law. He is required to apply it. Where an article appears to him to conflict with local custom, the article prevails, and the custom is to be reported.

Clerk's note: I have reported the custom of the Narrow eleven times. I have had no reply. I take it the custom prevails until somebody in the Organum can be found who has heard of it.

ARTICLE THE FIRST. Of the posting of writs. A writ of the Arbiterium has force from the hour of its posting at the gate of the place named. Its statutory period runs from the posting, and not from any reading.

Note: Oakhaven's gate is now a gap in a hedge of thorn and packed wool three men deep. I have posted the last four writs on the thorn. The thorn has kept them. I cannot get the third one back out.

ARTICLE THE SECOND. Of reading aloud. No writ touching a named person is served until it has been read aloud, in a clear and carrying voice, before witnesses.

Note: Clear and carrying. I have read a writ in a clear and carrying voice twice in my life, in '13 and in '14, and both times we lost somebody at the gate that night. I now read them to old Harrow in the gatehouse, in the Narrow, with my mouth an inch from his ear, and he witnesses with his thumb. Whether Oakhaven has been lawfully served with anything since '15 is a question I have decided not to put to the Arbiterium.

ARTICLE THE THIRD. Of standing writs. A writ under black ribbon is a standing writ. It shall be carried by hand to the person named and put into his hand, and its bearer shall not stop, nor be stopped, until it is delivered.

Note: The one article in this book that the Reach keeps without being told. Nobody stops for anything here any more.

ARTICLE THE FOURTH. Of attainder. Persons declared Dissonant under the standing form forfeit their goods; their testimony is void in any court of the Synod; their names are struck. Provided always that grace is offered to each, once.

Note: Once. In '16 I wrote to the Arbiterium to ask what follows if grace is refused and afterwards, on reflection, wanted. The answer came in eleven weeks. It quoted the form back to me: a man who refuses grace has chosen what follows. I had read the form. That was why I asked. (The blots on this page are the pen's, which catches.)

ARTICLE THE FIFTH. Of Symmetry. That law may never wound one without wounding the other, an Arbiter and his heir shall be bound in Symmetry, wound for wound, and neither shall outlive the other by an hour. (Edict of 389.) The magistrate need take no notice of this article, which touches the Arbiters alone.

Note: The only article in the book that binds the men who wrote it. It is also the only one with no penalty in it. I have thought about that more than a clerk should.

ARTICLE THE SIXTH. Of requisition. All bells, chimes and resonant metal within the province are requisitioned to the public works under the Consortium's warrant. A receipt shall be given.

Note: Ours went in '17, every one, the chapel's first. I have the receipt. It is the best-made piece of paper in Oakhaven. It says the bells have gone to the public works. It does not say what part of a work a bell becomes.

ARTICLE THE SEVENTH. Of assembly. No assembly of more than twelve persons shall be held for the purpose of song, save in the Church's keeping.

Note: We have not put twelve people together to sing since the Screaming Year, Synod or no Synod. The hounds drafted this one before the Arbiterium did.

ARTICLE THE EIGHTH. Of the Church. A warrant of purification under the seal of the High Patriarch is a matter for the Church and is not cognisable by a magistrate. No appeal lies from it to any bench. The Arbiterium alone may stay it.

Note: Not cognisable: that is, I may not know of it. It was read in the square on Dawnday, aloud, by a herald whose voice went up over the roofs as if he had never been told there was any other way to speak. Eighty-four souls in the Hollow; nineteen of them children. I have the census. I looked up the remedy. There is one. It is a stay from the Arbiterium, to be asked for in writing, at the Organum. The fire is on Hearthday.

ARTICLE THE NINTH. Of registration. Persons removing from the province under transit warrant shall present themselves to the Office of the Catalogue for registration at the first relief station. Registration is a condition of transit and may not be declined.

Note: Nobody has been given transit out of Oakhaven since the hedge went up, so this article has never been tried here. The Office has sent us the forms all the same. Four hundred of them, already numbered.

ARTICLE THE TENTH. Of persons under the mark. A person whom a Surgeon-Grafter has marked as past cutting shall be struck from the roll of the bench and of the census, and his testimony shall be received only upon the affidavit of a sound witness.

Note: Marked, Massday. Master Rooke did it himself, at my own table, and was gentle about it, and told me the mark means past cutting and nothing else, which I believe of him; and I have also read the warrant. I am entering it here because it is a fact, and the bench ought to have it, and a clerk enters things. My hand is not what it was. The nails want paring twice a day now, and by noon the ink is under them again. My wife has signed below as my sound witness, so that this note will be good in law.

Below, in a different and steadier hand: Witnessed. M. Fallow. He was a good clerk. He is a good clerk.
