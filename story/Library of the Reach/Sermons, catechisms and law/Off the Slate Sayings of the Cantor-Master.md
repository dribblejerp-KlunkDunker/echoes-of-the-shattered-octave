# Off the Slate: Sayings of the Cantor-Master

*Ilse Brennan, Cantor-Master of Reedside; copied before the rag by her pupil Hazel Thrup · 409–419 SR · the Reedside Conservatory, Oakhaven · philosophy*

> The sayings of a voiceless old teacher of Cantors, copied off her slate by a pupil before they could be wiped away.

Mistress Brennan lost her voice in the throat-fever of 408, after nine nights of singing the sick. She has taught by slate ever since, and by her hands, and by her breath, and at the end of every lesson she wipes the slate with a wet rag, because, she says, a slate is for saying things and not for keeping them. Some of us began copying them before the rag. She knows. She has never stopped us, which is how we know she does not mind, and she has never once said so, which is how we know she is a Cantor.

They are set down here the way the orchard sets things down: by what was growing.

WHEN THE GROUND IS SOFT

Four counts in. A long fall out. You will do this ten thousand times before you are any use to anybody, and ten thousand more after.

A Cantor's instrument is the breath. The voice is only the most common way of spending it.

Trees hear breath before voice. So do the dying. So, if you are honest, do you.

Do not be careful. Be exact. Careful is a fear in an apron. Exact is a craft.

WHEN THE REEDS COME UP

The reeds came up every spring of my life until the Screaming Year, and then they turned to glass in an hour and blew away. Hang the First Reeds over the doors anyway. A reed that does not sound is still a reed. It is still somebody's child's.

The tending note is not yours. You are carrying it for someone. Carry it the way you carry a bowl that is too full: slowly, looking at the bowl and not at your feet.

Ask with the hand. Lay it flat on the bark. Be still. A tree will answer a still hand before it answers a clever one, and so will a frightened man.

MIDSUMMER

In the Chapel, one note. In the orchard, as many as the tree asks for. Do not tell the Chapel about the orchard. (She wiped this one before any of us had finished copying it. I have put it together from three of us.)

The Narrow is not a cage. It is a reed. Too much breath and it shrieks; too little and it is dead. Every Cantor in the Reach has played that reed all her life. Now everyone has to, and they are angry that it is hard. Be kind to them. It is hard.

Sing the birth through the jar. The jar is not there to keep the sound in. It is there to make the sound small enough to hold.

A child who will not cry is a door left open. Hum at it until it is cross with you. Cross is alive.

Too loud is a good problem. Too quiet is a good problem. Not caring which is the only bad one.

HARVEST

Never sing to a thing you will not stay with.

The felling-song is not a goodbye. It tells the sap it may go down now. You learn it last, at sixteen, and I hope you will need it seldom. You will not need it seldom.

Hum while they bind the jaw and lay the stone on the tongue. It is the last thing they will hear, and it ought to be kind, and it ought to be low.

Written on the morning we heard about the orchard-cantor, in the spring of 417: Tobin Ashgrove went above the Narrow for a fevered child, and the child lived. Do not make him a lesson. He would hate it. Make him a reason. (Most of us cried. She let us. Then she cleaned the slate, and we did the breath.)

THE COLD MONTHS

On the Longest Night, hum into the hollow of the oldest tree in the orchard. My teacher did not know why. Her teacher did not know why. Do it anyway, and do it well, as though somebody were listening who had waited all year for you.

You will lose your voice one day. Every Cantor does, to the fever or the years or the one night when it was needed all at once. When it goes, you will find out what you were really using. I am still finding out.

Somebody asked her once, in my second year, whether she missed singing. She wrote: I have not stopped. Then she breathed the tending note at the window, four in and the long fall out, and the frost on the inside of the glass went off it in a ring the size of a hand.

THIS YEAR

The Last Sheaf has been forbidden. The Church has set its Rite for the same week, and the town has been told to put the harvest off. On the morning the notice went up, Mistress wrote one line on her slate and turned it to face the window, towards the north field, where the last stand is still uncut. She did not wipe it. It is there as I write this. I have copied it so that it is kept somewhere, though she would say that is not what slates are for.

CUT IT ANYWAY. QUIETLY. THINGS ARE ALLOWED TO END.
