# Telling the Bees

*Ennis Crowle, beekeeper, of the Flume road · 418 SR · the Flume road, by Oakhaven · manual*

> A beekeeper of the Flume road writes down, for the first time in her family's memory, how to keep straw skeps in the Narrow and how to tell the bees the news.

My mother kept bees on the Flume road, and her mother before her, and none of us ever wrote anything down, because a bee-woman's craft goes from mouth to ear, close, at the straw, the way everything that matters goes. But there are fewer mouths every year, and fewer ears, and the young ones who come to me for a swarm do not know how to tell. So I have written it in the plainest words I have, and if the bees think the less of me for it, they may say so.

SKEPS. Coil the straw and bind it with split bramble, never twine. Set the skep on a stone stool in the lee of a wall, with a straw hackle over it for the rain. A good hive hums low and steady, like a kettle thinking about it. Learn your own hive's hum the first week, because everything is in it. A hive that has lost its queen howls: a thin, ragged, high note that you will hear from the road, and that makes the dogs uneasy.

Since the quarry, the bees work too fast. Comb draws itself out overnight. I have had a skep fill in a week that ought to have taken a summer, and brood come out of season in the back-end. Take the honey early and often, or the comb will grow through the straw and split the skep from the inside.

TELLING. The bees must be told. Every birth and every death in the household, and every stranger who comes to stay. If they are not told, they go. You come out one morning and the straw is empty and still warm, and they are off in the wood, hanging in a clump on a branch like a bruise, waiting for somebody who will tell them things. My grandmother said the bees are the house's ears. I think it is simpler than that. They live with us, and they want to know what we know. So would you.

How to tell. Go at dusk, when they are all home. Stand close, closer than is comfortable, with your mouth near the straw. Knock once, softly, two knuckles and one tap, the way we knock in Oakhaven. Then murmur the news. Not loud. A bee minds a shout worse than a hound does. Say it plainly. A girl born to the house, and her name. Or the name of who has died, and that they are gone and will not be coming to the hives again. If there is more than one dead, tell them in the order of the years, oldest first, so the bees can keep them straight. Tell a stranger by name, and where they came from, and that they are to be let past. Then wait. When the hive has had it, the hum settles lower, the way a room changes when somebody has made room on the bench.

In my mother's day you sang it. There was a telling-song, four lines, which I will not write here, because nobody may sing it now, and a song written down and never sung is a sad thing to leave lying on a page.

SINCE THE HEDGE. When they planted the Hedge, they took every fleece in the Reach for the wool, and I gave my skeps' winter wrappings with the rest. That first winter a hive's hum carried a quarter mile on a still night, and the hounds came down the Flume road to it twice. I lost two skeps, and very nearly my own self. So now I wrap them in fleece again, bought dear, and I set them in the lee of the reed-banks where the water talks over them.

A hum is a sound, and a sound brings hounds, and there is no help for that. But a hive that has not been told goes, and a hive that goes leaves you with nothing at all, and I would rather the hum.

HONEY. Reach honey is the Cantors' honey. Warmed in the hand and stirred with a drop of oil, it is what they take for a torn throat, and every Cantor in the Reach has a torn throat now. I give it free to any Cantor who comes up the road. So should you.

MY OWN TELLING. When I die, somebody go down the Flume road and tell them. Go at dusk. Knock once. Tell them Ennis has gone and will not be coming to the hives again, and that they are to stay for whoever comes next. They will know already; bees always know. Tell them anyway. It was never for them that we did it.
