# The Narrow Larder: Receipts for a Lean Year, with Remarks

*Philippa Oatley, who kept the cookshop by the market, Oakhaven · 419 SR · Oakhaven · cookery*

> An Oakhaven cook's receipts from the Fracture winter and the shut years since, for bread that will not stop rising, pies with no egg, and suppers nobody else needs to hear.

Hetty says I am to write it down before I die, so that somebody in this town can still make a pie that does not go off like a mortar. I said I would, if she put it on her shelf where people could see it and not under the bar with the strong cider. She has promised. If you are reading this under the bar, take it out.

THE FIRST LEAN YEAR. Everybody under twenty thinks the lean years began with the Hedge. They did not. They began on the morning the sky rang, in 412, and the winter after was the worst eating this valley has had in anyone's memory. Milk turned between the pail and the dairy door. Cheese would not take. The cider went flat in the barrel, a quarter-tone flat, and the old men said they could taste it, and they could. The harvest went into the barns heavy and came out of them grey and tasting of nothing. Then the wagons came up the Greenroot road with grain, and the Governor said we would eat, and we ate, and nobody in this house asked what it cost. I will not start asking in a cookery book.

RECEIPTS OF THE FRACTURE WINTER, for when it comes again.

Hedge pottage. Nettles, cut young and with gloves; sorrel; a handful of barley if you have it, and an onion if you are a Governor. Boil. Do not salt it, because there is no salt. There will be salt later, and you will weep over it.

Acorn cake. Iron-bough acorns, which nobody ate before 412 and everybody ate after. Shell them and leach them in the Flume in a net for three days, or they will turn your mouth inside out. Pound them in a mortar. They ring in the mortar, so put a cloth over it. Bake flat on a hot stone. It tastes of a floor, but a clean floor.

Eel boiled twice. The Flume eel is green, and so is its broth, and the first water is not for drinking. Pour it off, fresh water, and boil again. Wrap what is left in dock-leaf. Children will not eat it the first time. They will eat it the third.

WHAT WE EAT NOW.

Bread. Since the quarry, bread wants to rise and does not know when to stop. Punch it down. Punch it down again. Never leave dough to prove overnight. I have seen a loaf come up out of its crock and across a table like a sleeping cat, and the woman next door had to take her oven door off with a crowbar. Bake in a lidded crock with a stone on the lid, and listen. Good bread is quiet. Bread that creaks is not done growing.

Flour. The fields regrow overnight now, which sounds like a mercy until you taste the third cutting. First-cut flour is for bread, second for porridge, and the third for the paste you hang wallpaper with. Keep the Last Sheaf's flour for the hearth bread and nothing else. The sheaf is up on the Hedge-gate as I write this, so you know the time of year, and they are saying the Church will want it put off. We shall see.

Mutton. There is none. The flocks went into the Hedge for their wool, and then they went into us, one a month, and I cooked a good many of them myself, and I am not sorry. If anyone in this town sells you mutton pie, ask him whose. If he says it is lamb, ask him whose lamb.

Eggs. There are none. The hens went quiet first, the autumn the Hedge went in. I will not say more than that, and what was left of them went in the pot. To bind a pie without egg: a spoon of honey and a spoon of flour, beaten with a little cider. To glaze it: milk, if you can get milk that has not turned, which you cannot, so do not glaze it. Nobody in Oakhaven has looked at a shiny pie in two years and we are none the worse for it.

Bettany's tonic, which she gives for weakness, is honey, raw egg and willow-bark. When you ask her where the egg comes from she looks at you as if you had asked her where babies come from, and she of all women should know.

Moot-curd. Aged beside a humming stone, and it tastes faintly of static, the way the air does before thunder. It is the one thing in this book that has got better since 412. I do not know why and I have stopped asking it.

COOKING QUIET. Felt your lids. Wooden spoons, and never iron on iron. Take the kettle off before it sings; a kettle that sings is a kettle calling. Grind before dusk. Chop on a folded cloth. If you must crack a bone for the marrow, do it under water in a bucket. Nobody needs to hear your supper but you.

THE LAST RECEIPT, FOR THE SHEAF. When the last field is cut, every house in the Reach bakes hearth bread from the new flour, one round loaf, and it is broken by hand and never cut, and you give the first piece to whoever is oldest at the table, and the heel to the youngest, and the crumbs to the hearth. That is the whole receipt. You do not need me for it. Your grandmother did it, and so will you.

If the Church will not let us keep the Sheaf this year, bake the bread anyway, after dark, and break it with the shutters to and the lamp low, and pass it round, and eat it. That is all lean-year cookery has ever been. You eat anyway, and you eat together, and you eat quietly. The rest is only salt.
