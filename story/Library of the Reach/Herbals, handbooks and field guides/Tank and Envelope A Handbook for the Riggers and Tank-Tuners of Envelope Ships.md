# Tank and Envelope: A Handbook for the Riggers and Tank-Tuners of Envelope Ships

*Ondine Abara, yard-mistress of the drift-yard below the Long Mole, Port Cantabile; this copy with pencil notes signed P. · 410 SR (fourth printing, 415 SR); pencil notes 417–419 SR · Port Cantabile · manual*

> A Rust Coast yard-mistress's sharp-tongued handbook on persuading drift-glass to lift and keeping an old envelope ship in the air, in a copy a young engineer has argued with in pencil.

This book is for the rigger and the tank-tuner of an envelope ship: sixty feet or thereabouts, oak and brass, a sailcloth envelope over her, lift-tanks slung beneath her keel. It is not for the courier-cutters, which have no envelope and no manners, nor for the great Consortium freighters, whose engineers will not read a book written by a woman who learned her trade on a slipway. You know who you are. Your ship is older than you, and patched like a quilt, and you love her more than is sensible.

ON DRIFT-GLASS. Everything begins with the glass. Drift-glass is Velocity glass tuned to lift. It is pale green as sea-ice when you hold it in your hand, and packed in a brass tank with a reed-valve at one end it burns a soft cobalt and wants, above all things, to move. The whole art of the envelope ship lies in persuading it that the way it most wants to move is up. Her core is another matter: Fortis-cut lodestone in an iron cage amidships, which takes the weight off her and asks nothing of you. Leave it to the Fortis men. The core makes her light. The glass makes her go. You do not command drift-glass. You persuade it. Captains do not understand this, and it is no use telling them.

ON LISTENING TO A TANK. A tank is full of song. When she is content she hums a low chord that you feel through the deck in your heels. Learn that chord in your first week aboard, as you would learn your mother's voice, because everything you need to know is in it. A sour note in the chord means the glass is unsettled: damp in the valve, a knock in the cradle, or salt. Salt above all. Salt gets into a tank and makes her moody, and a moody tank lifts in sulks and starts, and you will spend your nights with a tuning-hammer putting her right.

Pencil, in the margin: She likes to be told what you are going to do before you do it. Not in the book. True, though. P.

ON THE TUNING-HAMMER. Felt head, brass shaft, never iron. Tap the cradle, never the tank. A tap on the tank itself is an insult, and she will remember it for a week.

Pencil: Mine. Not the captain's. He uses it on the galley stove. P.

ON THE ENVELOPE. The envelope keeps what the tanks breathe out, gives her the last of her lift, and gives her most of her temper in a wind. Close-woven sailcloth, doped every season. Patch inside and outside both, in long laps, and stitch with the grain. A patch is no disgrace. An unpatched hole is.

Pencil: Better than sailcloth for a weep: weaverling silk. Do not ask where it came from. Deep Woods, 419. P.

ON BALLAST. Iron in the keel-sling. It is what keeps her from flying like a leaf in a squall. Every new captain wants to throw it over to make height, and every old rigger has watched what happens next.

ON LIFT. Do your figures on a slate, and do them twice, and when the captain does not like the answer, do them a third time and give him the same answer. A soul is a hundredweight and a half with his boots on, a child half that, and in the whole history of skyships nobody has ever come aboard with less baggage than he said he had. Water is the heaviest thing you carry that you cannot do without. The long gun is the heaviest thing you carry that you can.

Pencil: Eighteen. Not twenty. I said so. P.

ON A WEEPING TANK. Drift-glass does not burst. If a tank is holed, it weeps: a slow cobalt vapour, and the lift goes out with it. You will hear it before you see it, because the chord goes thin and climbs. Clip the hole, seal the clip, and get her down while you still choose where. A clipped tank is good for forty miles if you are lucky and four if you are not. A whole new tank wants a drift-bench and a yard, and somebody who knows how to tune one. There is a yard below the Long Mole, and I am somebody.

Pencil: Forty miles, then Cantabile. He will not like it. P.

ADDED TO THE FOURTH PRINTING: ON FLYING IN THE NARROW. Since the Screaming Year, riggers have asked me what the hounds make of a ship. At height, nothing on the ground hears her. Below a hundred feet a sour tank carries further than you would credit, and a hound will follow a chord as it follows a song. Keep her sweet when you are low. Moor high. Do not shout orders over the rail; learn hand-cant, as the deaf bosuns do, and hire one if you can. And if at the dead hour you hear your mother calling you from the rigging, it is not your mother, and you are not to go up.

ON THE WINCH AND THE LINE. A winch drum is only as strong as the deck-beams it is bolted through, and the beams are only as strong as the hull they hold together. If whatever hangs on the end of your line is heavier than your ship can lift, it is your ship that will go down to it, not the other way about. Keep an axe beside every drum. Everybody on the Coast knows the saying: the line is not a promise; the line is a rope.

There is no pencil note here. Somebody has underlined the last sentence, once, very hard, so that the lead has gone through the paper.

A LAST WORD. A ship is not a house. She is a set of arguments with the air that you win every day by a little, and lose all at once if you stop making them. Riggers come and go. Ships, if you are good at your work, stay. Be good at your work.

Pencil, on the back board, smaller than the rest: She hums sweeter when I sleep on the tanks. Mags says that is only my snoring. Mags cannot hear my snoring. P.
