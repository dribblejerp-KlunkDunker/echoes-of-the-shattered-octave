# Ears After Dark: A Field Guide for Those Who Sit the Crossings

*Tilda Dunnock, night-hummer of the Tannery Row crossing · 419 SR · Oakhaven · field guide*

> An old night-hummer's guide to the sounds of an Oakhaven night, arranged like a bird-book, for the new ones the Watch keeps sending her, who keep standing up.

I have sat the Tannery Row crossing every night since the Hedge closed, which is two years and a season, on the same stool and in the same shawl, and the Watch has asked me to write down what I know for the new ones, because the new ones keep standing up. Do not stand up. That is the first thing. A woman standing at a crossing in the dark is a woman about to go somewhere, and everything out there knows it.

This is a guide to listening. I have set it out the way the old bird-books are set out, by what you will hear, and it is short, because most of what you need to know is: hum, and do not answer.

WHAT YOU OUGHT TO HEAR. Learn the town first, so that you know when something is missing from it. A hush-lamp whistles high and thin and never quite steady. There are twenty-two within hearing of my stool, and I know them the way you know your children's coughs. A cradle creaks on the Row at the second hour and again at the fourth. The tavern shutters tick as they cool. The oaks chime when there is wind, and since the quarry, sometimes when there is none. Under all of it, in your teeth more than your ears, the bore. Write your own crossing down, every sound and its hour. You will want the list later.

HOUNDS. You will not hear a hound. You will hear what it has eaten. A hound comes the way rain comes onto a slate roof, a soft quick patter that is not quite in the right place, and inside the patter are sounds that do not belong to the hour: a child laughing where there is no child, a cartwheel where no cart is moving, a man shouting run. Worst and surest of all, a bell. Nobody in the Reach has rung a bell since the Synod took them for the bore, and the hounds still have them. If you hear a bell, it is not a bell.

Before any of that, listen to the roosting birds. A hound goes through a hedgerow like a hot iron through wax, and the birds go still in front of it, one bush at a time. If you hear the quiet walking toward you, it is not quiet. It is something with the birds' silence carried in front of it like a bow-wave.

What draws them: music, first and always, and the louder and the dearer the better. A scream will draw them. A name said aloud draws them faster, and I do not know why, and I would rather not. Keep names indoors after dark.

What to do when you hear one: nothing. Hum lower, not higher, and keep it in your chest. They come to a song, not to a murmur; a murmur is only the town breathing. If one comes close it is not your hum that will save you. It is glass, and you will not have any, so pray the Watch has.

BEDS. In the morning, look for where the dew is not. A hound lies up in an oval a little longer than a big dog, and where it has lain the grass is dry and flat, and no sound carries in it for a day. Drop a pebble in. If you do not hear it land, go round. Birds will not land on a bed. Children will want to stand in one, to see what it is like. Do not let them.

VOICES. This is the part the new ones ask about, and the part I like writing least. You will hear people you love. At the Hedge, mostly, at the dead hour, low and warm and a little tired, the way they sounded at the end of a long day. They will know things. They will know the name you had as a child. They will ask you to come and help them, or they will say that they are cold.

I will put it as plain as I can. Never answer a voice you cannot see. Not to say no. Not to say you are sorry. Answering tells it where you are, and whatever you say, it keeps. A word you give it on Rootday it will give back to you by Nullday, in your own mouth.

A trick works once. If you and your sister have a whistle that means come home, and you use it where it can be heard, it is its whistle now. Make another, and do not use that one either.

I know a man who listens to his wife at a crossing. Everybody knows him. I will not name him, because none of us has the heart. He does not answer. He only listens. I put him in so that you will understand I am not telling you what is easy.

SILENCE. Silence comes in three kinds, and you must learn the first two by heart, so that you are never mistaken about the third.

The first is the Hedge's silence. Wool and thorn swallow sound. Stand near the Hedge and your own voice comes back to you small and close, as if you were talking into a pillow. That is a good silence. That is a silence somebody made, on purpose and by hand, for you.

The second is the silence a hound leaves. It is dry, like the bed. It sits in one place, it does not move, and it wears off by the next night.

The third I will not write about. My mother said a thing written down is a thing invited, and I have sat out too many nights to start sending invitations now. I will tell you only how you will know. You will notice that you cannot hear something you ought to: the reed in the lamp, the cradle at the second hour, your own breath. And it will not frighten you. That is how you will know. When you notice it, hum. Hum anything, and hum it badly. Say your own name into your shawl.

WHAT WE FIND IN THE MORNING. Once in my two years I went to relieve a crossing at the far end of the town at first light, and I found the stool, and the shawl folded on it, and the lamp still burning, and the reed in the lamp gone still. Nobody on that street had heard a thing. That is the whole of the entry. I have put it here so that you will not stand up.

That is all I know. Sit down. Keep the shawl on. Hum. When the shift changes at the dead hour, say it to the one who comes to take your stool: all's well, all's quiet, keep to the Narrow. Say it when it is not true. It is not for them. It is so that there is a voice at the crossing.
