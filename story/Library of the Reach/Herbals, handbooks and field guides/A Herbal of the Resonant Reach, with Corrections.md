# A Herbal of the Resonant Reach, with Corrections

*Barnabas Yewdale, physician, of Greenroot; annotated in pencil by Prue Gorse, herb-woman, late of Greenroot · 391 SR; annotated 418–419 SR · Printed at Oakhaven; annotated at Oakhaven · herbal*

> An old physician's herbal of orchard, Flume and hedgerow, and the Greenroot herb-woman who has spent two years telling him in the margins exactly where he is wrong.

A HERBAL OF THE RESONANT REACH. Being the Plants of Orchard, Flume and Hedgerow, their Virtues and their Seasons, set down for the Use of Households. By Barnabas Yewdale, Physician, of Greenroot. Printed at Oakhaven, 391 SR.

In pencil, across the title page: Prue Gorse, late of Greenroot, her book now. Yewdale was my grandmother's doctor, and she said he never once washed his hands. He is right about willow and wrong about most of the rest, and since the quarry he is wrong about all of it. I have marked him. Read the margins first. P.G., the back-end of 418.

From the preface: The Reach is the kindest country in the Octave to a physician, for here the law of Growth is our partner. A wound in the Reach wishes to close. A bone wishes to knit. The physician's whole art in this province is to stand aside and let the country do its work, and to add, at the proper season, the herbs set down hereafter.

Margin: Stand aside. That is exactly what you must not do any more. The country does its work now whether you want it or not, and its work is more. A wound closes over the splinter in it. Two cut fingers bound together will heal into one finger. Clean everything twice before you let it close, and then watch it, because it will not stop when it ought.

SILVER WILLOW. The bark of the silver willow, which grows along the Flume below the lower lock and in great plenty in the Spectral Woods. Peel it in spring, dry it in the shade, steep it a quarter hour. For fevers, the aching of the joints, and the headache that follows a long singing. Bitter. The bitterness is the virtue.

Margin: Right. Still right. The only page in this book I would sell you. Bettany puts it in her tonic with honey and raw egg, and when there is no egg, which is always, she puts in more honey and says nothing. Peel it from the north side of the tree. Do not ask me why. My mother did.

SWEET REED-PITH. The white pith of the reed that grows in the margin of the Flume, called by children sweet-reed. Boiled in milk, it is given to Cantors in the orchard schools before a long tending, and to infants for the croup.

Margin: Boiled in milk. There has been no milk worth the name since the Cascade; it turns before it is out of the pail. Chew it raw. It eases a throat, and I will tell you something Yewdale never knew, because in his day nobody needed to: it steadies a person who is frightened of their own quiet. You keep chewing, and you keep making a small wet sound, and a small sound is what we all live in now. Give it to the night-hummers. Give it to anyone who has to sit up with the dying.

RESONANT MOSS. The green moss of the humming stones, which shivers when it is touched and takes the note of whatever it grows on. Laid on as a poultice, it draws a wound together in a night. The Reach's chief gift to physic.

Margin: In a night. It drew Jem Cutler's thigh together round a nail he had not told anyone was in it, and he walked on that nail till midsummer. Do not lay moss on anything you have not opened, cleaned and looked into with a lamp. I would strike this page out entirely if the Grafters did not buy the stuff from me by the sack.

KNITBONE, or comfrey. The root grated and bound about a broken limb, which it knits in half the common time.

Margin: Nothing in the Reach needs help knitting. Burn it.

LILAC MOON-MOSS. A moss of the drowned stones of Whisper-Grove, which gives a soft lilac light in the jar. I know it only by report from the physicians of the Woods, who prize it above gold for deafness and the ringing of the ears.

Margin: I had a pinch of it off a Woods pedlar the summer before the Grove went quiet, and it still glows in its jar on my sill, which is more than can be said for the pedlar. It is good for ears, the best thing there is. A carter's boy came to me deaf from a powder-flash on the quarry road and it gave him his hearing back in two days, and the first thing he heard was his mother crying at my door, and he did not know what the sound was. Nobody will come up the Grove road selling it again. When my jar is done, it is done.

LIME FLOWER. The blossom of the lime, as grows by every chapel in the province, taken as a tisane for sleep and for a fretful heart.

Margin: The chapel lime here still flowers, and the bees still go to it. Nobody sleeps anyway.

WHITE POPPY. For pain past bearing, given in drops, and at the last, in larger drops, by the physician's own hand and no other's.

Margin: The Grafters have their own drops for this now, the long drops, and they carry them in a brown bottle for the end. I do not know what is in them. I know what they are for, and I am told the surgeon at Oakhaven gives them gently, which is more than I will say for his Office.

On a blank leaf at the back, in her hand: MUTE-DROPS. Yewdale has no page for these, because no physician in his day would have dreamed of them, so I give them one. The Grafting Office's mute-drops: three on the tongue, and a quarter hour. They do not take the pain. They take the voice. A sawboy once left a bottle on my step by mistake. It smelled of bitter almond and wet slate and something I have no name for, and I poured it into the Flume, and the eels came up to look. It is not a herb. It is not physic. They say it is a kindness to the whole street. I say a medicine that is kinder to the street than to the patient is a policy, and I will not have a policy in my book.

Below it: BURL. Neither of us has a page for this. People come to my door asking for a herb for the burl, and there is none. I sold two, the first year, a sorrel wash and a bitter salve, because a woman wept on my step and I could not bear to send her off with nothing. I am ashamed of both. If a child's fingers are putting out white hairs toward the hearth, do not come to me. Keep the hands warm and dry and out of the damp, and go to whoever in your town still sings.

On the inside of the back board: To whoever has this book after me. Yewdale wrote that the physician's whole art in the Reach was to stand aside. Mine has been to stand in the way, mostly of things that wanted to grow. I leave it with the willow page folded down. It is the only page in forty years I never had to correct, and it is bark off a tree, and you peel it on the north side.
