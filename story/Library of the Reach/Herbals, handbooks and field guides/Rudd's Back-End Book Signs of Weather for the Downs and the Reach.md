# Rudd's Back-End Book: Signs of Weather for the Downs and the Reach

*Absalom Rudd, beacon-keeper of the sheep-walk · 419 SR · the Bellwether, Wethercombe · almanac*

> The old keeper of the fold-fires sets down the weather-signs his father taught him, marks which of them have lied since the sky rang, and admits to one he has no sign for.

I kept the fold-fires on three hills for forty back-ends, and a beacon-keeper who cannot read weather is only a man setting fire to good furze. These are the signs I go by. My father gave me most of them and his father gave him the rest. Since the sky rang, about half of them lie. I have marked those, so that you know which half.

A clerk will tell you it is Harvestmonth. A shepherd will tell you the sorrel has gone red and the swallows are lined up on the barn ridge. Down here a month is what is growing, and I have set the signs out that way. If you want the clerk's name for it, ask a clerk.

WHEN THE REED SPEARS.

When the Flume-reed shows its first green spear, the frosts are done. Still true.

A ring round the moon, rain inside three days. Still true. The moon does not care what anybody drilled.

Cows lying down before noon, rain by evening. I cannot tell you. I have not seen a cow on this side of the Downs since the Hedge.

WHEN THE HAY IS DOWN.

Red sky at night, a fair morning. Half a lie. The reds are not as red as they were; everybody over thirty will tell you so, and the young ones think we are mad. Judge by the gold in it instead. Gold still means fair.

A dog howling at noon means a death in the house. A lie. Since 412 every dog in the Octave howls at noon. Feed him and pay him no heed.

Milk turning in the pail means thunder coming. A lie. Since 412 milk turns overnight whatever the sky does.

When the oaks in the valley ring in a still air, there is wind behind the hill. That was true. Since the quarry they ring when they please. I no longer know what they are telling me, and I do not like to guess.

WHEN THE SORREL REDDENS, which is the back-end, and my season.

A heavy year for rowan, a hard winter. True, so far as a Reach winter is ever hard, which is not very. The valley never gets more than a skin of frost, bar the one winter after the sky rang, which I do not count; the Downs get a white morning or two, and the old men stamp and call it weather. My father said the Reach was kept warm from underneath. I have never had a better explanation, and I have asked.

The first hard frost is Ringing-Wood Day, and the fellers go out. You could hear it from Hob's Knap: every trunk ringing as it came down, the whole valley chiming like a dropped tray of spoons.

When the eels go down the Flume, the year has turned. They go late in Firstfruit, one night only, and the whole of the river street comes out along the channel with lanterns, quiet, because the eels can hear. This year it is Harvestmonth and they have not gone. I have no sign for that. I have put it here so that whoever comes after me can tell me what it meant.

When the last field is cut and the sheaf goes up on the gate, light the fires. That was the law of my office. Long Down to the north, Hob's Knap east above the orchard, Ewe Barrow west on the old mound, and every flock on the hills comes down to the light. Nobody has lit them since the wool went into the Hedge.

SIGNS TAKEN FROM SHEEP. I have a whole page of these and no sheep. A ewe that will not lie down at dusk. A flock that bunches with its back to the west. A tup that stamps in the fold. I will keep the page. Tamar Farrant has brought eleven ewes and a tup up from the Coast, and I mean to watch them and see if they still know what my father's sheep knew.

SIGNS FOR THE NIGHT.

Fog lying in the bottoms at dusk: sound runs along it like water along a pipe. Keep the fold quiet and the dogs in.

Rooks going home early and saying nothing: something is moving in the woods. Bring the dogs in, and the children.

When the wind drops dead on the Downs at the dead hour and stays dropped, go indoors. Do not wait to see why. Say something to your dog on the way, and keep saying it until you are inside.

SIGNS I HAVE HEARD OF AND NEVER SEEN.

That the beacons on the Downs can be seen from the Organum. Rubbish.

That the oaks of the Reach will one day drop every leaf they have in a single night. My grandmother had it from hers. I have never seen an iron-bough bare in my life, and I hope whoever sees it has a fire lit.

That there was a winter once, a real one, before the Reach was the Reach.

My knees do the hills by looking at them now. I sit at Gideon's window in the Bellwether with the three hills in front of me and watch the weather come over the Long Down, the way my father watched it. It comes all the same. That is the sign I would leave you, if I could leave only one: whatever else has gone wrong, the weather still comes from the west, and you can still see it coming over the hill, and there is still time to light the fires.
