# The Fellers' Book: On the Cutting of Iron-Bough

*Jethro Nettleford, feller-master of Oakhaven; with a postscript by Tomas, timber-cutter · 406 SR; postscript 419 SR · Oakhaven · manual*

> A gruff old feller-master's lessons for apprentices on knocking, felling and naming the timber that rings like bronze, and what his last apprentice had to add after the Hedge went up.

Iron-bough is not timber. Get that into your head on your first day and you will keep your fingers longer than I kept mine. Timber is dead wood. Iron-bough is a tree that has not yet agreed to be dead, and the whole of the fellers' craft is the getting of that agreement.

I have felled in the Reach for forty-one years and trained eleven apprentices. Nine are living. Two have beams named after them in the Greenhall roof, which is the only memorial a feller gets, and which neither of them would have chosen, being careless men.

ON KNOWING THE GROVE. A grove knows its own. Walk into a stand of iron-bough as a stranger and the trees go quiet round you, a ring of quiet that widens as you walk, and every feller in the valley lifts his head and says: somebody is in the north stand who oughtn't to be. So you do not cut a grove you have not walked. An apprentice walks his master's grove every evening for a month before he is let near it with an edge, until the trees chime round him as they chime round anyone else. Then he is furniture to them, and he may begin.

ON THE KNOCK. Before you cut, you knock. Strike the trunk with the back of your axe at shoulder height, and listen. A sound tree gives you a clean bronze note that hangs in the air as long as a held breath. A buzz in it means worm. Two notes at once means the heart is split, and the tree will twist as it goes. A dead note, a knock like a fist on a door with nobody home, means hollow, and you leave it, because a hollow iron-bough falls where it likes. Chalk the note on the bark. Your master will check it. He will be right and you will be wrong for about four years.

ON THE SINGING DOWN. No feller in the Reach puts an edge to an old tree until a Cantor has sung it down. This is not piety. I have no piety. It is practical. The felling-song tells the sap to go down and the tree to sleep, and a sleeping tree falls true, splits clean, and does not throw its crown back at you. I have seen a tree felled awake. It took Jago Holcombe's arm off at the shoulder and then lay there ringing for an hour as if it were pleased with itself. Wait for the Cantor. Pay her in cider. Stand still while she sings and take your hat off, and if you laugh I will put you in the Flume.

ON THE FELL. Cut the mouth on the side you want her to fall, a third of the way through, then the back-cut a hand higher. Do not cut through the hinge; the hinge steers her. When she begins to go, step back along the line you have already cleared, and never run, because a man running cannot hear what the tree is doing.

ON RINGING-WOOD DAY. We fell for the year at the first hard frost, when the sap is lowest, and the whole of Oakhaven comes out to hear it. Each trunk rings as it falls, and a big one rings for as long as it takes to sing the Sheaf Song through, and the children run under the note as if it were rain. Then the beams are named. Every beam in a Reach house has a name, cut small on the end-grain where it sits in the wall: the name of the tree, or of the feller, or of somebody's grandmother, or a joke. The beam over my own bed is called Not Tonight. My wife named it.

ON THE WOOD AFTER. Iron-bough goes on growing a little after it is cut, a hair a year, which is why Reach doors stick in the spring and why you must never trust a joiner from outside the province. A good beam rings faintly for years whenever the house is struck. Your grandchildren will know the beams you cut by their notes. That is all the fame a feller ever gets, and it is better than most.

Mind your hands. Mind your master. Wait for the Cantor. J. Nettleford, Feller-Master, Oakhaven, the back-end of 406.

POSTSCRIPT, in a different hand, written larger. Jethro is dead seven years and cannot argue, so here is what has changed. I am Tomas. I was his twelfth and his last; he had me one winter.

Everything he says about the knock is still true, and the knock will now get you killed. The wood rings louder since the quarry, and a bell-note in the open brings hounds the way a supper-bell brings boys. So: carded wool on the blade, bound tight, four strokes to a bundle before it beats flat. Cut in a gust, when the whole grove is chiming anyway and one more note is lost in it. If a hound comes up, stop. Call the job off. Leave the tree a day to forget the hour you were at it. Never run with a log. A log knocks against everything you own.

There is no Cantor to sing them down now. The orchard-cantor was taken the year of the Hedge, and you do not ask a Cantor to sing above the Narrow for a tree when she has a whole town to sing small for. So we lay a palm flat on the bark and say sorry, quietly, and I do not know if it helps. They fall awake. They throw their crowns and kick the saw. I have lost three fingers to it, and the fourth grew back wrong, and I keep it in a glove, and I will not show it you.

We held Ringing-Wood Day last back-end in the lee of the Hedge, with wool on every blade. Nobody came out to hear. Nobody named a beam. The timber goes up the quarry road on the Synod's quota to shore a headframe, and a headframe does not want a name.

Jethro wrote that your grandchildren will know your beams by their notes. Mine are holding up a hole in the ground. I have thought what I like of myself since Firstfruit. Knock first anyway. It is the only honest thing left in the job.
