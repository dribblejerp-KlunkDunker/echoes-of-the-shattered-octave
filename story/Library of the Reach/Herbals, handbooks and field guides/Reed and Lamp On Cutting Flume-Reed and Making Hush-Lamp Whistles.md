# Reed and Lamp: On Cutting Flume-Reed and Making Hush-Lamp Whistles

*Dorran Sedge, keeper of the river gate, Oakhaven · 418 SR · Oakhaven, at the river gate · manual*

> The river-gate keeper who once tended the Cantors' reed-beds explains, at length and against his wife's wishes, how to cut Flume-reed, why a cut reed dies in a day, and how a hush-lamp whistles.

I kept the reed-beds at Reedside for the Conservatory before I kept the river gate, which is near thirty years with my hands in reed one way and another, and Mabbie says that if I am going to talk about it every supper I may as well write it down and talk about something else at supper. So.

First, what is gone. The orchard wind-reed is gone. For three hundred years the Cantors planted it in rows and pruned it to pitch, until a whole valley of it would sing a chord when the wind came off the hills, and I tended those rows from a boy. On the morning the sky rang, every wind-reed in the Reach went to something like spun sugar in an hour, and the next wind took it. I was at the river gate that morning. I heard the orchards stop. I will not write more than that.

What is left is Flume-reed, the wild cousin, that grows in the wet along the channel, under the river gate and down past the lower lock. It was never planted, never pruned, and never taught a note in its life, and my belief, which the Conservatory has never asked for, is that this is why it lived. The orchard reed had been told what to be for three hundred years. When the world went a quarter-tone wrong it had nothing of its own to fall back on. Flume-reed had never been told anything. It just went on being reed.

CUTTING. Cut at dusk, when the reed has been drinking all day. Cut low and on the slant, one clean stroke, with a knife you keep for nothing else, and lay the reed in wet moss at once. Three from a bed in a day and no more, or the bed sulks and gives you nothing for a week. Cut quietly. A reed-knife makes no sound if you know your business, and if you do not know your business the hounds will teach it you.

WHY A CUT REED DIES IN A DAY. Every cutter is asked this and every cutter has his own answer. Mine is that a cut reed is a reed waiting to be told what it is. While it is green it will hold any pitch you give it. Give it one before this time tomorrow and it will keep that note the year round, till the next Reed-Hanging, and longer if you oil it. Leave it untold past the day and it gives up waiting. It goes pale and light and brittle, and it will not take a note again for love or money. A dry reed is good for kindling and nothing else. Do not keep dry reeds about the house. Mabbie says they look like bad news, and she is right.

TUNING. You tune by the slot and by the length. Cut the lip with a fine knife, a sliver at a time, blowing across it between cuts, until the note sits where you want it. A reed is tuned against the Root note, a hundred and seventy-four, which any Cantor will give you and most old men can hum. For a First Reed you want the child's own note, and the Cantor finds that for you; I have cut the reeds for half the children on the river street. For a lamp you want nobody's note at all.

THE HUSH-LAMP. A hush-lamp is a lamp with a reed in its chimney. The heat goes up, the draught draws across the reed's lip, and the reed speaks for as long as the flame burns: high and thin and never quite steady. The unsteadiness is the whole of the art. A steady note is a thing that can be learned and followed. A wavering one is only the house breathing.

To make one, you want a brass collar to hold the reed above the throat of the chimney, a hand's width over the flame and no closer, or it scorches. Set the slot to face the draught. Seal the collar with beeswax; Ennis Crowle's, from the Flume road, is the best, and she will tell you so herself. Tune a lamp-reed a little sharp of the Root, and never to any living person's note. A lamp that sounds like a person is a lamp that something may come and talk to.

Trim your wick right. Too high and the reed shrieks. Too low and it stops, and a lamp that is burning with its reed stopped is worse than a dark one, because you will look at the flame and think you are safe. Listen to your lamp before you sleep. If you cannot hear it, get up.

OIL AND FROST. Oil your reeds once a month, colza or a drop of lamp-oil on a rag. A reed cracks in the dry and splits in the frost, and a First Reed that splits is a grief to a house. If it splits, the note is not lost. Any old body who knew the person can hum it near enough, and a reed tuned near enough is still a comfort. A reed that is a little wrong is a comfort, sometimes, in a way that a right one is not.

REED-HANGING. In Reedmonth, when the reed comes up, every house takes down last year's reeds and hangs new ones, tuned to each child in it. The street used to be loud with tuning for a day; you could not hear yourself think on the river street for the whistling. These last two years we have done it with our mouths an inch from the reed and the doors shut, and you would not know from the street that anything had happened at all. Every door on the river street has a new reed over it all the same.

People ask me why I bother, when nobody past the thorn will hear a reed of ours again, with the Hedge eating every note before it is halfway down the lane. I tell them a reed does not have to be heard to be right. It only has to be given its note.

Mabbie says that is enough about reed. It is never enough about reed. D. Sedge, at the river gate.
