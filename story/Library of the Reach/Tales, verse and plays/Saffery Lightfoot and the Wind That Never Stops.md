# Saffery Lightfoot and the Wind That Never Stops

*Captain Teodor Brisk, of the Fair Excuse · 418 SR · Port Cantabile, Broadsheet Row · adventure*

> A penny tale of the Rust Coast: a skyship captain, a Synod revenue-cutter with a brass ear, and the one wind on the Coast nobody flies into.

A COMPLETE TALE IN ONE NUMBER. ONE PENNY. Printed on Broadsheet Row, Port Cantabile. Not to be read aloud below decks.

They said no skyship on the Coast could outrun a revenue-cutter of the Synod. They said it mostly in taverns that Saffery Lightfoot had not yet drunk dry, and they said it to her face exactly once.

Reader, she was thirty-one years old and had been flying since she was nine. Her ship was the Patient Jenny, a lift-bellied coaster patched more often than a deacon's stockings, with two brass tanks that sang flat in cold weather and a captain who did not. Her bosun was Old Mattock, who had one eye and a great many opinions. Her lookout was the boy Fairweather, who could read a wind by licking his finger and could read nothing else.

On the night this tale begins, the Jenny was lying off the mast-tower with forty crates of moss-poultice in her hold. Every crate was untaxed, every crate was bound for Driftwood Cay, and every one was a crime. On the Cay the salvage crews work the blue jelly bare-handed, and their hands crack open in it, and the Consortium's poultice costs a sovereign a pot. Saffery had bought hers in the Reach for a tenth of that. She meant to sell it on the Cay for the same tenth. Reader, she was not a saint. She simply disliked the Consortium more than she liked money, which on this coast is very nearly the same thing.

She cast off at the dead hour with no lamps lit. Halfway across the harbour mouth, Fairweather licked his finger, held it up, and went white.

"Cutter," he whispered. "Off the Cay. Coming round."

There she was against the last of the moon: the Rectitude, revenue-cutter of the Synod, long and grey, with three tanks and a brass ear on her bow the size of a chapel door. Her listening-horn could hear a cough at a mile and a dropped spoon at two. Her commander was Grume, who had never in his life done anything that was not written down first, and who had sworn on the Synod's own paper to see Saffery Lightfoot dropped from the derrick on the Mole-head.

"Nobody breathe," said Saffery, very softly.

Nobody breathed. The Jenny ran south on the land breeze with her tanks throttled down to a murmur, and for a quarter of an hour the brass ear swung left and right like a dog's nose and found nothing. Then Fairweather sneezed.

It was not much of a sneeze. It was the smallest sneeze ever sneezed on the Rust Coast. But the brass ear swung round and stopped, and the Rectitude's tanks opened up with a roar, and she came after them like a gull after a herring.

"Well," said Old Mattock, "that's torn it."

There is only one place a ship can go on this coast where a Synod cutter will not follow, and every captain knows it, and none of them goes there. South of Cape Dissonance there is a wind that has been blowing west since the year the world went flat, and in all that time it has never once paused for breath. The fishermen call it the Endless. In the Drift, a thing set moving does not stop. A gull that flies into the Endless is never seen again. Neither is a ship.

"Mattock," said Saffery, "get out the Widow's Washing."

The Widow's Washing was a storm-kite. It was forty feet across and stitched out of every sail Saffery's mother had ever owned, and it lived rolled in the bow with a thousand fathoms of tarred line on the big winch. Saffery had never once flown it. Her mother had flown it once, long ago, and come home white-haired at forty and never said where she had been.

They put it up over the Cape with the Rectitude a quarter-mile behind and closing. For a moment it hung in the ordinary air, a great patched flag of a dead woman's sails. Then it rose into the Endless, and the line came taut with a crack like the sky breaking its knuckles, and the Patient Jenny was snatched forward so hard that Fairweather sat down on the deck.

Reader, they went. They went faster than any ship on the Coast has ever been dragged. The sea went by underneath them in a blur, and the Cape fell behind them in a single breath. Behind them the Rectitude's brass ear filled with the roar of the Endless and heard nothing else at all. A cough at a mile, a spoon at two, but not a ship inside the loudest wind in the world.

Commander Grume consulted his regulations. His regulations said that no vessel of the Synod was to enter the Endless. He turned for home, and he wrote it down.

So the Jenny was free. And the Jenny was going west at a speed no ship can live at, behind a kite in a wind that would never stop, towards an edge of the world nobody has charted, and the big winch began to smoke.

"Cut it," said Mattock.

Saffery looked up at her mother's sails, high and small and pulling. She thought of her mother at forty with her white hair, saying nothing. She took out her mother's knife.

"Captain," said Fairweather, "will it come down?"

"No," said Saffery. "Not ever. Not in the Drift." And she cut the line herself.

The Jenny fell out of the Endless like a stone out of a sling and came down into ordinary air above an ordinary sea, slow and quiet and very surprised. They coasted to Driftwood Cay on what was left of the land breeze and sold forty crates of poultice for a tenth of a sovereign a pot. On the Cay, a man with a cracked hand shook Saffery's, and it was the best price she ever got.

Fishermen off the Cape say that on a clear evening you can still see it, very high and very small, going west, and going faster. In the Drift, nothing that is let go of ever stops. Saffery Lightfoot knew that when she cut it.

Reader, she cut it anyway.
