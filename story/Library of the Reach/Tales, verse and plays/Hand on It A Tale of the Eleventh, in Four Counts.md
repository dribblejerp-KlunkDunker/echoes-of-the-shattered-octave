# Hand on It: A Tale of the Eleventh, in Four Counts

*Magda Rusch, of no House · 418 SR · Cinderhold, Tier Eleven; a Bellows chapbook, carried west by a Deep Guild fitter · folk tale*

> A deaf old striker, the granddaughter who swore to be his ears, and the day the Great Beat missed a fall.

This is told the Eleventh way. Three counts of story, and then the fall, when you shut your mouth. Where it says Fall, wait for it.

Kolb struck plate on the Eleventh for forty years. He swung the bar. He kept the count.
Fall.

He could not hear the Beat any more. Forty years of it had taken his ears. He felt it in his boots instead.
Fall.

His granddaughter Grete carried water to the crew. She was eleven, and of no House. She talked between the falls, like everybody.
Fall.

The crew had a word for Kolb now. They said it between the falls, behind his back, though he could not have heard it to his face. The word was cold.
Fall.

It was the whistle. On the Eleventh everybody listens for it. A pipe that has run cool for a week remembers all at once, and before it remembers it sings, high and thin, F-sharp. Then you have four counts to get behind basalt. Mouth open. Eyes shut.
Fall.

A deaf man does not hear the whistle. A deaf man on a crew stands there while it sings. And whoever goes back for him is the second man burned.
Fall.

The foreman, Ruprecht, said it to Grete's face, because he could not say it to Kolb's ears. Your grandda's off the crew at end of shift. I'm sorry for it.
Fall.

Grete said: I'll be his ears.
Fall.

Ruprecht laughed. A child for ears, on the striking floor. Then he saw she meant it, and stopped laughing, and said: Who'll swear for that?
Fall.

Kolb had read every word off their mouths. He went to the furnace mouth. He took the ingot out bare-handed and held it.
Fall.

He held it through a whole count of four, with the crew watching and the smell going up, and he said: She's my ears. Hand on it.
Fall.

Nobody called him cold after that.
Fall.

So it went. Grete at his elbow, with the water and with her hand on his wrist. They had a sign between them. For the whistle: the flat of her hand, hard, once. Then you run.
Fall.

It went that way all spring and all summer. Twice the whistle came. Twice the flat of her hand came down on his wrist, and twice old Kolb was behind the basalt before the younger men.
Fall.

Then, on a Driftday at the back end of the year, the Great Beat missed.
Not stopped. Nobody alive has heard it stop. It missed one fall, the way your heart does when you miss a stair in the dark.
And there was no

There was no fall.

Every soul on the Eleventh froze where they stood. Mid-word, mid-swing, mid-breath. A body raised under the Beat does not know what to do in the gap where a fall should be. It holds you like a hand. Four counts. Sometimes more.

And in the gap, high and thin, a pipe began to sing.

Grete heard it. Grete was frozen with the rest, with her mouth open on a word and her hand on her grandda's wrist, and the hand would not come down.

Kolb did not hear the whistle. Kolb had not heard the Beat in eleven years. There was nothing missing in his ears, because there had been nothing in them to miss.

He looked at the crew standing like a row of nails. He looked at the child at his elbow with her mouth open. He looked where she was looking.

He picked Grete up under one arm. He took Ruprecht by the collar with the other hand. And he walked behind the basalt like a man going home for his supper.

One.
Two.
Three.
And the pipe remembered.

Fall.

The Beat came back. It always does. Five of the crew were burned, and they lived. Ruprecht has a scar from his ear to his collarbone, and on the Eleventh that is a rank, and he wears it like one. Nobody died. On the Eleventh, that is worth a date.
Fall.

Ruprecht came to Kolb after and said it between the falls, loud, so the whole crew heard: He kept his hand on it. There is nothing higher you can say of anybody in Cinderhold.
Fall.

Kolb could not hear it. He looked at Grete. She took his wrist and tapped it out with her fingers, four little taps, one to a count.
Fall.

That is what four taps on the wrist mean on the Eleventh now. Not the whistle; the whistle is still the flat of the hand. Four taps with the fingers mean: he kept his hand on it.
Fall.

Tell it between the falls. It is short on purpose.

- **Canon notes:** Canon: Cinderhold's four-beat industrial pulse, speech between hammer-falls (Vol I §III.3; Vol II, Cinderhold); the Great Beat, which has stopped only twice in two hundred years, and beat-drift, in which every Cinderholder in earshot freezes mid-word when it skips (Vol II, Cinderhold, hazard 4); the F-sharp whistle, four seconds to basalt, 'dive behind basalt, mouth open, eyes shut', and the pipe that 'remembered' after a week cool (Vol I §III.3, the Tier Three disaster of 417); the bare-handed ingot oath, 'cold' meaning cowardly, and 'he kept his hand on it' as the highest praise (Vol I §III.3; Vol II dialect); Tier Eleven, of no House; the Bellows, Tier Eleven's union (Vol II). Struck, not sung, music (Vol I). The Moor serial's resolution set the precedent that Cinderhold texts use the Beat instead of the Narrow. The burns are not described, and the child is not hurt. [PROPOSED]: Magda Rusch; Kolb, Grete and the foreman Ruprecht; the four-tap sign; the Bellows printing chapbooks; a Deep Guild fitter carrying it to the Oakhaven works. No Furnace House is named.
