# Under a Hand's Width

*Edwy Marchbank, formerly reed-pruner of the Reedside orchards · 418 SR · Oakhaven; hand-sewn, one copy · poetry*

> Nine short poems by an old orchard pruner, from the last singing summer to a child born quiet and brought round.

I pruned reeds in the orchards for thirty-one years. Now I sweep the Chapel. These are short, because everything is. Whoever finds this in the pew: it is yours. Read it under your breath.

I. PRUNING
You cut a reed to make it sing.
Too long, it mumbles. Too short, it shrieks.
My master put the knife in my hand at nine
and said, Listen first. Cut after.
Thirty-one years I listened first.
I should like that written on me somewhere.

II. THE HOUR OF SUGAR
The reeds went clear as barley-sugar
in the time it takes to say a grace,
and then the wind came down off the hill
the way it always had, meaning no harm,
and I watched thirty-one years of listening
blow over Greenroot like a fine snow.

III. FLAT
My wife says the reds have gone.
I say the reds are the same; it is our eyes.
We have had this argument for six autumns.
Neither of us has once said the other thing:
that we would both give a great deal
to be wrong.

IV. THE BELLS
They took the bells on a Massday.
Nobody rang them going.
That was the worst of it, I think:
not that they went, but that we let them
leave the town as quiet as stolen spoons.

V. WOOL
We planted the Hedge with our own hands
and packed it with our own fleece,
and every cough in Oakhaven goes into it now
and stays. I think of them some nights:
all those coughs, all those dropped pans,
all those children told to hush,
kept in the wool like letters never sent.
I hope it is a kind keeper.
I know what it is keeping them from.

VI. NIGHT-HUMMER
Old women on stools at the crossings,
humming nothing in particular
so that the street will not go quiet.
I used to think the Cantors held the Reach together.
It was these. It was always these.

VII. THE OAKS
Since the bore, the oaks ring when the wind moves:
a spoon on a bowl, a bell in the next valley.
The children think it lovely.
I pruned for sound all my life, and I know
what a thing sounds like when it has been
pulled too tight.

VIII. INSTRUCTIONS FOR MY BURYING
Bind my jaw. Fine. Put the stone on my tongue.
I have said what I meant to.
But leave my hands loose, Hannah,
in case there is pruning.

IX. FOR A CHILD BORN QUIET, WHO WAS BROUGHT ROUND
You came out without a sound,
and the whole room held its breath with you,
and then the Cantor hummed into the top of your head
and you creased your face up like a walnut
and made a noise the width of a hand.
Nobody cheered. We have forgotten how.
But I want you to know, small one,
that an old man in the next house,
who used to tune the orchards,
heard you through the wall,
and stood up,
and took off his cap.
