# Fireside Tales of the Reach, Lately Told

*Margery Fallow, who keeps the little school on Cooper's Row · 418 SR · Oakhaven · folk tale*

> Three new tales told as if they were old, written down by a schoolmistress who noticed how quickly the children changed them.

I keep the little school on Cooper's Row. When the slates are put away and the hush-lamp is lit, we tell tales. I have written three of them down, because I noticed something about them and I want somebody after me to notice it too.

In every one of my grandmother's tales, the clever girl sang. She sang the wolves to sleep. She sang so loud that the miller heard her at Greenroot. The children will not have those tales now. If I tell one, a hand goes up: But Mistress, the hounds would come. So the tales have changed, and they have changed in about three years, and the children tell them back to me as if they were three hundred years old. Here they are as the children like them. I have only tidied the grammar.

CLEVER DORCAS AND THE JAR

There was a girl called Dorcas who lived on the far side of the water-meadow, back when there were people on the far side. Her mother sent her to her gran's for a jar of honey and told her to take the baby, because the baby would not settle for anyone else.

So Dorcas went over the meadow with the baby on her hip, and she came back with the honey in the good brown jar with the blue ring round its neck, which had been her gran's mother's. She was halfway home when the baby woke, saw the dark, and opened his mouth.

You know what a baby can do. He cried properly, a cry like a gate dragged over cobbles, and far off at the edge of the trees something heard it and turned. Dorcas saw the air go shivery there, the way it shakes over a summer road.

She could have clapped her hand over his mouth. But her gran had told her what becomes of babies who are made to go quiet in the dark, and Dorcas was not going to carry home a quiet baby. So she did a clever thing. She ate the honey. She ate all of it with two fingers, and never tasted a drop. Then she held the empty jar to the baby's mouth and let him cry into it, the whole cry, the long one. When he stopped to breathe, she pushed the stopper in.

She threw the jar as far as she could towards the Flume. It struck the stones by the bank and broke, and out of it came the baby's cry, every bit as loud as he had made it. The hounds went to the jar. Of course they did. They go where the loud thing is.

Then the baby, having cried himself empty, went still on her hip, and stiller, and she felt him getting cool. So she put her lips to the top of his head, where the bones are soft, and hummed into him: not loud, not quiet, the width of a hand. She walked home through the meadow humming, step for step, and the hounds were busy with the jar the whole way.

When she got in, her mother said, Where is the honey? Dorcas said, In me. Her mother said, Where is the jar? Dorcas said, Over by the Flume, with the hounds. Her mother sat down on the settle and laughed without making a sound, which Reach mothers can do, and then she cried the same way. Then she sent Dorcas to bed with no supper, which was fair, because Dorcas had had the honey.

And that is why, in the Reach, nobody will lend you a good jar. (The children insist on this ending. One of them tried to borrow mine afterwards.)

EBBO STINT, WHO ANSWERED

Ebbo Stint was the meanest man in Oakhaven, and in Oakhaven that is a contest. He had a wife called Tilda who did the kindness for both of them, and she died of it, the children say, though they say that about everybody. She was buried the proper way, with her jaw bound and a stone on her tongue. Then Ebbo went home to count his money, which she had always hidden for him, because he could never decide where. It was not where she had said. He turned the house inside out.

One night at the Hedge he heard her. Ebbo? Ebbo, love, are you warm enough?

Everyone knows you do not answer. Ebbo knew it better than anyone, because answering costs something, and he never paid for anything he could get free. So he walked past. For a month he walked past. She asked whether he was eating. She asked about the cat. She asked whether he had found the money yet, and he did not even slow down, though it nearly killed him.

Then one night she said, very sweetly: I put the thirty sovereigns under the hearthstone, love. You'll find them there.

Ebbo stopped dead in the lane. He turned to the thorn, and for the first time in his life he raised his voice. It was forty! There were forty!

In the morning there was his stick, leaning against the Hedge, as neat as if he had stood it there himself.

They lifted his hearthstone, of course. There was nothing under it. They took up the floor and the chimney, and to this day nobody has found Ebbo Stint's forty sovereigns. But if you walk along the Hedge behind the tannery of a night, and you are mean, and you are listening, you may hear a woman's voice say, very gently: Forty, love. It was forty.

The moral, the children tell me, is not to answer. I think the moral is that it did not know the number until he told it. That is the whole of the Hedge in one sentence, and I would rather they learned it from Ebbo than from anyone they love.

WHY THE OAKS RING

When the Synod's men came for the bells, the year the quarry opened, they took the six from the Chapel and the little wind-bells out of the reed-rows. They put them in a wagon under sacking and drove them up the quarry road to be made into teeth for the bore. Nobody said anything. By then everybody knew what bells bring.

But the oaks watched too. The oaks of Oakhaven are old, and they had heard those bells every morning for three hundred years, the way you hear your mother come down the stair. When the wagon went by under the branches, the oaks reached down, only a little, the way a sleeper reaches for the blanket, and each of them took a ring of sound out of the wagon and kept it. Not the bronze. The Synod could have the bronze. Just the ringing.

That is why the oaks of Oakhaven chime like a spoon on a bowl when the wind gets up. They are holding the bells for us. They keep them very quiet, because they know about the hounds too, and they let out only a little at a time, so that we will not forget what bells sounded like. And one day, the children say, when it is safe, the oaks will give the bells back all at once, and the whole town will ring the way it did on a feast morning, and nobody will be afraid of it.

A Consortium man told me last winter, quite kindly, that the oaks ring because the wood has gone soft and dense since the bore began drawing on the Root, that it is a known effect, and that it is not a good sign. I expect he is right. He is paid to be. I have kept the children's version. I notice that he did not say what will happen on the day the oaks stop.
