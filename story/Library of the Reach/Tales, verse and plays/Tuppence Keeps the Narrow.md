# Tuppence Keeps the Narrow

*Elspeth Garland (words and pictures) · 418 SR · Oakhaven, Cooper's Row; copied out by hand · children's*

> A picture-book for small children about a lamb who learns, the hard way, to keep her voice the width of a hand.

For the children of Cooper's Row, who have not seen a lamb this year.

Tuppence was a little lamb
with a very big voice.
BAA! said Tuppence in the morning.
BAA! said Tuppence at noon.
BAA! said Tuppence to the moon.

Hush now, said her mother, the old grey ewe.
Too loud brings the hounds, little one,
and too still brings the Hush.
We keep to the Narrow, you and I:
a hand's width of voice. No more. No less.

But Tuppence liked loud.

One evening, at the edge of the fold, she gave the biggest BAA in the world, just to see how far it would go.
It went over the wall.
It went over the hill.
It went all the way to the dark trees.
And in the dark trees, something that had been asleep lifted its head.

Tuppence saw the air at the edge of the wood go shivery, like the air over a hot stone.
She ran all the way back and pushed her nose into the old grey wool, and her mother stood very still and hummed, low and steady, until the shivery air went away.

All right, thought Tuppence. If loud is bad, I shall be quiet. I shall be the quietest lamb that ever was.

She did not baa in the morning.
She did not baa at noon.
She did not baa at the moon.
She lay down in the long grass and kept so still that the beetles walked over her hooves.

It was very quiet in the long grass.
After a while, Tuppence felt cold, though the sun was out.
After a longer while, she could not quite remember what her mother called her.
Her wool had gone pale, like the mist on the Flume.

Her mother came looking. Tuppence? Tuppence?
But a lamb who makes no sound is a hard lamb to find.

So the old grey ewe did a clever thing. She stopped calling, and she began to hum: the low, warm, middle hum, a hand's width wide. She hummed the way a hush-lamp whistles. She hummed the way the night-hummers hum at the crossings.

And Tuppence, in the long grass, heard it, and before she could think about it she hummed back.

Mmm, said Tuppence.
Not loud. Not still. Just the width of a hand.

And all at once she was warm, and her wool was her own colour again, and she remembered her name. It was Tuppence, which is what you call a small thing that is worth keeping.

That night, in the fold, by the whistling lamp, Tuppence murmured:
Goodnight, Mother.
Goodnight, lamp.
Goodnight, wall. Goodnight, hill.
Goodnight, dark trees, far away.
I am not talking to you.

And her mother laughed without making a sound, and the two of them kept the Narrow together all night long, a hand's width apart, until the morning came.
