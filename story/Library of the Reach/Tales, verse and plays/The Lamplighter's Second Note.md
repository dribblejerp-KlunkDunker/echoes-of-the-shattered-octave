# The Lamplighter's Second Note

*by a Lady of Cooper's Row (anonymous) · 418 SR · Oakhaven; a fair copy passed from house to house · romance*

> A cooper's daughter waits all autumn for a stammering lamplighter to propose at a hand's width, and he finds another way to say it.

In the Reach a proposal of marriage has always been made at a hand's width: mouth to ear, so softly that nobody else could swear to the words. The orchard people said it was so the reeds would not learn it and sing it about the valley. If a third person overheard and could repeat it, it did not count, and the poor man had to begin again. My grandmother said half the weddings in Reedside were delayed a year by a sister behind a door.

It was a pretty custom when the rest of the world was loud. It is a hard one now, when everybody in Oakhaven speaks at a hand's width from morning to night. A girl cannot tell, any more, which murmurs are meant.

Mercy Hatchard could not tell. She was the cooper's daughter on Cooper's Row, twenty-four, with her father's hands and her late mother's patience. Every evening at the Listening Hour she stood at her door to watch Crispin Whitlock light the lamps.

He lit them the way other men say their prayers, one at a time and without hurrying. He put the taper up on his pole and held it to the wick, and in the moment the flame caught, before the reed above it began to whistle, he looked down at her. Then the reed found its note, thin and high, and under the cover of that note he would say something. It was never much. Cold tonight. Your father's hammer was going late. There's frost coming; I can smell it. They had eleven seconds, from the catch to the full whistle, and in a year of evenings they had spent every one of them on the weather.

Crispin had a stammer. In the old days nobody minded it. In the Narrow it was a cruelty, because a word that sticks has to be pushed, and a pushed word comes out loud. So he said short things, and he said them under the reed, and Mercy, who could have listened to him talk about frost until she was eighty, began to fear that he would never say anything else.

Her father did not fear it. Gabe Hatchard had views. He had a man in mind: Mr Osgood, a clerk at the works, who had clean cuffs, a room near the Greenhall and wages in Consortium scrip, green and black, and who came to supper on Rootdays and praised the pickled walnuts in complete sentences.

In the week the eels went down the Flume, Mr Osgood asked her. He did it properly. He leaned in at a hand's width and gave her the whole proposal in one breath, without a stumble: his prospects, his regard, a cottage with a slate roof. It was perfectly done. It was like being read a receipt.

Mercy said she would give him her answer on Nullday. Then she went upstairs and sat on her bed and did not cry, because crying carries.

On the evening before Nullday, Crispin came down Cooper's Row with his pole as usual. He lit the Bramleys' lamp, and the widow Ferrier's, and then he came to the Hatchards' and put the taper to the wick. Mercy stood at the door with her arms folded, because she had decided that if he talked about the weather tonight she would marry Mr Osgood and have done with it.

The flame caught. Crispin looked down at her and said nothing at all.

Then the reed began to whistle, and it was not her reed. He had cut a new one and set it over the lamp in the afternoon while she was at the well. It found its note, thin and high like every lamp in Oakhaven, and then, a breath later, it found another one, a little above the first. Two notes. Sweet, and wrong, and leaning on each other the way two reed-rows used to answer across a valley before the Cascade.

Every child in the Reach knows the catechism. What is holiness? One note, held without end. What is sin? The second note.

Mercy stood very still. A lamp that sang two notes could be reported, and anyone on the Row who wanted to could hear it. But nobody on the Row could have told you what it meant. It said nothing to anybody but her. He could not say it at a hand's width, so he had put it in a reed, and a reed could be overheard all it liked and nobody could repeat a word of it. By the oldest rule in the Reach, it counted.

She did not trust her voice, and in the end she did not need it. She leaned in towards the lamp, close enough to feel the heat on her cheek, and hummed the second note back, softly, at a hand's width, so that only the man on the ladder could hear.

Crispin's taper shook so badly that he dropped it, and it went out in the gutter.

Mr Osgood had his answer on Nullday, in a short note that was kinder than he expected. Gabe Hatchard came out in the morning, looked at the lamp for a long time, and said, That'll want reporting. Then he went back in to his hammer, and he never reported it. He had courted Mercy's mother in the orchards, long ago, and some people say he did it with a reed.

If you walk down Cooper's Row at the Listening Hour, among all the lamps keeping their single notes, you will hear one that does not. Nobody has reported it yet. A good many people stop to listen.

Crispin still stammers. He has never needed to say it twice.
