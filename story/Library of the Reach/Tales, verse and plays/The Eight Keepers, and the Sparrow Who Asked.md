# The Eight Keepers, and the Sparrow Who Asked

*Constance Farthing, who taught the small voices their letters · 409 SR · Whisper-Grove, the Cloister road · fable*

> Nine bedtime fables for a children's choir: eight animals who each keep one thing in the world, and a sparrow with no job at all.

For the small voices of the charitable choir, Cloister road. These are told one a night before hymn practice, and on the ninth night the Sparrow. They are very old and I have not improved them, except to take out a bear, who frightened the youngest.

THE BADGER WHO KEPT THE ROOTS
In the beginning everything grew every which way: up and down, and sideways into next week. So the Badger went under the ground and told every root, one at a time, which way was down. He is telling them still. If you put your ear to the earth in spring you will hear a sort of humming. That is the Badger going round, saying down, and down, and down.
Moral: somebody has to say it every time.

THE SALAMANDER WHO KEPT THE HEARTH
Fire did not used to know its own size. A candle might burn down a barn, and a bonfire might not warm a cup. So the Salamander sat in the middle of every flame and told it how big to be: the size of a hand for a hand, the size of a house only for a house.
Moral: mind the size of things.

THE TORTOISE WHO KEPT THE WEIGHT
When you put down a cup, it stays where you put it. That is because the Tortoise is holding it down for you, and holding down the table, and the house, and the hill the house stands on. It is slow work, and he does not complain.
Moral: say thank you to your cup.

THE HERON WHO KEPT THE HOURS
The Heron stands in the river of the hours and does not move, so that the hours can go past him in their proper order, one and then the next. If he ever stepped out, they would all pile up against each other like logs at a weir, and next Nullday would happen on top of breakfast.
Moral: stand still when it is your turn to stand still.

THE COCKEREL WHO KEPT THE MORNING
Every day the Cockerel calls the light up over the edge of the world, and every day it comes. Nobody has ever thanked him, because he is noisy.
Moral: a thing can be useful and still be annoying. This is true of choirs.

THE HARE WHO KEPT THINGS GOING
The wind, the rivers, a ball thrown in the yard: they all go because the Hare keeps them going, and she never sits down to do it. That is why you cannot catch a hare.
Moral: do not try. She is busy.

THE MAGPIE WHO KEPT WHAT WAS REMEMBERED
The Magpie loves bright things, and the brightest things of all are the things we remember: the way to your gran's door, the words of the third verse, the names of everybody you love. She keeps them in her nest so that nobody loses them for good.
Moral: if you forget something, it is not gone. The Magpie has it. Ask her nicely.

THE OWL WHO KEPT THE RESTS
Between every note of every song there is a little rest, and the Owl keeps those. A rest sounds like nothing, but it is not nothing. It is our nothing: the good quiet, the quiet of a house asleep. Outside the edge of the world there is another quiet, which belongs to nobody, and it would very much like to come in. It cannot get past the Owl. She keeps all the gaps filled with our own quiet, the way you fill a doorway by standing in it.
Moral: not all quiet is the same quiet.

THE SPARROW WHO ASKED
The Hedge-sparrow was very small and had no job at all. She went to each of the Keepers in turn and asked: When do you sleep?

The Badger said, When somebody comes to say down for me.

The Salamander said the same, and so did the Tortoise. The Heron said it without moving his beak. The Cockerel said it loudly. The Hare said it running past. The Magpie said it and then forgot she had, and the Owl said it very softly, in a rest.

Who is coming? asked the Sparrow.

Nobody answered. Nobody knew, and it is rude to make a thing up for a sparrow.

The Sparrow thought about this for a whole afternoon, which is a long time for a sparrow. Then she went back under the ground to the Badger and said, Show me how you say down.

And the Badger laughed, which nobody had ever heard him do before, and began to teach her.

Moral: little ones should make themselves useful. That is what I tell the choir, at any rate. The children have decided it means something else, and they will not tell me what.
