# The Bellwether Book: Songs Writ In Behind the Bar

*kept by Reuben Thrale, landlord, and the drovers who stopped; last entry by G. Thrale · c. 380–417 SR · the Bellwether, Wethercombe · song*

> The drovers' inn's songbook, written in by many hands over forty years, until the night the house was shut.

Rule of the house. Any drover who sings a song in this house that is not in this book shall write it in, or buy the room a jug. Spelling no object. R. Thrale.

THE DROVE-ROAD
(Every man knows it. Written in so the boys get the verses in the right order.)
Over the Long Down and down by the thorn,
eight hundred ewes and a tup with a horn,
bell at his throat and the dogs at his heel,
drive 'em to Oakhaven, home for a meal.
Walk on, walk on, it's only a mile
to the Bellwether's fire at the foot of the stile.

(The second verse is ruder. You know it. A. L.)
(He means he don't know it. R. T.)

FOLD-FIRE
(For the back-end of the year, when the beacons are lit.)
Long Down burning, north and high,
Hob's Knap answers from the eastern sky,
Ewe Barrow west on the old long mound:
come down, my flock, come down, come down.
Come down to the light, come down to the fold,
the night is long and the hill is cold.

(Sung at the window while Absalom goes up and lights them. He cannot sing. He lights them anyway. R. T.)

SCOURING THE RAM
(For the seventh summer. Sung on the scarp, one verse to a stake.)
Chalk him white and cut him clean,
the whitest Ram that ever was seen;
scour him once in seven year
or he'll walk off the hill and the flock with him, dear.

Scoured 395. Scoured 402. Scoured 409, rain all week, and the Farrant girl fell in the trench and came up white as the Ram. R. T.

THE SHEAF SONG
(Oakhaven's way. Wethercombe cuts its own last stand to it. One cut to a beat, and never one man alone.)
Cut it low, and cut it slow,
the last of the year is let to go.

(That's all there is of it, and all there needs to be. You sing it round till the stand's down. R. T.)

THE MILLER'S DAUGHTER
Not written. Both verses. If you need it written down, you are too young to hear it. R. T.

A NOTE, IN THE SAME HAND, LATER AND SMALLER
Since the year the reds went, a song carried over the Downs brings what we do not name in this house. Sing them low now. Any man who sings full voice outside this door buys nobody a jug, because he will not be here to. R. T., 413.

THE LOW SONG
(Made in this house the winter of 414, for the Narrow, by all of us, but mostly by Cicely from the fold.)
Low, low, the width of a hand,
lambs in the fold and the ewes on the land;
not loud for the hounds and not still for the other,
low, low, as you'd hum to your mother.
Low, low, and the lamp on the sill,
somebody's humming on every hill.

(Best sung by three or four, so there's always one breathing in while another breathes out. That way it never stops. A. L.)

My nephew Gideon has the house from Reed-Hanging. My chest will not do the stairs, and the house wants a pair of legs. Keep the book, boy. Make them write in. R. T., 416.

416, high summer. Nobody went up to scour the Ram. Next year, we said. G. T.

417, back-end. Shut tonight. The wool has gone down to Oakhaven for the Hedge, and the flocks with it, and we are all to go in behind it: Tamar and all the Farrants, the fold, me. The road is empty. I sang the Drove-Road on the doorstep, low, the way Uncle said, to nobody.

I am leaving the book on the bar for whoever opens the door next. If it is me, I will know the songs. If it is not, learn the low one first.

G. Thrale.
