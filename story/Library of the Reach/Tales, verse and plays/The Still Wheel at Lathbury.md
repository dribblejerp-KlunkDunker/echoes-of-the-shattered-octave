# The Still Wheel at Lathbury

*told by Kester, a miller's man of Stillwater Landing; written down by a raftsman waiting on the lock · 418 SR · Stillwater Landing · ghost story*

> A miller's man tells what he did not find at a mill up the Flume, which is the only safe way to tell it.

You'll want me to tell you what was in the mill. Everyone does. I'll tell it the way my gran told hers, which is the only safe way: I'll tell you what was not there. If you want the other kind of story, there's a pedlar on the wharf who sells them by the sheet, with pictures. He has never been to Lathbury.

Lathbury Mill stands on the leat off the Flume, two miles above the lower lock, where the grown-wood channel lets a little water down to turn a wheel for the Lathbury farms. The Pargeters had it: the miller and his wife, two girls, a boy and the old mother. In the spring after the Hedge went up round Oakhaven, Lathbury stopped sending flour down to the Landing. It didn't stop all at once. The sacks came lighter, then late, then not at all. My master, Saul Ainsley, who has the mill here, was owed for a season's sacks, and on a Hearthday he took me up the towpath to ask about it. That's all it was: a man going to ask about a debt.

The first thing that was not there was the noise. You can hear Lathbury wheel from the bend below. It has a knock in the third paddle that every boatman on this water knows. We came round the bend and the wheel was turning, and there was no knock. There was no sound from the leat at all. The water came down the race white and fast and went over the wheel, as quiet as milk being poured in another house.

Saul said, Hm. That was all.

The second thing that was not there was the dog. The Pargeters kept a black bitch who barked at every raft that passed. The kennel was there, and the chain, and the collar on the end of the chain, still buckled.

The third thing was the hush-lamp. There was one in the mill-house window, and it was lit, in daylight. I could see the flame, steady, with the reed set over it the way they are, and the reed was not whistling. A lit lamp with a dumb reed over it. I have thought since that I would give a great deal to have heard that reed.

We went in, because Saul was owed. The door wasn't locked. In the kitchen the table was laid for six, and somebody had cut the bread: six slices, and the knife lying by the loaf with crumbs on it. The bread had not gone hard. That is what I could not stop looking at. It had been cut, and it had not gone hard, and there was nobody.

In the mill the stones were turning, grinding nothing, with the hopper empty. There was flour on the floor as thick as a fall of snow, and in the flour there were footprints, small ones and big ones, six people's worth. They went from the kitchen door across to the far wall. Up to the wall. Not back.

There is no door in the far wall at Lathbury. I went and looked. I put my hand on it. It was a wall.

I said to Saul, We ought to go. He said nothing. I said it again, and then I understood that I had been talking for some time, about the knock in the paddle and the bread and the wall, the way you talk when you are frightened, and he had not answered me once.

He was standing by the stones with his cap in his hands. He was there; I want to be clear about that. I could see him. But he was standing the way a man stands at a graveside, and he had not said Hm in a long while, and when I said his name it seemed to take him a moment to know it was his.

I did the only thing I knew. I hummed. It's my gran's trick, the one every child in the Reach is given with their first spoon: a hand's width, not loud, not still. I took hold of his wrist, and it was as cold as a jug from the cellar. I hummed the first thing in my head, which was a hymn, because it was Hearthday. I walked him out of that mill backwards, the two of us, keeping our eyes on the wall, and I did not stop humming until we were past the lock.

Saul Ainsley is my master still. He paid off the Pargeters' debt himself, out of his own pocket, which nobody understood, and he has never gone up the towpath again. He is a fair man and a good miller, and the Landing likes him. His wife says he was always a quiet one.

He said good morning to me today, the same as every day. I couldn't tell you how it sounded. I've lain awake trying. I have known the man eleven years and I cannot remember his voice. Nor can she, if you ask her plainly.

That's all. That's what wasn't there. If you hear me humming at the lock of a night, now you know why, and you'll do me the kindness of humming back.
