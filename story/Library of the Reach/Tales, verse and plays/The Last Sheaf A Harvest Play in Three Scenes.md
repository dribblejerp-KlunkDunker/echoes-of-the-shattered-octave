# The Last Sheaf: A Harvest Play in Three Scenes

*as played at the edge of the north field, Harvest Home 418; set down by Ursula Dimmock for next year's players · 418 SR · Oakhaven · play*

> The old harvest play as Oakhaven now plays it: a reaper who cannot cut alone, a voice at the gate nobody may answer, and a sheaf that asks to be let end.

A NOTE FOR PLAYERS. The play is older than anyone in Oakhaven. In my mother's day the third part was the Thief of Bread, and the whole field shouted him off. You cannot shout anybody off now, so since the Hedge the Thief has become the Voice at the Gate. He is not shouted off. He is not answered. It works better, I think. It is certainly more frightening.

It is played at the edge of the field by lamplight, at a hand's width, so the watchers must come close. Nobody, player or watcher, is to answer the Voice. Whoever answers brings the cider next year. The Voice may say anything it likes to the crowd except the true name of somebody dead. We are not cruel, only careful.

THE PLAYERS
THE SHEAF, a child in a straw coat, who stands in the last stand and says nothing until the end.
OLD REAPER, with a wooden sickle.
THE BINDER, his wife, who has the sense.
THE VOICE AT THE GATE, never seen; played from behind a hung fleece by whoever has the best ear for other people.
THE VILLAGE, which is everybody else.

SCENE ONE. THE FIELD. Evening.
(REAPER comes on with his sickle and finds THE SHEAF standing very still in the stand.)
REAPER. There she is. Last of the year. I'll have her down before supper.
BINDER. You'll not. Not on your own.
REAPER. I've cut the whole field on my own.
BINDER. And it grew back by morning, every stalk of it, and the bread tastes of less each time.
REAPER. Bread's bread.
BINDER. Bread was bread.
(REAPER swings at THE SHEAF. THE SHEAF does not fall. He swings again. THE SHEAF folds its arms.)
REAPER. She's a stubborn one.
BINDER. The last stand is never cut by one. Everybody knows that.
REAPER. Then fetch everybody.
BINDER. (turning to the watchers) Everybody's here. Look at them, standing about with their hands in their shawls.
REAPER. They'll want feeding first.
BINDER. They always do.

SCENE TWO. THE GATE. Night.
(The fleece is the Hedge. REAPER and BINDER sit with their backs to it, eating. THE SHEAF stands where it stood.)
VOICE. (behind the fleece, in an old woman's voice) Son? Son, come out to the gate. I've brought your supper, and it's hot.
REAPER. (half rising) Mam?
BINDER. (her hand over his mouth) Your mam's been a year in the churchyard with a stone on her tongue. Sit down.
VOICE. (in the BINDER's own voice, exactly) Husband, leave her. Come out. I'm out here. That's not me in there with you.
(REAPER looks from the fleece to his wife, and back.)
REAPER. (muffled) Which of you is it?
BINDER. Which of us has her hand over your mouth?
(REAPER thinks about this, and sits down.)
VOICE. (to the watchers) You there, in the blue shawl. Your boy's out here. He says he's cold. Won't you say something to him? Only a word.
(Here the watchers say nothing, and the Voice goes along the crowd, coaxing, for as long as the player can bear it. The Voice must never be answered. If it is, the play stops, the one who answered is told what they owe, and we begin the scene again.)
VOICE. (at last, softly) Nobody? Nobody at all?
BINDER. Not tonight, thank you.

SCENE THREE. THE CUT. Before dawn.
(THE VILLAGE comes forward round THE SHEAF, all of it, as many as there are. Everybody who has a blade holds it up, and everybody who has none holds up a finger. The tune is hummed, low, sixteen beats, and on every beat every hand makes one cut in the air.)
ALL. (under the breath) Cut it low, and cut it slow, the last of the year is let to go.
(On the sixteenth beat THE SHEAF kneels down. Then, for the first time, it speaks.)
THE SHEAF. Thank you. I was so tired of growing back.
REAPER. Will you grow back tomorrow?
THE SHEAF. No. Not this one. This one you let end.
(THE SHEAF stands up out of its straw coat, and is only a child again. BINDER plaits the coat into the sheaf-figure, and the whole field carries it to the Hedge-gate and hangs it there till spring.)
VOICE. (behind the fleece)
(The Voice says nothing more. It has nothing to say to people who are all together. That is the end of the play.)

AFTERWARDS. Nobody claps, because clapping carries. The players hum the tune once more through, and the child who was the Sheaf is given the first slice of the hearth bread and goes home asleep in somebody's arms. This year Hodge Lorkin answered the Voice in Scene Two, because it did his father very well. He has promised the cider for next year without being asked. U. D.
