# The Duty-Scribe's Day-Book

*the scribe styled Ochre, duty-scribe of the House · 416 SR · the House of the Silent Veil · journal*

> The last entries of the scriptorium's day-book, kept by whoever lit the east stove.

The day-book of the scriptorium, kept by the duty-scribe of the week, who lights the east stove at the second bell and writes here what the House did with its day. Nothing in this book is a record. Records go on vellum. This is only the day.

First day. Lit the east stove at the second bell. Damp in the flue; it smoked until the third. Fourteen at the desks. Names entered: forty-one, all from the Grove, none disputed. Old Gall's hand is going; I have moved him nearer the window. The pool is high.

Second day. Stove lit at the second bell, drew well. Sixteen at the desks. Names entered: thirty-eight. A carter from the Office brought a letter with a seal I did not know and waited in the yard for an answer. The Prior read it in the kitchen and put it in the stove, which I note only because it was my stove.

Third day. Stove lit. Twelve at the desks; four sent down to the stacks to listen, as the roster says, and they came up quiet, as they always do. Names entered: thirty. The third desk from the east, north side, finished the Fenmark register a day early and asked for more. I gave her the Mossbank strays. She is the quickest hand we have had since I was young, and she knows it, and she is right to.

Fourth day. Stove lit late. I overslept; the bell-boy did not wake me, because there was no bell. The Office has asked for the bell. Nobody has said yes. Nobody has said no. Names entered: twenty-six. The carter came back with another letter and did not wait this time.

Fifth day. Stove lit. The Prior spoke to the whole House in the refectory, which he has done twice in my life. He said the Office is the Veil under another name and that the work goes on. He said it well. He did not say where the vellum would go. Names entered: none. We sat at the desks with our pens dry and listened to the stove.

Sixth day. Crates in the yard. Grey wood, Office stencil, numbered. They are very good crates. Two clerks from the Office, very polite, wearing gloves to touch the registers, which no scribe of the House has ever done. They counted the shelves and wrote the count on a slate and did not read a single page. I asked one of them if he knew what was on the shelves. He said, Units. He said it kindly, as if I had asked the time.

Seventh day. The vellum went down the hill. Four wagons. The pool took a wheel and they spent the afternoon getting it out, and every scribe in the House stood at the windows and did not help. I am not proud of that. I am not ashamed of it either.

Eighth day. Stove lit at the second bell. There is nothing to write on. The desks are clean. The third desk from the east, north side, is writing anyway, on the backs of her own letters, the names she remembers from the Grove. I did not stop her. I sat at my desk and wrote this.

Ninth day. The Office has sent word that the House will close for the winter and the scribes will be found other work. Found. I am to go to the Organum and copy what we wrote. Most of us are. Some are going to the Grove to take names, which is a different thing from keeping them, whatever the letter says.

Last day. I have let the stove go out. Whoever lights it next, the flue wants a hand on the damper before the second bell, or it smokes. The day-book stays here. It is not a record. Nobody will take it.

What is written is not lost. I have written that at the top of every page for thirty years, the way the House taught us, without once wondering whether it was true.
