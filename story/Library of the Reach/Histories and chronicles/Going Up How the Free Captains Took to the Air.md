# Going Up: How the Free Captains Took to the Air

*Piet Ollander, keeper of the mast-tower, Port Cantabile · 416 SR · printed on Broadsheet Row, Port Cantabile · history*

> The old keeper of Port Cantabile's mast-tower explains, with no great respect for anybody, how skyships came to be, and why sea-captains who swore they never would have started to fly them.

I have kept the mast-tower at the end of the Long Mole for twenty-two years. It is sixty feet of iron rungs with a cradle at the top, and in the first ten of those years I moored perhaps one ship a month, and every one of them flew green and black and paid me in Consortium paper and looked at me as if I were part of the iron. Now I moor four a day, and half of them are ours, and they look at me as if I were their mother. I liked it better before. Here is how it changed.

Skyships are not ours. I say that first, because the young ones forget it. They were the Consortium's, and before that they were nobody's, because a Fortis-cut core will make a hull light but will not make her rise, and there was no drift-glass to be had. My father's generation never saw one. Then the drills got good. When the runic bore came in, the Consortium began to bleed the Velocity country, and what came out was a green glass, pale as sea-ice, that wanted more than anything in the world to move. Pack it in brass with a reed-valve on the end and it will strain at the valve like a dog at a door. The whole art of the skyship, the engineers say, is to persuade it that the direction it most wants to go is up. They say this as if it were a small thing. I have seen what it does when it changes its mind.

So the first skyships were survey craft, and then freighters, slow and fat under their envelopes, carrying for the Consortium what the Consortium did not trust to a road. A six-day march became a day's flight. The Synod built its own cutters and painted them iron-grey. The Coast watched them go over and spat, and went on sailing, because a Free Captain has a deck and a keel and the sea, and the sea has always been enough trouble for anyone.

Then the Screaming Year, and the sea changed its mind too.

You will have heard. The tides started singing in keys nobody could read. The water would forget, for an hour, that it was water, and go to a blue jelly round a hull, and when it remembered, the hull would be gone. And the trenches: a mouth in the sea, opening under a ship with no warning, with no bottom to it, and closing again on the far side. A captain who had sailed forty years could not tell you on any given morning whether the channel would still be a channel by noon. Hulls began to be laid up at their moorings. Crews went to the bottle-trade, or the drink, or both.

The first of ours to go up was Ysmay Cordell. She bought a condemned Consortium freighter at the yard auction for the price of its brass, patched the envelope with her own mainsails, and took four lift-tanks off a Synod cutter that had come down on the Cay, which is another story and a better one. She named her the Gull's Errand and brought her into my cradle on a still evening with half the Coast on the Mole to watch her fall in the harbour. She did not fall. She moored like a feather, came down my rungs, and said, I am not going back down there, and pointed at the water.

Then came the question of the Articles. The Articles were written for the sea, and the Free Captains will not have a thing they do not have an Article for. Was a ship in the air at sea? If she came over the harbour with nobody at the wheel, did the Ninth give her to the Harbour, as it would a hull drifting in under no hand? If she dropped a crate in the water, did the Sixteenth give it to whoever fished it up? The captains argued it three nights running. The Harbour ruled, in the flat voice it uses for hangings and tide-tables, that anything over the harbour is at sea, however high it goes. There is a rope from my cradle to the Mole-head derrick now, and on the derrick a board, and on the board the Articles, and somebody has chalked under them: and above.

Most of the Coast still sails. The old ones will not fly; they say a ship ought to have something under her she can feel. The young ones will not sail; they have seen too many laid-up hulls. And there is a third sort, which I have moored more than once, who fly and will not even look at the water. They come down my rungs with their eyes on the iron, and walk the whole length of the Mole looking at the town, and if you ask them about the sea they talk about the price of drift-glass, which is a scandal, a hundred and ten sovereigns a tank and rising.

People ask me if the air is safer. It is not. A holed tank does not burst. It weeps, a cobalt smoke, very slowly, and takes the lift out with it, and you have as long as it takes to weep to find somewhere to come down. I have watched ships come in nose-high and crabbing and nobody speaking on deck.

But I will tell you what the captains tell me, at the top of the tower, when they have had a drink. The sea wants you. That is what changed. It always had you, if it could get you, but now it wants you, the way a hound wants. The air does not want anything. It does not care whether you live. Some days, they say, that is a great comfort.
