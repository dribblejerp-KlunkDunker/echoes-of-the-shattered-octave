# The Fires on the Ground

*Orrin Pale, sexton of Oakhaven · 413 SR · Oakhaven · chronicle*

> The Oakhaven sexton's account of the winter after the Fracture, when the orchards blew away, the town starved, and the ground had to be burned before it could be dug.

I am not a writing man. I dig. But the Cantor says that what is written is kept, and somebody should keep this winter, and he has enough to carry, so I have taken it on. These are the dead of Oakhaven from the day the orchards went to the day the wagons came, and what it took to put them down.

The orchards went on a still morning at the back end of summer. I was in the churchyard turning the new plot for old Hesketh's widow, who was ailing, and I felt it before I heard it: a sort of hiss in the air, like milk boiling over on the far side of a wall. Then the wind came off the hills, and the reed-rows below the town, that had sung every wind of my life, did not sing. They chimed once, thin, like a glass knocked with a nail, and then the whole valley of them lifted off their stalks and went past me in a glitter. It got in my hair and my collar and my mouth. The children ran after it with their tongues out because it looked like sugar. It tasted of nothing at all. That frightened me more than the rest of it, and I could not have said why.

By evening there was not a standing reed in the Reach, and the Cantor was walking the rows with his hands open, the way you walk a field after hail.

The milk soured that night, every pail in the town. The dogs began howling at noon, which they had never done, and went on doing it all winter. When I tolled for Hesketh's widow in the first week of autumn, the bell came out flat. Not cracked. Flat, by a hair, as if it were somebody else's bell ringing in the next valley. Every person in the square stopped and looked up at the tower. I rang it out, because she was owed it, and then I tied off the rope. I have not tolled since. The Chapel-reader says it is the same everywhere, that the whole world has gone a little out of true and will come right with prayer. I pray. I have not untied the rope.

Then the grain from the south stopped. The carters said the roads were bad. The carters stopped coming to say it. The Governor stood on the Greenhall step and told us to keep faith and to ration, and we did both, and only one of them lasted.

The winter wheat came up wrong. It came up early and white and very fast, and stood in the field like a crop of straws, and when you split the ear there was nothing in it but a bead of water. Coker the miller ground what there was and wept at the stones, which I had not known a miller could do. By midwinter we had eaten the seed-corn. Everybody knew what that meant. Nobody said it.

Then the frost came. I have dug this ground forty years and never once met frost below the turf. The Reach does not freeze; everybody knows it. That winter it froze a spade's depth down and then two, hard as a step, and the spade rang off it and jarred me to the teeth. So I went to the woodpile.

This is the way I found to do it. The night before a burying I laid a fire on the grave's length, furze first and then whatever would burn, and sat up with it till the small hours, and raked it out and dug the soft hand's depth it had made, and laid another on top, and so on down. Three nights for a grown man. Two for a child. I will not write how many nights there were. I will write who they were for.

Wilf and Alban Wetherby, twins, four years old, the week before Candlefast, in one grave, because I had not the wood for two and their mother said they had never slept apart.

Avis Brindle, who went into the Flume at the sluice-gate. There was nothing to put in the ground. The Cantor sang her down at the gate, and I stood by with my spade because it did not seem right to come without it.

Tilda Hale, nine years.

Mother Peascod, who gave her bread to the Wetherby girl and did not tell anyone, and was found in her chair.

By Candlefast we were boiling harness. I want that written plainly, because people will say afterward it cannot have been so bad. It was the Governor's own carriage-harness first. He sent it down to the Greenhall kitchen himself and stood at the copper while it boiled, so that nobody could say he had kept it back.

Lettie, Osgood and the baby, the miller's three, in the space of nine days. Lambert Coker dug alongside me for the last of them and would not be stopped, and the two of us sat by the fire on his daughter's grave all night, and he told me every word she had ever learned, in order, which I think is the longest anybody has ever spoken to me.

Jonas Garlick, who walked out into the dead reed-rows one night and lay down in the dust, and was found smiling.

Hobday's infant, three days old. Bettany the midwife brought her to me herself, wrapped, and said that was the seventh child I had had off her that winter and she would bring me no more if she could help it, and then she sat down on my step and could not get up for a while.

The Sawtell brothers, both together, of the cold, in the cart-shed where they had gone to save the walk home.

Widow Dimmock. Old Hesketh's sister. Two of the Ackroyd household, whose names their mother has asked me to leave out, and I have. A carter from Greenroot whose name nobody knew, who came up the road with an empty cart to ask if we had anything, and stayed.

That is seventeen, and Hesketh's widow before them, who would have gone in any year. The Cantor sang every one of them. Some nights he sang with nothing left in him but breath, and I could hear his breath going down into the ground with them, and it was enough. He says a song for the dead does not have to be heard by the living. I hope that is true. There were not many living out by then.

In the spring the frost went out of the ground all at once, in a night, and the next morning forty wagons came up the Greenroot road with grain under the sheets and men in green and black walking beside them. The Governor went out to meet them in the square in his good coat. There was a paper. He read it at the table in the Greenhall with the doors open and the whole town standing in the square outside, smelling the sacks, and nobody inside or out said a word. Then he signed it.

I do not know what was in it. I have heard it said since that he sold something that was not his to sell. I will say this, and then I will stop. I am the one man in Oakhaven who knows to the night what it cost not to sign. I have put the spade back on its pegs, and I have let the fires go out, and I do not mean to be the one who tells him he paid too much.
