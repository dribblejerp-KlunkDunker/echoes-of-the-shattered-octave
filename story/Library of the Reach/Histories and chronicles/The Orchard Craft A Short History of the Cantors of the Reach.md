# The Orchard Craft: A Short History of the Cantors of the Reach

*Jessamy Yarrow, Cantor, of the Reedside Conservatory · 396 SR · Oakhaven (the Reedside Conservatory) · history*

> Written while the wind-reed orchards still sang, a Cantor's proud, funny and slightly cross account of what her craft is, where it came from, and why it is not a church.

I have been asked by the Cantor-Master to write down the history of our craft for the new students, because the Conservatory has a library and the library has a gap on its shelf where our history ought to be. I said that the craft has no history, only a great many old women who remember things. She said, then write down the old women. So I have.

Nobody knows how old the craft is. Ask a Cantor and she will hum at you, which is not an answer but is the truth. It is older than the Greenhall, which the Cantors built three hundred years ago as a guildhall for the reed-growers, and where a Cantor still sits at the Governor's right hand on court days and hums when the petitions grow dull. It is older than the reed-orchards. My own teacher, Mother Sefton, who was ninety when she taught me and had been taught by a woman of ninety, used to say that the craft was old when the Synod was a rumour, and that it would be old when the Synod was a rumour again. She said this in the Chapel porch, loudly. She was a great trial to the Chapel-reader.

What is the craft? People from outside the Reach think it is singing. It is not, or not chiefly. It is listening, and then giving back the right note at the right moment, and very often the right note is barely a note at all.

A student comes to the Conservatory at eight, and the first thing she learns is not a song but a breath: four counts in, a long fall out. For a year she breathes on seedlings. Trees hear breath before voice. Children who come wanting to sing find this very dull, and the best of them go on finding it dull for about a month and then stop finding it anything, and that is how you know.

Then she learns the Root note, the note the whole Reach is tuned to: one hundred and seventy-four beats to the second, sung a hand's width above the ground with the mouth nearly closed. That is the note for bringing things on: seedlings, grafts, wounds, babies. A Cantor at a lying-in hums it into a clay jar pressed to the mother's belly, so that the note goes into the body instead of into the room. That trick is older than anything else we have, and it works, and nobody knows why, and every midwife in the Reach has learned it from one of us and pretends she thought of it herself. We let her.

Then she learns the falling fifth, which is the Root note and the note a fifth below it, for letting things down: a fever, a fright, a branch that has grown wrong. And the calling-song, the one loud thing in the craft, for bringing lost lambs home across three fields, which every student practises on the Conservatory roof at dusk and which the whole of Reedside has learned to sleep through. At sixteen, last of all, she learns the felling-song, which is sung to an old tree before the axe: the falling fifth held, let go, and then a note below it that you feel in the floor more than hear. It tells the sap to go down. I have sung it four times in my life and I wept at three of them, and so does everybody, and that is correct.

That is the whole of it. A breath, a note, a fifth, a call, a farewell. Everything else a Cantor does is made of those five things, put together in the right order for the person or the tree in front of her.

We have no catechism. I want that understood, because the Chapel-reader has more than once called the craft a superstition and more than once called it a heresy, and it cannot be both. We do not pray to the trees. We ask them things. A Cantor can lay her palm flat on the bark of an old pear and be still, and the tree will answer one question, yes or no, if you ask it plainly. That is not worship. That is manners.

The Church teaches that holiness is one note held without end, and that the second note is sin. I do not quarrel with the Church; it has its own business. But I will point out, for the students, that there is not one tending in the whole craft that is done on a single note. The fifth is two notes. The felling-song is three. The craft is made out of the distances between notes, the way a hedge is made out of the gaps between the stakes, and anyone who has stood in the orchards in a west wind knows that one note on its own is not a song. It is only a noise that has not been answered yet.

The orchards. The Cantors planted the wind-reed orchards row by row over three hundred years, and tuned them by pruning: you cut the long cane to sound the Root, and you let the others down a little at a time over many seasons, until a whole slope of them gives a chord when the wind comes off the hills. People in Greenroot, three miles off, know the weather by which chord the orchards are singing. There are rows on the south slope that were tuned by women whose names nobody remembers, and they are still in tune. That is our history. It is standing in the field.

Two customs I cannot explain, and will set down so that somebody else may.

First: there is a lullaby, five notes, a turn, the five again lower, and a fall, that every mother in the Reach sings, and every Cantor learns before any other song. It is the oldest thing we have. No one has ever said who made it or what it was first made for. Mother Sefton said it was made for something very large that could not sleep.

Second: on the Longest Night, every Cantor in the Reach goes out to the oldest tree in her orchard and hums into its hollow until dawn. I have done it every year of my life, with frost on my shawl, and I have never once been told why. When I asked Mother Sefton, she said only that it was done in place of. I said, in place of what? And she looked at me for a long time and said, in place of what we used to do, and would not say more, and I do not think she knew.

Every mending borrows. That is the last thing a student is told, and the first thing she forgets. A Cantor's voice is her own body, and whatever she gives out through it comes out of the same account. There are old Cantors in the Reach with no voice left at all, and they are not sorry.

I have written this down not because I am afraid our craft will be lost. Nothing the whole Reach hums in its sleep can be lost. I have written it so that the students have something on the shelf to argue with. If you want to know what this book was trying to say, put it down and go and stand in the reed-rows when the wind comes off the hills. They will tell you better than I can.
