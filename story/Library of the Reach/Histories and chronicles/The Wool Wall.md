# The Wool Wall

*Dorcas Ribble, carder at the Carders' mill · 417 SR · Oakhaven · chronicle*

> The carder who first proposed the Hedge tells how Oakhaven planted it in one season, out of thorn and the fleece of every sheep in the Reach.

It was my idea. I want that written down in my own hand, while there is still time to be blamed for it properly.

The hounds came at the turn of the year. Not into the town. They came to the edge of the orchards on a Massday night and sat down in a row in the dust where the reeds used to be, eleven of them, with their heads on one side, like a congregation waiting for the reader. They did not howl. They listened. In the morning they were gone, and every one of us had lain awake till dawn trying to remember whether we had made a sound.

The Cantor sent to the three nearest parishes for help. Two Cantors came. The third sent a note that said: We have stopped being able to send. They sat in the Greenhall all one afternoon with the Governor and the bench, and talked about walls and fires and singing, and every plan was a loud one, because men's plans are. I was there to bring the bread. I am not on the bench and never wished to be.

I said, it isn't a wall you want. It's a stopper. You pad a cradle against a draught. Pad the town. Wool eats sound; I have stood in the fleece-loft at the mill with forty bales round me and heard my own heart. Plant thorn deep, so nothing can come through it, and pack the thorn with wool, so nothing can hear through it.

Nobody laughed, which was worse than if they had. The Cantor wrote yes in his register, and looked at it, and said he did not feel any better, and nor did I.

He did not see it go in. The hounds had him at the Pellow farm in the spring, and the Hedge went in that summer without him. The Consortium paid. I set that down too, since it is the one time. They sent wagons of blackthorn and hawthorn whips from somewhere south, and bramble crowns in wet sacking, and a young engineer in green and black with a bag of pegs and a measuring chain, who walked the line of it round the town in two days and stuck a peg every ten paces and never once asked anybody what the Hedge was for. He knew. He had the kind face of a man who has been told not to look up from his chain.

The wool was ours. Every sheep in the Reach came to be shorn. The Farrants brought the Downs flocks down off the sheep-walk in one great grey river, and the Wethercombe folk came in behind them with their carts and their bedding, and the Bellwether put up its shutters, and the fold on the Downs was left standing empty with the gate open. We shore in the square, in the lanes, in the Chapel porch. We shore ewes that had been shorn in spring and had barely a fleece to give. The mill book says nine thousand fleeces by the end, and that is not counting what came in loose in sacks from the far farms, smelling of the hill.

We carded it all. That was our part. The mill engine ran day and night until it was too hot to stand by, and then we carded by hand on our knees in the yard, all of us, the old women and the girls and the men who had never touched a card in their lives, by lamp, in our aprons, with our hands bleeding into the fleece. Nell sang the work-song all night, the whole of it and then from the top again, and nobody asked her to be quiet, because it was the last night anybody would have to ask.

This is how it went in. Thorn in a trench as deep as a man's waist. A man's height of packed wool either side of it, rammed down with posts. Thorn again. Wool again. Felt and old sacking hung over the top, straw in the roots, three men deep all the way round. Four hundred hands, and the Governor's two, which blistered on the first morning and which he would not have bound. He worked the north side with the carters and did not speak to anybody, and was there at dusk and there at dawn.

When the last row went in at the North Gate, somebody said we should try it. So the whole town stood on the rise inside the thorn and shouted together, every one of us, as loud as we could, the first loud sound any of us had made since the hounds came. It went into the Hedge like a stone into a pond with no bottom. No echo. Not a whisper of one. It was the most terrible thing I have ever heard, and we cheered it, and then we stood there and were very quiet, because we could be.

Harrow has the gate key now. The gate shuts at dusk.

I go round the Hedge most evenings, along the inside, looking for where the wool has slipped. It has grown a yard since we planted it, the thorn. Everything grows here now, faster than it ought. The flocks are inside with us, in the paddocks behind the tannery and the orchard closes and anywhere there is grass, and one of the Farrants walks them every morning and counts them every night. There are a great many sheep and a great many of us, and the winter is coming, and I have not yet heard anyone say aloud what everyone has worked out. I will not say it either.

I said a stopper. I did not stop to think what a stopper does with what it stops. A cork does not throw the wine away. It keeps it.

I know wool. I have handled it all my life. Wool keeps. It keeps the warmth of the one who wore it, and the shape of them, long after they have taken it off; you can lift a shawl off a peg a week after a woman has gone and still find her shoulders in it. Every sound this town makes goes into my wool now and does not come out the other side. That was the whole of the plan, and it works, and I am proud of it, and on still nights I put my hand flat on the fleece at the cooperage wall, the way a person feels a loaf to see whether it has proved, and it is warm.
