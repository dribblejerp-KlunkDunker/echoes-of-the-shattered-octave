# What Is Written: A Short Account of the Silent Veil, for Novices

*Umber, scribe of the House · 410 SR · the House of the Silent Veil, the Spectral Woods · history*

> An elderly Veil scribe welcomes new novices to the House with a short, proud history of her order, its rules and customs, and its happy arrangement with the Office of the Catalogue.

You have come up the long path through the willows, and stood under the round arch, and read the words over it, though you could not read them, because they are written in the old pitched hand. Here is what they say. What is written is not lost.

That is the whole of our faith, if we have one. The rest of this little book is only the furniture.

The House has stood on its rise above the pool for eleven hundred years. The Veil is older than the House. Nobody knows how much older, which is a strange thing to admit in a house of records, and I admit it. The order kept its books in other places before this one. They were burned, or drowned, or forgotten, which is how we learned to bind the Ledgers as we do. The first of them was bound eleven hundred years ago out of something that is not paper, and what it holds it holds against fire and water and time. You will not touch one for some years. When you do, you will feel why.

There are three rules, and you will learn them before you learn your letters. Write what happened. Write who it happened to. Do not comfort yourself in the margin.

The third is the hard one. You will be asked, in your life, to stand in a place where something terrible is happening and write it down while it happens. That is what the Veil is for. Kings have been crowned and realms have ended and cities have gone under the water, and in every one of those places there was a scribe of the Veil standing a little way off with a board on her arm, writing. We do not help. We do not hinder. We take no side, because a record that takes a side can be unwritten by the other one. This will seem cruel to you at first. It is the opposite of cruel. It is the one kindness that lasts. A thing that has been witnessed truly cannot be made never to have happened, by anyone, ever.

When you are sworn you will set your own name aside, as you set your cloak on the peg at the door, and take a designation from the ink-cupboard, as every scribe of the House has done. Mine is Umber. I have had it fifty-one years and I do not miss the other. When I lay the pen down it will go back in the cupboard for the next novice, as it came to me. It is kept. Nothing here is lost.

The House. The scriptorium is on the first floor at the east end: fourteen sloped desks in two rows, a stove at either end. The duty-scribe lights the east stove at the second bell. On the last day of every month we clean the desks with vinegar, so the whole floor smells of it, and of tallow, and of wet wool, because the Woods are damp and so are we. You will love that smell before your first winter is out, and you will never be able to describe it to anybody, so do not try.

Below the kitchens a stair goes down into the labyrinth. Its passages were cut to the shape of a chord that silences, so that nothing below can be heard above. Below the labyrinth are the stacks, where the roots of the Weeping Canopy come down through the ceiling. Novices are not taken there. On Memoryday the duty-scribe goes down with a lamp and reads one page of the dead aloud, in the flat voice, from wherever the last scribe left off, and comes up again. Nobody has ever been able to tell me to whom she reads it. It has been done every Memoryday for longer than the House has stood, and it will be done every Memoryday after I am gone.

The dead of the Woods are not like the dead of other places. When a soul goes out here the Echo catches it, as the songs say, and the Grove keeps it. So we keep the Canopy, the remembering of the dead, with more feeling than they do elsewhere, and on the night the Grove's water is highest we set lanterns on it, one name to a lantern, read aloud by whoever sets it. You will set your first lantern this year. Choose your name carefully.

The Office. A hundred and ten years ago the Veil came into the Synod's house as the Office of the Catalogue. Some of the old scribes wept, I am told, and three walked out into the Woods and were not seen again. I understand them, and I think they were wrong. Before the Office we were three hundred and twelve scribes with a leaking roof, begging vellum from the Moot. Now we have wages, and grey ink by the cask, and a place in the Organum's great archive where our oldest books are drier than they have ever been. The Office has never once asked us to alter an entry. The Registrar rules a page more beautifully than anyone I have known, and he likes to say that every name is a unit, by which he means that none may be counted twice and none left out. That is the Veil's own rule in a clerk's coat, and I was glad to hear it.

This year the Office has asked the House for twelve scribes for a great register of the Woods: every soul in the Grove and the villages round it, by name and voice and age, the most complete account of any country that has ever been attempted. I have put my own name forward, old as I am. I hope you will one day be chosen for such work yourselves.

It is a great thing to be trusted with every name in a country. I do not think a scribe of the Veil has ever been trusted with more.
