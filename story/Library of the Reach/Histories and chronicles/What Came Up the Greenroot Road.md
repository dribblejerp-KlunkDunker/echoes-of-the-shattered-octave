# What Came Up the Greenroot Road

*Josiah Seddon, under-clerk at the Greenhall · 418 SR · Oakhaven · chronicle*

> A Greenhall under-clerk sets down, very carefully, how the Consortium of Bores came to Oakhaven between the famine spring of 413 and the bore, and what he copied along the way.

I have been under-clerk at the Greenhall for eleven years. I copy what I am given and I file what I copy, and I have never once been asked what I think of it, which suits me. What follows is not what I think. It is only the order things happened in, which is a clerk's business, and which I find is already being forgotten.

Spring, 413. The forty wagons came up the Greenroot road the week the frost went out of the ground. I counted them from the Greenhall step because counting is what I do when I am frightened. Forty, each with a sheet roped over it and a man in green and black walking at the wheel. They stopped in the square and nobody unroped a single sheet. They did not need to. The whole town could smell the grain.

The paper was two sides long. I held the wax. The heading was the Consortium of Bores, and the body was grain against survey: so many wagons now and so many to follow, in return for the right to measure the quarry and the ground beneath it, and first refusal of whatever the measuring found. The Governor read it through twice at the table with the doors open and the square full of people outside, not one of them speaking. Then he said, "Write that the Governor signs for bread," and I wrote it in the margin of the minute, and he signed. His hand is large, and it shook, and the S of his name goes through two lines. I made the fair copy myself. I have it still.

Frostmonth, 413. The surveyors came. Three of them, in grey, with brass staves and a measuring chain, walking the Flume bank by the Carders' mill one pace at a time and writing every pace down. They drove a stake above the sluice and hung a little glass bell on it, and the bell hummed by itself, very low. The Cantor put his hand on the post and said it was our note, coming up out of the ground. I minuted the visit as Survey, Consortium, one day, no charge.

Harvestmonth, 414. The lease. I want to set the words down exactly, because people say them wrong. The Consortium of Bores takes the quarry, the shaft, and such resonances as lie beneath. In return, bread: a cart a week, at a fixed price, for nine years. The Governor read it aloud standing, in his good coat, and did not look at the room. The bench voted. One voice against, and it was the Cantor's, and I wrote it down: Ashgrove, no. Then I wrote the rest, ayes, carried. I have looked at that page a good deal since. It is the neatest page in the book.

415 and 416. The works. You will know them. The fence round the headframe, that hums if you lay a hand on it. The sheds, the stores, the pay-office where the men are paid in the Consortium's paper, which the Consortium's stores take and nobody else's do. The mast, a hundred and twenty feet of riveted iron on four legs bolted into the quarry rim, that took a summer to raise and that every child in the Reach climbed the hill to look at. A Factor came to live in the works house, in black with green at the cuffs. Grain came every week, as the lease said. Nobody went hungry. I want that set down, because it is true.

417\. The bore. They began to sink it and the ground began to ache. You felt it in your teeth before you heard it, a grinding a long way down that has not stopped since. The bells went that year: the Chapel's six and the clock-bell off the Greenhall roof and the little bells in the old reed-rows. A young man of the Church came with the Consortium's cart and said it was a blessing, and the bronze went down the quarry road to be made into teeth for the bore. I minuted it as Requisition, bronze, by order, no charge. That is the second time I have written no charge on a thing that cost us a great deal.

The tankers began after that, once a day, east toward the Organum, green glass sloshing behind iron grilles, and a soldier with a bell for a head walking on either side. The oaks began to ring when the wind moved them. A notice came saying no oak was to be felled, the oaks being now the property of the Consortium, like the quarry, and the Root, and the glass. I had not known until I copied that notice that the Root was a thing that could be owned. I had not known it was a thing at all.

Then we lost the Cantor, and the week after, two men in green and black came to the Greenhall with a crate, and carried his chair out from the Governor's right hand, and put the Queen's seal up in its place: pale green glass, a yard across, with her face pressed into it. I was told to see it dusted.

That is the order things happened in. Here is the one thing I have noticed, and then I will put this away.

Every decree that comes to the Greenhall now comes BY ROYAL ASSENT, under Queen Maelis's seal and signature. I have copied some hundreds of them. I have them in the press beside my desk, and in the same press, at the bottom, I have my fair copy of the paper of 413. When nobody is in the hall I sometimes lay them side by side.

The Queen's hand is small and beautiful and it does not shake. It has not shaken once in a hundred and some decrees. People in the square say that is because she is a hundred and forty years dead, and they say it very quietly, and I do not repeat it.

The Governor's hand goes through two lines.

I am a clerk. I am not supposed to have an opinion about signatures. But I know which of those two hands was afraid of what it was signing, and I know which one I would rather trust.
