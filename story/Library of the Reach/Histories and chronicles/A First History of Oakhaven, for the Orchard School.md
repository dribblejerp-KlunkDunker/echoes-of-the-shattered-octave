# A First History of Oakhaven, for the Orchard School

*Garra Thorne, teacher at the orchard school · 419 SR · Oakhaven · history*

> A schoolteacher's history of Oakhaven in eight short lessons, from three rival stories of its founding to the planting of the Hedge, with questions for her pupils at the end of each.

To the children of the orchard school. This book is to be read aloud in class, quietly, a lesson at a time. When you come to the questions, answer them on your slates. Do not shout out. You know why.

Lesson One. Where Oakhaven stands. Oakhaven is the chief town of the Resonant Reach. It stands on a rise above the Chlorophyll Flume, which is the green river that runs in a channel of grown wood and climbs when it should fall. Downriver, three miles east, is Greenroot. West are the Watch Downs and the combe at Wethercombe. East, a long way east, past the Spectral Woods, is the Organum, which you will not see, and need not. All round us are the iron-oaks, which ring when the wind moves them.

Questions. Which way does the Flume run? Which way does it want to run? Why do we not climb the oaks any more?

Lesson Two. How Oakhaven began. Nobody knows, and so there are three stories, and you may choose.

The drovers' story is that a man was bringing sheep down off the Downs in a storm, and lay down under the great oak on the green, the one we call the Old Haven, and the oak hummed him to sleep, and in the morning he would not leave, and built a house against its roots. That is why it is Oakhaven: a haven in the oaks.

The Cantors' story is that there were Cantors here before there were houses, singing to the orchards, and the town grew up round their school the way a village grows round a well.

The clerks' story is that Oakhaven began when somebody first wrote it down, and the first roll in the Greenhall is three hundred years old, so Oakhaven is three hundred years old.

The clerks' story is the only one with paper in it. It is also the only one nobody believes.

Questions. Which story do you like best? Which story do you think is true? Are those the same story?

Lesson Three. The Queens. Long ago the Reach had its own crown, as every one of the eight realms did. The last of our queens was Queen Maelis. Her face is on the green glass seal in the Greenhall and on every notice at the well-house. She reigns still, at the Organum, among the other monarchs of the Glass Court, and signs the Synod's decrees for us in her own hand.

We do not say more than that in school.

Questions. Copy out the words at the top of the newest notice on the well-house. Whose seal is under them?

Lesson Four. The Greenhall and the orchards. Three hundred years ago the Cantors built the Greenhall for the reed-growers, and carved every beam with reeds and apple boughs and singing birds. Over the same three hundred years they planted the wind-reed orchards below the town, row by row, and tuned them by pruning, so that when the wind came off the hills the whole valley sang. Your grandparents could tell you the weather by which chord the orchards were singing. Ask them.

Questions. Ask someone at home what the orchards sounded like. Write down what they say. If they will not say, write that down instead.

Lesson Five. The year the Spire broke. In 412, far away in the Dawning Heights, the great Spire of Light broke, and the whole world went a little out of tune. On the same morning every wind-reed in the Reach turned to something like glass and blew away as dust. The winter after was the hard winter, the only one anyone remembers, and seventeen people of Oakhaven died of it. The sexton has written an account. In the spring the Consortium of Bores brought grain up the Greenroot road, and the Governor signed for it, and Oakhaven ate.

Questions. Why do you think the Governor signed? What would you have done? (You may leave this one blank.)

Lesson Six. The quarry. In 414 the Consortium leased the quarry north of the town, and the shaft, and what was under it. They built the works, and the fence that hums, and the great iron mast. In 417 they began the bore. The bells of Oakhaven were taken down that year, all of them, and sent to the quarry. That is why there are no bells, and why, if you find a bell, you do not ring it.

Questions. Put your hand flat on the schoolroom floor. What do you feel? Do not write it down. Just know it.

Lesson Seven. The Hedge. In the summer of 417 the town planted the Hedge: blackthorn, bramble and hawthorn, three men deep, packed with the wool of every sheep in the Reach. Everybody helped. Some of you helped, though you were very small, carrying fleece in your aprons. The Hedge swallows every sound we make, so that nothing outside can hear us. That is why we are safe. That is why we murmur. That is why, when you are sent to the gate, you go no further than the gate.

Questions. Name the three trees of the Hedge. What is the wool for? Who keeps the key?

Lesson Eight. The last lesson. When you are grown, someone from outside may ask you what Oakhaven was. You will tell them about the oaks and the orchards and the Greenhall and the Hedge. I hope you will also tell them that the children of Oakhaven learned to murmur before they learned to read, and learned to read anyway, and that this was not nothing.

And one question, as always, to finish.

What is on the other side of the Hedge?

Do not answer this one aloud. Write it on your slate, and look at it, and then rub it out.
