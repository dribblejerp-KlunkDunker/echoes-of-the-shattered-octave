# The Commonplace Book of the Scribe Styled Bistre

*Bistre, scribe of the House · c. 371–414 SR · the House of the Silent Veil, the Spectral Woods · journal*

> Forty years of things one Veil scribe liked: receipts for ink, a prayer for Memoryday, sayings worth stealing, and small observations from a cold room.

Bistre. The designation given me from the ink-cupboard by the Prior's hand, Stillmonth 371. The last Bistre was an old man who left me a cracked horn, a good penknife, and the habit of humming while he ruled a page, which the duty-scribe asks me every week to break.

Things worth keeping, copied as I find them. This is not a record. Records go on vellum. This is only what I liked.

THE INK OF THE HOUSE. Oak-galls, from the oaks along the Organum road, because there are none in the Woods. Crush them, do not grind them, and steep them nine days in rainwater. Never pool water: the old scribes say pool water makes an ink that remembers what it would rather have written. Green copperas, a thumb's weight to the pint, which turns it as black as the second note. Gum, until it hangs from the quill and thinks about it before it falls. Stir with a willow stick. Never with iron.

LAMPBLACK, for the pitched hand. The soot of the east stove, scraped on the last day of the month when the desks are done with vinegar. Work it with gum and one drop of honey, so that it runs in the long held strokes and does not break.

The three rules, which every scribe knows and which I copy here only because a commonplace book without them looks undressed. Write what happened. Write who it happened to. Do not comfort yourself in the margin.

A thing said by the Registrar of the Office to an apprentice, in my hearing, in the year 405: Rule the page before you write on it. A margin is not decoration. A margin is the place where you are not permitted to put your own opinion, and leaving it empty is the discipline of the whole trade. I copy it because it is good, and because I wish I had said it, and because I have been putting my own opinion in the margins of this book for thirty-four years and have no intention of stopping.

BEFORE THE PAGE ON MEMORYDAY, said under the breath on the stair, with the lamp. You who are on this page: I am not the one who forgets you. I am only the one who comes. Hear your names, and hold them a while longer. I will be back in eight days.

There are no birds in the Woods. I have looked for forty years. A novice from the Peaks asked me where they had gone. I said they had never come. She said that was worse, and she was right, and I have liked her ever since.

The pool under the House is highest on the night the Grove sets its lanterns. From the north windows you can see, a long way off through the willows, a glow on the mist where they are putting their names on the water. We set ours from the steps under the arch, one each, and say them in the flat voice, and the pool takes them round the bend and out of sight.

Vinegar day. The whole floor smells of it, and of tallow, and of wet wool. A novice asked me today to describe the smell to her, because she had just come and could not tell it from damp. I could not. I said she would know it in a winter. She asked what I meant. I said: you will know it the way you know your own name, which is to say you will never once think about it until somebody takes it away.

THE OFFICE'S GREY INK, which comes by the cask and is free. It does not bite the vellum. It sits on it, like oil on a pool. It is good for registers that are to be read within the year and poor for registers that are to be read in a thousand. I have written to the Office to say so, in grey ink, as required.

FOR AN INKY HAND. Sorrel juice, pumice, and patience. Nothing takes out the second knuckle. Wear it.

A thing the Prior said to a weeping novice, which I copy without his leave: The House does not ask you not to feel. It asks you not to write it down. Feel all you like. Feel it on the stairs.

412, the Screaming Year. My minium has gone brown on the page, and I will swear on the Rule that it is the same pot. The Prior says it is the light that is wrong, not the ink. A scribe from the south wing says the light is wrong everywhere and the ink knows it. I have written down both and I do not choose.

413\. Sent down to the stacks by the roster, four of us, as the roster says. We came up quiet, as one does. The Rule lets me write this much and no more: it is not silent down there, and it is not sad. It is like standing in a crowded room where everyone is being introduced.

414\. My hand shakes in the cold now. The duty-scribe has moved me nearer the east stove, which I resent and enjoy in equal parts. I have copied the three rules once more below, in the pitched hand, slowly, to see whether I still can.

Here follow the three rules in the old notation: small even marks, each line falling at its end, the held strokes long and unbroken.

Still can. Whoever has this book after me: it is not a record. Read it anyway. It is only what one scribe liked, in a cold room, for forty years, and I liked all of it.
