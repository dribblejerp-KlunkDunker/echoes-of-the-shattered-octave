# Receipts of the Basin Table

*Edith Amory, of the Bell Yard; with additions by her daughter Rosamund · 405 SR, with additions to 415 · the Bell Yard, Whisper-Grove · cookery*

> A Grove cook's receipts for the feast nights on the Weeping Plaza, with her daughter's notes in the back pages and one receipt that was never written down.

**Flyleaf:** Edith Amory,Rosamund Amory

Edith Amory, of the Bell Yard, her receipts. To Rosamund when I am done with them, and not before, and she is not to lend them to her aunt.

The Grove is not a hot-kitchen people. On the feast nights we eat out of doors, on the plaza steps, in the damp, and so we eat things that are good cold and better shared. Everything in this book can be carried in one hand, because on a feast night the other hand is always busy.

One rule before any receipt. Never cook a fish out of a remembering pool. It is perfectly wholesome, and it tastes of somebody else's supper.

LANTERN CAKES, for the night the water is highest. A plain dough of fine flour and butter, one egg to the pound, rolled thin and folded three times and rolled again, and cut in rounds the size of a child's palm, with a thumb-print in each for a spoon of plum or bramble. Bake them the afternoon before, because nobody has a minute on the day. They are for eating on the steps while you wait your turn at the basin. A child with a lantern cake in one hand will not drop his lantern in the water with the other. I have never known it fail, except with Rosamund, once.

STRAW-BOX BROTH. There are no fires on the plaza, by order of the Moot, on account of the colonnade. So the broth is made at noon: barley, leeks, the end of a ham, and a fistful of the cress that grows at the edges of the clean pools, the ones that show you nothing but your own nose. Bring it to a hard boil, then put the whole pot into a box packed with hay, a blanket over the top, and carry it down. At the ninth hour of the evening it will still take the skin off a tongue.

MOOT PASTIES. The Moot sits from the second hour of the afternoon until it stops, and it never stops. Make the pastry short and the filling dry, so they can be eaten on the benches without crumbs or noise: chestnut and mushroom, a little onion, a good deal of pepper. The householders say they come to the Moot for the business. They come for the pasties.

THE REMEMBERING LOAF, for Canopy, the first Memoryday of Ashfall. A round wheat loaf, glazed with milk. Before it goes into the oven, score the top once for each of the house who has gone, and say the name as you draw the knife, not loud. At supper the loaf is broken, never cut, and everyone at the table eats a piece of everyone. Light the candle in the window facing in, so that the light is for the house and not for the street. This year we scored it six times: my mother; my father; my husband's two brothers; the little one before Rosamund; and old Nan, who was nobody's but ate at our table for thirty years and is entitled.

MY CANOPY CAKE, for after the loaf. A dark cake: treacle, ground chestnut, dried pears chopped small, an egg, and the spice. I keep the spice in my head. A cook keeps one thing in her head or she is only a book.

Here the hand changes. The rest is in a younger, faster hand, with the letters leaning forward.

412\. Mother says the pears are not as red this year as when she was a girl, and the plums have no bloom on them. I cannot see it. She says I will when I am old, and that this is the whole trouble with being old: you can see everything that has gone.

413\. Lantern Night. Mother too tired for the steps, so I took the cakes down alone and gave them out along the basin. A small boy from the Carters' Yard asked whether he might have two, because his sister was too big for hers. I gave him two. His sister, who was not too big for anything, took one off him at once.

414\. Mother died in Thawmonth, in her chair by the window, with her shawl on and her feet on the fender. I am told it is a good way to go and I am sure it is. The Echo will have caught her. She was never one to wander off.

Canopy, 414. I scored the loaf seven times. My hand went on the seventh, and I had to stop and start again. Broken, not cut. Everybody at the table had a piece of everybody, and that included her. I lit the candle facing in and sat up with it, which you are not meant to do, and nobody told me not to.

415\. The Lantern Night. Water higher than any year I remember. I made her cakes and they were right. The broth was right. A woman from the Reach was on the steps, lodging with the Lantrys, a lantern on her knees she could not set down; I gave her a cake and she ate it without noticing, which is the best thing a cake can do.

Canopy, 415. I have tried her cake four times this year. The treacle, the chestnut, the pears: all right. The spice is wrong. It is not cinnamon and it is not clove and it is not the pepper off the Organum carts. She never wrote it down, and I never asked, because she was always going to be in the kitchen.

So when I find it, I will keep it in my head, the way she did, and not write it down. Whoever has this book after me will have to come to the Bell Yard and ask.
