# The Lamp Book of the Bell Yard

*the Fellowship of Lamp-Makers and Lighters of Whisper-Grove; kept by Osmer Lowry, warden · 396–415 SR · the lamp-makers' loft, Bell Yard, Whisper-Grove · chronicle*

> The ordinances, rounds and yearly remembrances of the Grove's lamplighters, kept by an old warden for the nephew who will have the book after him.

**Flyleaf:** Osmer Lowry,Kitto Lowry

Osmer Lowry, warden of the Fellowship of Lamp-Makers and Lighters, his book; which is to say the Fellowship's book, while he keeps it. After him it goes to Kitto Lowry, his sister's boy, as soon as Kitto can trim a wick without saying a word his mother would not like.

THE ORDINANCES OF THE FELLOWSHIP, as they were read to me when I was bound, and as I have read them to every boy and girl bound since.

First. A lamp of the Grove is lit at dusk and not before, whatever the Moot says about winter afternoons. The pools light the faces; the lamps light the way. Until the pools come on, the willows give light enough, and oil is dear.

Second. Every lighter knows his round by heart and by count, so that he could walk it blind; and every lighter walks his round the same way every night, at the same pace, so that a child who is lost at dusk has only to stand under a lamp and wait, and the lighter will come by within the hour and know her.

Third. No lamp is left half-lit. A lamp that is begun is finished, or it is put out and begun again. A lamp half-lit is a promise half-kept.

Fourth. The lamp at the Moot-house door burns all night while the Moot is sitting. The lighter of that round sits up with it on the steps, and may listen, and may not speak, for in that room he is not a householder, only the lamp-man.

Fifth. On the night the Grove's water is highest, the Fellowship gives paper and wax and wick for the basin lanterns to anyone who asks, at no charge, and no lighter is to ask whose name the lantern is for.

Sixth. Whoever lights the last lamp of his round says, Goodnight, Grove, not loud. Nobody knows when this began. It is an ordinance because a warden once tried to strike it as foolishness and the Fellowship sat in the loft until morning and would not let him.

THE ROUNDS. There are nine rounds in the Grove, and the Ashlane is the longest because of the steps. The Bell Yard round is the oldest and the shortest: eleven lamps on iron arms, from the Moot-house corner down past the joiners' shops to the turn, and back along the other side to the plaza, so that the lighter ends where he began. By custom the warden keeps it for himself. I have kept it since 396, and my father before me.

REMEMBRANCES. A warden writes a line a year, or more if the year deserves it.

396\. Made warden, nine hands to two. The two were right; I was too young.

398\. New oil up from the Reach, rape oil, cheaper than willow-oil, and it smokes. The Moot complained of the smell. We mixed it half and half and told nobody, and the Moot stopped complaining. Write that down, Kitto: the Moot complains of what it knows.

401\. Kitto bound to me at nine years old. He asked me whether lamps were afraid of the dark. I told him a lamp is the only thing in the Grove that is not, and that is the whole of the trade.

404\. Kitto burned off both eyebrows on the Ashlane round and lit the rest of the round anyway. Fined a penny for language. Paid it himself, and did not argue, which is more than I did at his age.

407\. The water highest in thirty years. We gave out four thousand papers and ran short of wick by the ninth hour of the evening. A little girl came for a lantern for her grandmother and could not say the name for crying, so her brother said it for her, and the people on the steps said it after him, as they do. Ordinance Five. I did not ask.

409\. The colour of the flame, written here so the book remembers it if I do not. At the Moot-house door on a still night the flame is the yellow of a pear in Sheafmonth, with blue at the root.

412\. The Screaming Year. The Fellowship's book ought to know, so I write it. Since the sky went wrong in the south, the flame is not the colour it was. Same oil. Same wicks. Every lighter over thirty says it, and the young ones look at us as though we were simple. It is paler. A pear in Firstfruit, not in Sheafmonth. The Moot has ordered the lighters to go quietly on the rounds, no calling and no whistling, because singing in other places has begun to bring things. We have always gone quietly. A lighter who has to be told to be quiet has never lit a lamp.

413\. The Moot asked whether the lamps ought to be left dark, so as not to be seen from outside. The Fellowship met in the loft and voted to keep lighting, eleven hands to none. I said to the Moot, A lit street is a street where somebody is expected home. They let it stand.

414\. My knees. Kitto has the Bell Yard round, though it is the warden's by custom, and I walk behind him and tell him what he is doing wrong. He does nothing wrong. I tell him anyway. That is also custom.

415\. Kitto made journeyman at Canopy, fourteen hands to none, and the old ones banged the table. He says he means to marry. I have told him a lamp-man's wife sees him at dusk and at dawn and never in between, and he says that is twice a day more than most men manage.

415, later. I have taken the Bell Yard round back for the winter while Kitto does the Ashlane, which wants young knees. Eleven lamps down, eleven back, and the plaza at the end of it with the pools coming on. I find I minded watching more than I mind the knees. It is the best walk in the Grove and I would not give it to anybody.

Kitto. When this book is yours, keep the Ordinances, and add to them if you must, and never strike the sixth. Light the lamps whether or not anyone is coming. Somebody always is.
