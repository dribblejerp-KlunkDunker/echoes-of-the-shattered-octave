# Meditations on the Keeping of Names

*Orpiment, scribe of the House · 413–416 SR · the House of the Silent Veil, the Spectral Woods · philosophy*

> An old Veil scribe's private meditations on her calling, written in the last years of the House, as her own colour fades and the registers are taken away.

These are not entries. Nothing here happened to anyone but me. I keep them in the drawer of my desk, under the blotter, and if the Prior finds them he may burn them in the east stove, which is what it is for.

THE FIRST. ON THE CURVE. The Rule says the world forgets on a curve, steep at first and then slow, and that the House stands at the bottom of it with a cup. I have been thinking about the cup. It is a very small thing to hold under a curve that long. A novice once asked me what happens to the names that miss it. I told her that was why we must never miss. She went away satisfied. I did not.

THE SECOND. ON MY OWN NAME. I gave my name into the House forty-four years ago. It was written once in the Prior's hand and sealed and laid in the cabinet, and I have not missed it, and I am told that not missing it is the proof that it is safe. Some evenings I go and stand by the cabinet. Not to open it; it is sealed. The way you stand outside a room where someone you love is asleep, to hear them breathing.

THE THIRD. ON MY COLOUR. Orpiment is the brightest yellow in the ink-cupboard, and a poison. We grind it wet, with a cloth over the mouth. The scribe who had it before me died very old, so it is a slow poison, or a courteous one. Since the Screaming Year it is less yellow. Not on the page only: in the shell, in the cupboard, under my hand. Everyone over thirty says the same of every colour, and the young ones look at us kindly. I find I mind this more than I can account for. A designation is not a name. And yet I have answered to it for forty-four years, and it is going out like a lamp.

THE FOURTH. ON MEMORYDAY. Tonight it was my turn. I went down with the lamp to the lower hall and read the page aloud, flat and unhurried, from where the last scribe stopped. I do not know to whom I read it. Nobody in the House knows; the Rule says only that it is done. I read it the way you read to a child who has fallen asleep halfway through: you finish the chapter anyway, in case.

THE FIFTH. ON THE STACKS. Eleven times down by the roster, and I have kept the Rule each time and written nothing of what is heard there. I will set down only what I felt, which the Rule does not mention, because nobody who wrote the Rule thought it needed saying. I felt expected.

THE SIXTH. ON A WORD. The Office's clerks came again to count the shelves. They wore gloves to touch the registers, and they did not read a page. One of them, a pleasant young man, called what was on the shelves units. He was not being cruel. He did not know there was another word. I have been turning it over for a month. A unit is a thing that may be counted and not missed. A name is a thing that is missed. The whole of the House, eleven hundred years of it, is the distance between those two words, and I do not think it is a long distance, and I do not think it is getting longer.

THE SEVENTH. ON THE VELLUM. It went down the hill today in four wagons. The pool took a wheel and they spent the afternoon getting it out, and the whole House stood at the windows and watched and did not help. The registers are to be kept in the Organum, they say, drier than they have ever been. I believe them. I have never in my life believed anything that comforted me less.

The desks are clean. There is nothing to copy. One pen in the north row is still moving, and nobody has stopped it. I have been sitting at my own desk with my pen dry, listening to it, the way you listen to rain on a roof you are not sure of.

THE EIGHTH. ON TAKING AND KEEPING. The Office has asked for scribes to go down to the Grove with boards and take names for the great register, and I have been chosen, being quick and old and having nothing on my desk. I have taken names all my life. I have stood a little way off from fevers and floods and once a town on fire, and written down who it happened to. That was keeping. I do not know yet what this is. The letter says register. The letter is very well ruled.

THE NINTH. Ashfall, 416. Tomorrow I go down the hill with a board on my arm. Write what happened. Write who it happened to. Do not comfort yourself in the margin. I have kept the third rule for forty-four years and I am going to break it once, here, at the end of my own book, where nobody will read it: I am afraid, and I do not know of what, and I would give my colour to be wrong.

If I do not come back up the hill, whoever has this desk: the names were never ours. We only kept the door on the latch.
