# Exercises in the Pitched Hand

*a novice of the House, his name scraped from the cover; corrected in minium by the scribe styled Woad · 388 SR · the House of the Silent Veil, the Spectral Woods · manual*

> A homesick novice's exercise book, learning the Veil's notation one mark at a time, with his master's red corrections and one long argument about the motto over the door.

On the cover, a name in a boy's hand, scraped away with a penknife until the vellum is thin. Beneath it, in red: Novices do not write their names on House paper. You will have it back at the door. W.

EXERCISES IN THE PITCHED HAND. For the novice at the fourth desk, south side. Set by Woad.

THE FIRST LESSON: THE MARKS. The pitched hand does not write a word as it is spelled. It writes a word as it is said: how high, how long, and where the breath falls. The common hand keeps what a name means. The pitched hand keeps how it sounds in the mouth that said it. That is why it is ours, and why it is slow.

The line, which is a sound held. The hook up, which is a rise. The hook down, which is a fall. The dot, which is a rest; a rest is not nothing, and it is written. The tie, for two sounds that may not be parted. The stroke through, which is a breath taken. Five heights, ruled faint, and the marks sit on them like birds on a rail, if you have ever seen a bird, which here you will not.

Below, in the boy's hand, a page of marks copied over and over, line, line, hook down, dot, and in red at the foot: Your dots are blots. A rest is small. Make it small.

THE SECOND LESSON: HEAR AND WRITE. I will say a word at the door. Write what you heard, not what you know.

Bread. Willow. Water. Good morning.

In red, beside the last: You wrote good morning rising at the end, the way they say it in the villages, because that is how your mother says it. I said it flat. Write what you heard. Your mother is not in this room.

Beneath that, in the boy's hand, small: I know.

THE VOICE FOR ENTRIES. When you read an entry aloud, read it flat and clear and unhurried, the way a person reads a will. The voice for entries does not rise and does not fall. It is not yours to colour. The name has its own colour. Leave it alone.

On the facing page, in the boy's hand, a list: It is very cold. There are no birds. The stove smokes until the third bell. I miss the noise of the mill. I am not allowed to write this in this book. Beneath it, in red: No, you are not. I have read it, so it is done. Turn the page.

THE THIRD LESSON: THE MOTTO. Over the round arch, cut in the stone in our notation: What is written is not lost. Seven sounds. You walked under them on your first morning and could not read them. By the end of this book you will be able to read them, and write them, and you will never walk under that arch again without hearing them.

First attempt, in the boy's hand. In red beneath: You have put a hook up on lost. You are asking a question. It is not a question.

Second attempt. In red: Better. The tie on written is broken. Do not part what may not be parted.

Third attempt. In red: You have put a dot after not. Why? Below it, the boy: Because I wanted to stop before lost. And in red: So, I expect, did the scribe who cut it over the arch. He did not. Neither will you.

Fourth to eleventh attempts, filling two pages. The red marks get fewer. One is only a tick. One says: The rise has crept back. You are still asking.

At the foot of the eleventh page, very small, in the pitched hand and not in any exercise: a name, three sounds, the last one falling. It is not struck through. Beside it in red: This one is well made. W.

THE FOURTH LESSON: A NAME HEARD AT A DOOR. When you are sworn you will stand a little way off from things, with a board on your arm, and people will tell you their names because they are frightened, or because they are dying, or because nobody else is listening. You will write each one as it was said, the fear in it and the accent and the cough, so that whoever reads it in a thousand years hears that person and not you. Practise on me.

Below, the boy's attempt at the master's own designation, Woad, said at a door. In red: That is very like. You have caught my cold.

The last page. The motto once more, in the boy's best hand: the line held, the tie unbroken, no dot before lost, and the last sound falling to the bottom of the ruling and stopping there.

In red, beneath it: Kept. You may have a designation in the spring. It is not a question. Write it always as if you knew.
