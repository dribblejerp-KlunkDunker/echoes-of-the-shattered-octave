# The Day-Book of a Midwife of the Ashlane

*Grisel Imrie, midwife, of the Ashlane · 414–416 SR · the Ashlane, Whisper-Grove · journal*

> Two years of births in the Ashlane, set down by a brisk and loving midwife: the hard nights, the naming on the water, and the count she means to finish one day.

**Flyleaf:** Grisel Imrie

Grisel Imrie, midwife, of the Ashlane, her day-book, begun in Stillmonth 414 because the last one is full. If found, return it to the house with the blue door at the top of the steps. If I am out, I am at somebody's bedside. Leave it on the step and the cat will mind it.

For whoever comes after me and was not raised in the Grove: we keep our names on the eighth day. The child is carried down to the nearest pool and its name is said over the water three times, once by the mother, once by whoever stands for the father, and once by the midwife, so that the Grove has heard it from three mouths and will not let it go. The Church's man says the custom is harmless. Nobody asked him.

3 Stillmonth 414. The cooper's wife at the top of the lane. A girl, seven pounds and a bit, the fourth hour of the night, and she came out shouting at the cold, which is right and proper. I sang her down low, the way we all do now, and put her to the breast, and the shouting stopped, and her father sat on the stairs and wept. There are no hounds in the Ashlane and there never have been. But since the Screaming Year every father in the Grove listens to his own child as though it were a bell rung in the street, and I do not blame them.

11 Stillmonth. Said on the water: Petronel. The mother said it, the grandmother stood for the father, who is away with the willow-cutters until spring, and I said it third. The pool showed the grandmother a kitchen none of us knew, which she says is a good sign. I said that every sign is a good sign after a safe birth, and she said that was not how signs worked, and we were both right.

Thawmonth. Nine this month and every one of them whole. I am too old to write that without touching wood, so I have touched the table.

Reedmonth. A first child at the fuller's, a boy, eleven hours of it, and the mother so frightened of being heard that she would not push. I said to her, dear one, you may shout at me all you like. Whatever is out there is listening for singing, and nobody in the history of the world has ever mistaken this for singing. She laughed, and then she pushed. Said on the water on the eighth day: Bede. A good plain name. He will grow into it.

Firstfruit. Hot. The pools steam at dusk. I have told every mother due this month to sit with her feet in a basin and her opinions to herself, and none of them will do either.

14 Longsun 415. The fuller's girl again, her second lying-in, and it was two. The first came roaring. The second came blue and still, and would not take a breath, and I did everything there is: the cold water and the warm, the rubbing, my own mouth over its face. The old women say a child that will not cry is a door left open. I do not hold with sayings. I hold the child upside down.

For a long minute it would not. Then the first one, in her mother's arms across the room, roared again, very loud and very rude, and the second one opened its eyes and roared straight back at her. Would not be outdone. In thirty-one years I have never seen anything so like a sister. Said on the water: Columb and Sabra. I said both names twice over, to make up for the fright, and nobody stopped me.

Dustmonth. A girl from the Cloister choir came to my door to ask whether babies can hear singing before they are born, because the choir-mistress had told them so. I said yes, but that mostly what they hear is their mothers' stomachs. She was disappointed in me. I told her to sing to her aunt's belly anyway, softly, because it would do the aunt good whatever it did for the baby.

Harvestmonth. Fourteen this month. I am tired in a way that sleep does not mend. I have taken a girl from the Bell Yard to carry the bag and learn. She is good with mothers and hopeless with fathers, which is the right way round.

Lambmonth 416. The lamp-man's boy from the Bell Yard has married, and his wife is expecting at the back end of the year, and they came to me together to ask what they ought to be doing. I said: nothing, and less of it. He is to keep the lamps lit on the stair. She is to eat.

Sheafmonth 416. Two clerks of the Office came up the steps with a great book for the register, very civil, with gloves on, and asked for every child I have brought since 400, with the date and the house and the voice. I asked what they meant by the voice. They said: the cry, whether high or low, strong or thin. I said that a cry is a cry, and that I do not write down how a baby sounds any more than I write down how it smells, which is lovely. They wrote that down, word for word. I gave them the names, because a name is not mine to keep back. I did not give them this book.

Sheafmonth, later. The works at the end of the Bell Yard go all night now, a grinding you feel in your teeth more than hear, and it comes up the lane when the wind is wrong. The mothers ask me whether it will harm the babies. I tell them babies have heard worse, and I believe it, and I do not like it either.

I have counted tonight, for no reason except that the lamp was good and the kettle was on. Since I was seventeen and held my first one for my mother: one thousand and ninety-one, and every one of them said on the water. When I am too old to kneel I mean to sit at the top of the Ashlane steps of a morning and count the ones who go by and say good morning, little mother. I expect it will take me the whole day.
