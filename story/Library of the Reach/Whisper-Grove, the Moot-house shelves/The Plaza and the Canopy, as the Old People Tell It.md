# The Plaza and the Canopy, as the Old People Tell It

*Carrick Thackray, clerk to the Moot of Whisper-Grove · 411 SR · the Moot-house, Whisper-Grove · history*

> The Moot's own clerk writes down what the Grove believes about its great tree, its drowned-lamp basin and its quarrelsome Moot, giving both versions wherever the old people disagree.

**Flyleaf:** Carrick Thackray,Joliffe Thackray

Carrick Thackray, clerk to the Moot, wrote this. For Joliffe, my grandson, who asked me how old the plaza was and would not take very for an answer.

The Moot asked me at its Sheafmonth sitting to set down a history of the plaza and the Canopy before the old people who know it are all gone, which is true, and was rudely put. I have done my best. Where the old people agree, I have written it plain. Where they disagree, which is everywhere, I have written both, and the reader may choose. I am a clerk, not a judge. The Moot pays me by the page and not by the verdict.

OF THE CANOPY. From any street in the Grove, if you look up past the roofs, you will see it: wood and crystal grown together into one thing, silver at the root and pale as ice where it goes up into the mist, so tall that nobody has ever seen the top. Nobody has ever seen it grow, and nobody remembers it shorter.

The old people say it was the first willow, and that the Grove grew up around it because people like to live near something that remembers. They say its roots run under the whole of the Woods. The scribes up at the House of the Veil say the roots come down through their own cellars, a long day's walk from here, and the scribes do not tell lies, though they do not tell much else either.

The old people say also that when one of the Grove dies, the Echo catches them, and the Canopy keeps them. That is why we keep Canopy, on the first Memoryday of Ashfall, with a candle facing in. My grandmother said that on a still night you could hear them if you put your ear to a root, saying their names over to themselves, the way children say their letters. I tried it when I was eight. I heard my own blood going. My grandmother said that was the same thing.

The Church says otherwise. The Church's man, who is a decent fellow and eats with the Moot on feast days, says that the Canopy is the Spire of Memory, one of the eight that sing the laws of the world, and that it sings the law by which things are remembered, and that the dead are with the Pure Note and nowhere else. I have written down both. I do not choose.

The pools are the Canopy's too, or so the old people say: it drinks from them, and dreams into them, and that is why a pool will sometimes show you a stranger's kitchen, or a stranger's morning. Children are taught to look and not to stare. It is somebody else's, and you would not want them staring at yours.

OF THE PLAZA. It is the size of four fields, all white marble, with a colonnade on three sides and the Moot-house on the fourth. The old people say the Moot-house was put on the fourth side so that the Moot should always have the whole Grove in front of it, and the whole Grove should always be able to see whether the Moot was awake.

It was laid, they say, in the time of the crowns, before the Reckoning, by masons who were paid in nothing but supper for forty years, because the Grove had nothing else to pay them in. That is why a Grove mason will still not take a fee for mending the plaza, only his supper, which the Moot finds very convenient and does not mention.

Down the middle runs the basin, cut long and shallow for the lamps. Here the old people disagree. Some say the first lantern was set by a woman whose son went out into the Woods and did not come back; she put a lamp on the water so he could find his way, and said his name aloud, and her neighbours said it after her. He did not come back. The lamps did, every year after, on the night the water is highest. Others say the custom is older than the basin, and that the basin was cut for it and not the other way about, and the scribes at the House take this side, and will show you nothing to prove it.

OF THE MOOT. Every householder may sit. Anyone may speak once on a question, and twice if they are very old. Nothing is decided until the lamp-man has lit the lamp on the Moot-house door, which is an old rule and a good one, because it means nothing is ever decided in a hurry. The longest Moot in the rolls sat nine days on whether the plaza steps should have a rail. It ruled that they should not, because a rail would spoil the steps for sitting on. Nobody has fallen off them since that anybody will admit to.

Above the hall is the gallery, where the light is best, and behind it the muniment store, where the rolls are kept, tied in blue. Below is the cellar, where the Moot keeps its cider in a barrel made by a cooper of the Ashlane. The barrel has not leaked in eleven years, and the cooper is insufferable about it.

Along the west wall the Moot keeps the town's books: anything a householder leaves to the Moot, and the books that travellers bring. Long ago two houses of the Ashlane fell out over whose family book should stand on the top shelf, and the Moot sat three nights on it, and ruled that the town's books should be shelved by size and not by name, so that no house's book stands above another's: the tall ones at the back, the small ones on the left. The clerk shelves them. I have shelved them for thirty-one years. It is the most restful work in the Grove, and it is the only ruling of the Moot that nobody has ever asked to have reversed.

OF THE HOUSE. The scribes of the Veil keep their House in the deep Woods and come down to us when there is something to write. For as long as the rolls go back, the Moot gave the House its vellum, and the House copied the Moot's rolls fair once in every generation and kept the copy, against fire. Since the Veil was taken into the Synod's Office the scribes come with grey ink, and are very polite, and do not stay for supper. We liked them.

That is the history, or as much of it as the old people would agree to let me write. The Moot has met on this plaza for longer than anybody can count. In all that time it has never once agreed about anything, and it has never once failed to meet. I think that is what a town is.

Joliffe. When you are a householder, go to the Moot. Sit at the back. Speak once a year. And when the Moot rises, put the books back the way you found them: the tall ones at the back, by size, not by name.
