# The Carrying of Ketil

*anonymous; as it is said at Skarth in the Iron Peaks, written down by a carter · c. 409 SR · Skarth, the Iron Peaks · poetry*

> A blunt mountain saga of a daughter who carries her dead father to the standing-ground while the weight of the world will not stay still.

This is said standing.
Sit, and you are not listening.

Ketil of Skarth kept goats on the high shelf.
Grey goats. Bad-tempered. Good milk.
He had one daughter. Hild.
He had one son. Arnulf.
The son was light.
That is all that needs saying of the son.

In the tide-month the mountain forgets its sums.
A stone weighs a stone at breakfast.
At noon it weighs a cart.
At dusk a goat floats off the shelf, bleating,
and comes down a mile off, surprised.
The Peaks were not always like this.
The old men say so. The old men are heavy.
We believe them.

Ketil died in the tide-month.
He sat down on the shelf with the milk-pail.
He did not get up.
That is how a heavy man dies. Sitting. Nothing spilt.

The dead of Skarth are not laid down.
Laid down, a man is only a man.
Stood up, the mountain carries him.
The standing-ground is above the shelf, under the Tooth.
Half a day's climb with a goat.
With a father, longer.

Arnulf said: wait for the tide to turn.
Hild said: the tide does not wait.
Arnulf said: the priest will come in the thaw.
Hild said: the priest is light.
She took the rope.

She put her father on her back
and tied him to her
the way you tie a kid that cannot walk.
His beard was in her ear.
He smelled of goat. He always had.

At the first cairn he weighed what a father weighs.
At the second cairn he weighed what a house weighs.
Her knees went into the scree.
She did not set him down.
Set down, a man that heavy stays down.
She stood in the scree until her legs were stone.
Then the tide went over,
and he weighed what a father weighs again,
and she went on.

At the third cairn he weighed nothing.
This was worse.
He rose off her back like a held breath.
The rope sang.
He wanted the sky. The sky wanted him.
She lay on the rock and held the rope
and the dead man stood up on the air above her
with his arms out
like a man about to dance at a wedding,
which he never did, being heavy.
She held.
She told him: not yet.
She told him: you are not light.
I will not have it said.

Night. Cold. The Tooth black on the stars.
The tide turned at the dead hour.
He came down into her arms
and weighed what a father weighs.

At the standing-ground the stone was soft.
Under the Tooth, where the old dead stand,
the ground gave under her heel
like bread.
She heard no ring when she struck it.
Only the dull thing. The dead note.
A light woman runs from that.
Hild did not run.
Running is heavy. Heavy goes through.
She stepped back in her own footprints,
one, and one, and one,
and she dug where the rock still rang,
and she stood him up in it,
and she put the stones about his knees,
and the mountain took his weight.

That was the bargain.
She had carried him far enough.
The mountain would carry him the rest.

She came down at noon.
Arnulf was at the shelf, milking.
He did not ask. She did not tell.
The goats were bad-tempered. Good milk.

At the fair in the thaw a stranger asked her,
the way it is asked:
You're heavy?
She said: heavy enough.
She did not say what she was carrying.
She was carrying him still.

This is said standing.
Now you may sit.
