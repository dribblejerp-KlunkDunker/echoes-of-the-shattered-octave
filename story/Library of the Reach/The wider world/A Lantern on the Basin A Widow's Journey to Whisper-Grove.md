# A Lantern on the Basin: A Widow's Journey to Whisper-Grove

*Tabitha Fernly, of Greenroot Hamlet in the Reach · 415 SR · printed at Oakhaven for the author · travel*

> A Reach widow walks to Whisper-Grove to say her son's name aloud on the night of the lanterns, and writes down the city that took her in.

I am not a writer and this is not a proper book. The printer at Oakhaven says it will make forty copies and that is a book whether I like it or not. I am writing it for anyone in the Reach who has a name they cannot say, and for the Lantry house in the Bell Yard, who will laugh at it.

My son Rafe went out to the reed-beds at Greenroot in the Screaming Year to bring in the last of the cutting, and he did not come home, and there was nothing to bring home. It was the hounds, we think. In the Reach we bury our dead in silence, and the Cantor sings them down, and that is right, and I would not change it. But I had nothing to bury and nothing for the Cantor to sing over, and so for three years Rafe was not buried and not anything. I could not say his name. If I said it in the house it went nowhere. If I said it outside I was afraid of what might hear, and answer.

The carter's wife at Greenroot, who is from the Woods by her mother, told me that in Whisper-Grove there is a night every year when the water in the plazas is highest, and the people float paper lanterns on the long basin, one for each of their dead, and say each name aloud as they set it down. And she said that there, a name said aloud is never lost, because the whole Grove remembers it for you.

So I went.

THE ROAD

From Greenroot the road goes east through the orchards and then the orchards stop being ours. You will know the Woods by the willows. They are silver, not green, and they weep right down into pools that shine from underneath, so that at dusk your own face is lit from the chin like a child telling a ghost story. The mist is the colour of lilac, and it lies at the height of your knees all day, and you walk through it like a woman wading. There are no birds. I noticed that on the second day and it frightened me, and the carter I rode with said, kindly, that there never were, and that the Woods did not miss them.

I had expected the people of the Woods to be sad, because the Woods look sad. They are not. They are the most courteous people I have ever met. The carter called me "little mother" from the first mile, though I am older than him, and every woman at every well called me "dear one", and at the inn on the third night the landlord's girl asked if I would like my supper "now, or presently, or when you are quite ready, dearling", and I cried, which was foolish, and she did not seem to think so.

THE CITY

Whisper-Grove is marble, white marble gone soft and grey with moss, and it is sunk into its own hollow so that you come down to it by steps from every side, and the great spire of the Canopy stands over all of it, wood and crystal grown together, so high that the top of it is in the mist and you cannot tell where it stops.

I lodged with the Lantry house in the Bell Yard, which the carter's wife had written to. There are nine of them, three of them children, and old Isbet Lantry at the head of the table, who is eighty and has a voice like warm milk. They gave me the room at the top of the stair, which is very steep, and Nonie, who is six, showed me the pools in the courtyard. She said the pools remember. I looked into one and saw a kitchen I have never stood in, and a woman's floury hands, and a loaf going into an oven, and it was not frightening at all. Nonie said that is only somebody's morning, and somebody always lets you have a bit of theirs.

In the evenings, from the Cloister road, you can hear the children of the charitable choir at their practice behind shut doors. They sing very low, in the Narrow, as we do at home. I did not know that children could sing so quietly and so clear. Old Isbet said they are the pride of the Grove and the despair of their teachers, both.

THE NIGHT OF THE LANTERNS

The Weeping Plaza is a square of white marble the size of four fields, with a colonnade on three sides and the Moot-house on the fourth, and down the middle of it runs a long shallow basin. On the night the water is highest, it is brimming, and lit from under, and still.

They came down in the dusk from every street. Thousands. Old Isbet said seventy thousand live in the Grove and every one of them comes, and I believe it, because I have never seen so many people and heard so little. They do not crowd. They wait their turn at the edge of the water. Each one kneels, and sets a paper lantern on the basin, and says a name aloud, not loud, but aloud; and then, this is the thing, the people nearest say the name after them. Softly. So that it is said by more than one mouth, and held by more than one.

I had made my lantern at the Lantry table, badly, and Nonie had mended it. When it came to me I knelt down at the water with my knees wet through and I could not do it. Three years. My mouth would not.

Old Isbet put her hand on my back, and waited, as if we had all night. Then I said it.

"Rafe Fernly."

And the Lantry house said it after me, all nine of them, and the strangers beside them said it, and the strangers beside those, a little way along the basin, so that I heard my son's name go away from me along the water in other people's voices, kindly, the way you pass a child along a line of hands to keep him out of a river.

The lantern went out on the basin with all the others. There were so many that the whole plaza was lit, and the mist over it, and the colonnade threw its shadows up into the fog.

COMING HOME

I am home at Greenroot now. I have said his name four times since, in my own kitchen, and nothing has answered, and nothing has heard, and that is right.

I will go back to the Grove next year for the lanterns, and I will take this book with me. I will give one copy to the Lantry house, who will laugh at how I have made them sound grander than they are, and say their stair is not so steep. And I will leave one at the Moot-house, on the shelf where they keep the books that travellers bring, so that the next woman from the Reach who has a name she cannot say will know where to lodge, and what to do, and that she will be welcome.

In Whisper-Grove nothing is ever forgotten. I have left him there, where he is safe.
