# On the Constancy of the Light: A Treatise for the Provinces

*Canon Placidus Sorell of the Basilica, Sol-Luminary; rendered into Low Common by Eustace Garner, curate · 410 SR · Sol-Luminary, the Dawning Heights · philosophy*

> A Canon of Sol-Luminary explains to the lesser provinces, kindly and at length, why the Light is the first of the laws and can never fail.

THE TRANSLATOR TO THE READER

This little work was written for the instruction of the faithful in the provinces below the cloud, and it is my privilege to put it into the common tongue for the chapels of the Reach. I have rendered the Canon's remarks upon our own province faithfully, as a translator must, and I ask the reader to receive them in the spirit of a physician's candour, which can sting and is meant to heal. E. G., at the Chapel of the Pure Note, Oakhaven.

I. OF THE ORDER OF THE LAWS

Every child is taught that the world is held up by eight Spires, and that each Spire sings one law. It is less often taught, and it is time it was, that the eight are not equal.

Consider them. Growth is a rustic law. It knows one word, which is more, and says it to the cabbage and the cancer alike. Heat is a law of furnaces and its people are scarred and loud. Mass is a law of weight, which is to say of falling. Time is a merchant's law and keeps accounts. Velocity is the law of sailors and of the late misfortune in the Valley, which I attribute, in charity, to the coarseness of that law and not to any fault of its stewards. Memory is a melancholy law. Decay is not spoken of at table.

And Light? Light is the law by which all the others are seen. Without it Growth would be a rumour in the dark, and Heat would be a cough in a cellar, and Mass would be a thing that bumps you. It is the first law, because it is the condition of the others being known at all, and its note, five hundred and twenty-eight, is the note to which every Sovereign rings when it is dropped upon a good floor. The coin of the realm agrees with me. I can think of no higher authority below the Basilica.

II. OF THE ONE NOTE

Q. What is holiness? A. One note, held without end. Q. What is sin? A. The second note.

The provincial reader will forgive me if I press this upon him, for he lives where it is least observed. I have heard the Cantors of the orchard country, on a visit made in my youth, sing two notes together over a sick child, and I do not doubt their kindness, but kindness is not the measure of a thing. The second note is a crack in the bell. A people who sing in two notes will in time think in two minds, and a man of two minds will cast a shadow, as we say here, at noon.

The Light sings one note and has sung it since the world was pinned. It does not harmonise. It does not need to. It is entire.

III. OF SHADOW

A shadow is the place where the Light has been refused. It is not wicked in itself, any more than a stain is wicked; but it is a refusal, and a refusal repeated becomes a habit, and a habit becomes a province. I say nothing against the orchard country, whose cider I have taken with gratitude. I say only that it is green and dim at noon, and that a people who live under leaves must take especial care of their souls.

In Sol-Luminary we build so that no wall throws a shadow on another. Our children do not know the word for the dark side of a street. When they go down below the cloud they are sick for a week, and I count that sickness an honour to them.

IV. OF THE GLASS

The Aether-Glass that comes from the Dawning Spire is the Light's surplus, as all the Synod's learned agree: a generosity so great that it overflows the stone. It is blue at the root, as a flame is blue at the root, and pale as it cools. There are those who say the drawing of it wearies the Spire. These are people who have never stood beneath the Spire. It does not weary. You may as well say the sun is tired of rising.

V. OF THE DAWN

Every morning of the world the Sky Choirs have sung the dawn service, and every morning the dawn has come, and the faithless say this is a coincidence. I say it is an answer. The Light is attended. It is sung to, in its own note, as a king is addressed in his own tongue, and it is pleased.

It is spoken of in the Basilica that a greater offering is being prepared, a unison such as has not been sung in the history of the Church. I may not say more. I will say that I have stood beneath the Spire at sunrise, when the chord comes down the mountain over two hours like a tide of gold, and that I have never heard so much as a tremor in it, and that I do not expect to in my lifetime, or in yours, or in the lifetime of the world.

VI. A CONSOLATION FOR THE PROVINCES

Let the lesser provinces take comfort. Your oaks may ring and your sands may sing. Your weights may wander and your seas may forget themselves. Here and there a law may falter; we hear of a valley where the rain stands still. But look up. Whatever else in the world may fail, the Light will not fail. The red of the Sol-Luminary rose is the reddest thing in creation, and it will be as red when your grandchildren's grandchildren come up the chains to see it.

Of all things under heaven, this alone is certain: the Light does not tire.
