# Songs of the Long Mole: Shanties of the Rust Coast, with Their Soundings

*collected by Jacomina Roe, printer, of Broadsheet Row · 417 SR · Port Cantabile · song*

> Five working songs of Port Cantabile, taken down from pilots, widows and wet-nurses by a printer who explains what each one is for.

TO WHOEVER BUYS THIS

A Coast shanty is not for pleasure. It is a chart you can carry in your mouth. The verses hold the pitch of the reefs, the set of the current and the turn of the channel, and a captain who forgets a verse loses a ship. That was so when I was a girl, and it is still so, with two differences.

The first is that we sing them low now. In the Screaming Year a capstan chorus on the Long Mole brought the first hounds in off the harbour, and since then every song on this Coast has been sung under the breath, the way you would sing to a sleeping child in a house with thin walls. The second is that the coral has gone flat since the Fracture, by a hair, so the old soundings are a hair wrong, and the pilots sing them flat on purpose to come out right. I have printed the words as they were. Sing them a little low, in both senses.

I. WHAT'S SHE SINGING

The greeting of the harbour, made into a pilot's catechism. One voice asks; the leadsman answers; the helmsman steers by the answer.

What's she singing? She's singing high.
Then the Cay's awash and the Grinners are dry.
Keep the Mole-light over your starboard rail,
and the Grinners will grin, but they'll not get your sail.

What's she singing? She's singing low.
Then the bar's gone soft where it used to show.
Lay her off by a cable, lay her off by two,
for the sand that held your father won't hold you.

What's she singing? She's singing wrong.
Then she's changed her key and the channel's gone.
Hand the lead, hand the lead, and the lead again,
and say what she's singing to the next poor men.

The last verse is new since the Fracture. My father never sang it. Every pilot I asked has sung it to me, and not one of them would say who made it.

II. CHORUS TO YOU

On the Coast a debt is holy, and the oath is "I'm chorus to you": I owe you, and I will pay, if not in coin then in the singing of your verse when you cannot. This one is sung at the capstan when a ship is warped out owing money, which is most of them.

I'm chorus to you for the rope and the tar,
I'm chorus to you for the drink at the bar,
I'm chorus to you for the berth and the bread,
and I'll sing out your verse if they number you dead.

So heave, and she'll go, and heave, and she's free,
and the sea takes the rest, and the rest is the sea.

The Harbour-Queen is said to dislike this song. It is said she dislikes it because it is true.

III. THE MOLE-BELL

When a soul goes into the water, the bell at the Mole-head is struck once. Once for each. It is not rung for traitors. This is sung by the women on the Mole while they wait for a boat that is late, and it is the only song on the Coast that has no soundings in it at all, because it is not meant to bring anybody in.

One for the man who went over the side,
one for the boy that the tide took wide,
one for the wife at the top of the Stair
who counts them, and counts them, and counts them there.

Strike it once, and once is enough.
The sea does the rest of the ringing.

IV. THE BOTTLE

Since the Screaming Year there has been a trade on this Coast in bottles held over the trench with the stopper out, to catch what comes up. Some say it is the voices of the drowned. I will not argue with a widow about what she has heard in her own kitchen. But here is the song the old hands teach the young ones, and every line of it was paid for by somebody.

Not in the harbour and not in the hold,
not on a deck, however you're told,
not by a lantern, not for a lark,
open her out at sea, and dark.

Ear to the reed and never your lips,
and listen for breath that comes back and drips;
for a bottle that answers is one thing, son,
but a bottle gone quiet is the worst one.

And if it goes quiet, you know what to do:
the maul to the seacock, and over with you,
and row for the light that the Mole-bell shows,
and never look back at the place where it goes.

A note for landsmen. A quiet goes to the light. That is why no Coast man takes a lantern into a hold that has gone silent, and why the song tells you to open a bottle in the dark. I do not know more than that, and I have not tried to.

V. WENDMERE RINGS THREE

This is the newest song in the book and the one I sing myself. Wendmere was a fishing town south of the Cay. In the Screaming Year the shelf gave way under it in a single afternoon and it went down whole, tower and all, and since that first winter its bell has rung under the water, out past the Cay, three strokes and a silence, three strokes and a silence, every night of the year. Nobody has rowed out to see what rings it. The opinion of the Coast is that a bell which rings the drowned to sleep is doing a service, and that you do not go poking at a service. The Lantern Yard learned to sleep by it within the month. The wet-nurses made this to its count, and there is hardly a child in the lower town who has gone to sleep without it.

Hush now, hush, and listen to me,
out past the Cay, Wendmere rings three.
One for the boats and one for the bay,
and one for the people who went away.

Hush now, hush, and what does it say?
It says they're sleeping under the bay.
It says the water is kind and deep,
and it rings for them so that they can sleep.

Three for the drowned and three for the deep,
and three for my darling, and go to sleep.
