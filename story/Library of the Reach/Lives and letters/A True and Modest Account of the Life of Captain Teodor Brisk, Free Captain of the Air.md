# A True and Modest Account of the Life of Captain Teodor Brisk, Free Captain of the Air

*Captain Teodor Brisk, written by himself, with a note by his bosun, Walter Gurney · 417 SR · Broadsheet Row, Port Cantabile · autobiography*

> A Rust Coast captain who left the sea for the sky tells his own legend at full sail, and his bosun is given the last page to correct it.

I was born on the Rust Coast in a storm so famous that three separate taverns on the Long Mole claim it, and I have drunk free in all three on the strength of it. My mother said it was a squall. My mother was not a reliable witness; she was busy.

I signed the Articles at fourteen, like every man on this coast, and I could read them, which was rarer. I read them all the way through before I signed, and the old captain who held the pen laughed so hard he had to sit down. I tell young men this so they will know it can be done.

By twenty I had run the Roaring Strait eleven times by night with no lamp, steering by the pitch of the tide. Any child on the Coast can tell you what the sea is singing, but it takes a captain to hear when she is lying. I have been round Cape Dissonance in a gale with the coral howling under the keel like a choir that has been told bad news. I have wrestled an eel forty feet long in the shallows off the Cay with nothing but a boathook and my natural charm, and I have the scar. I have outrun a Synod frigate across open water in a coaster so slow that the gulls used to land on her to rest. I have dined with the Harbour-Queen herself, at her own table, and she laughed at my stories, and she does not laugh at many.

I have never once been afraid. I mention it only because people ask.

In the year after the Fracture the sea became unreliable in its manners. You will know what I mean if you are from the Coast, and if you are not, I will tell you plainly. The tides began to sing in keys nobody had charted. Water forgot what it was, here and there, and went soft and blue and thick as jelly, and a good hull would settle into it at her moorings like a spoon into custard. Holes opened in the sea. Not storms. Holes. I watched a coaster go down into one off the Cape with all her sails set, and the water closed over her afterward without a ripple, and there was no sound from it at all.

I am not a fool. I sold my hull while she was still a hull, and I bought a ship that does not touch the water.

She is the Fair Excuse. Ninety feet of patched grey envelope, a gondola built by a man who drank, and four lift-tanks under the keel that burn blue at night and hum like a congregation that has forgotten the words. On a still night you can hear her coming two miles off. She sounds like a barn being dragged across the sky by somebody who loves it. I have flown her from the Coast to the Reach and back more times than I have counted, and I have carried anything you care to name. Black glass. Rum. Salt mutton. Cheese, which is worse than glass for the smell. A bishop, once, who was sick on my boots and blessed them after.

I have never carried the bottles. I want that printed, and printed large. The Coast sells the last words of the dead in stoppered blue glass to anyone who is grieving and has money, and when the grieving pull the stopper the hounds come to the sound. I will not have them aboard. I have no wish to meet a hound at four hundred feet with nowhere to step back to. If you want to hear your dead husband say goodbye, get somebody else to fly it to you, and stand well away when you open it.

I hold to the Articles. All of them, but the First above the rest: no captain shall leave a hand in the water while the ship can bear the weight. The Harbour-Queen amended it in her own hand three years ago, as every captain knows, with the words or until she cannot. I am told that amendment was needed. I have never had cause to use it, and I never shall.

That is my life so far, and I commend it to the young as an example of what courage and modesty can do with very little money. I have been asked to let my bosun write a page at the end, for balance. I said he could have a short one.

A note by Walter Gurney, bosun of the Fair Excuse.

The eel was a conger and about four feet long and it bit him, and that is the scar. The frigate was a customs cutter and it caught us, and he talked us out of it, which is the cleverer thing and he will not tell it. The Harbour-Queen has asked the printer to set down that she has never dined with Captain Brisk, has never laughed at him, and is owed eleven sovereigns by him, payable at the Mole office.

He says he has never been afraid. He sleeps with a lamp lit and the hatch open every night of his life, so that he can hear the ship breathing. Make of that what you like.

But the part about the Article is true, and it is the only part I would have you remember. There are nineteen of us aboard a ship built to carry nine. We have a cook who cannot hear and a rigger with one hand and a lookout who cannot see past his own nose and listens instead. We have two old women who were hands on his first coaster and are no use to anybody, and a boy who was crying on the Mole one morning and has never been asked where he came from. Every captain on this coast has told him to put us ashore. He has never put one of us ashore. He says the ship can bear the weight.

That is why she flies so low.
