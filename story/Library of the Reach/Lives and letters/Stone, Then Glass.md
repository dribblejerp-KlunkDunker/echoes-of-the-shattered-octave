# Stone, Then Glass

*Silas Hurlock, quarryman, of Oakhaven · 419 SR · Oakhaven · autobiography*

> A quarryman who cut Oakhaven's millstones and chapel steps for thirty years tells, bluntly, what it is to work the same hole for the Consortium.

My name is Silas Hurlock. I have cut stone out of the Oakhaven quarry for thirty-four years, and for the last two I have cut none at all and been paid better for it, and I should like somebody to explain that to me before I die.

I went into the quarry at twelve, under old Master Cleeve, who had a voice like a dropped anvil and the softest hands in the Reach, because he had not used them for anything but pointing since before my father was born. In those days it was a hole in the ground and a good one. A bowl of grey stone, stepped down in terraces a quarter-mile across, and at the bottom of it the water, black and still, so cold in the summers that when you dived your teeth sang in your head. Half of Oakhaven learned to swim down there. I courted Sukey on the third ledge with my boots off. She will tell you it was the fourth. It was the third.

The work was simple. You read the stone, you set your wedges along the line it wanted to break on, and you struck, and if you had read it right the block came away clean and rang when you tapped it, like a struck pot. That ringing was how you knew. A good block sang. A bad one thudded and you broke it up for rubble and said nothing about it in the alehouse. We cut millstones for every mill from here to Greenroot, and lintels for half the doors in town, and the steps of the Chapel of the Pure Note, which I will come back to.

In the year of the lease, the Consortium came with paper from the Governor and pumped the bottom dry in eleven days. I stood on the rim and watched the water go down off the ledge where I courted Sukey. Then they put the headframe in where the water had been, and since the bore the quarry has been lit from underneath, green as a bottle held up to a candle, all night and all day, so that you lose the habit of shadows.

I will tell you what the work is now, because nobody else will. We do not cut. We haul. We move timber for the shoring and iron for the gantries, we clear spoil off the terraces, we wheel barrows up and down the old steps I cut myself as a boy. We keep the glass-channels clean. We load the tanker-wagons at the bays and stand back while they go out east with a bell-headed soldier walking beside each one. Day-hands do not go down the shaft. I have never been down the shaft. I can feel it, though. Everyone can. It is in your back teeth from the moment you come through the gate, a grinding that does not stop, so that you go home at night and lie by your wife with your jaw aching like you have been chewing gravel. Sukey says I grind my teeth in my sleep now. I tell her that is not me.

We are paid in scrip. Green and black paper with a drill and a wheel on it, which is to say a promise from the Consortium, signed by the Consortium, that the Consortium owes me. I am told this is money. The works store takes it at its face. The Boar & Dulcimer takes it too, and I will not say at what rate, because I have to go on drinking there.

Some things a quarryman learns. You do not stand near the soldiers at the gate. You pass them close because you must, and as you pass you hear what everyone hears, the murmur inside the bell, like somebody talking in their sleep in the next room. You learn to say good morning to them, very civil, because the Factor walks the line of them every morning with her coloured lenses on and listens to each one, and the young engineers say she can hear back everything they have seen. So I say good morning, and I say it nicely, and I have never once complained about the scrip within ten yards of a bell.

There is a crew at the pumps none of us has ever had a drink with. Contract men, the foreman says, brought in from elsewhere, who keep to the pumps the way the pumps keep to them. They do not come up to the canteen. They do not come up at all that I have seen. I asked once. The foreman looked at me the way Master Cleeve used to look at a block that thudded, and I have not asked since.

This year the bore-head went. I was on the third terrace with a barrow of spoil, and I felt it before I heard anything, a pitch climbing in my teeth like a kettle coming to the boil, faster than any kettle, and then the whole bowl jumped under me once, the way a dog jumps when you tread on its tail in its sleep. Then the green light down the shaft went brighter for a moment, and then quiet. They brought up four from the platform at the face. Marten, who owed me two Chimes from before the Fracture and knew it. Old Coyle. The Tanner twins, who were not twenty. The Cantor sang the twins down side by side, in the Narrow, so low that I had to stand near to hear it, and I stood near. The Consortium paid their mother in scrip.

Now the chapel steps. I cut them the summer I was twenty-two, eleven of them, out of the best seam the quarry ever gave. The third step from the top has a flaw in it shaped like a running hare. Master Cleeve wanted it broken up. I said nobody would ever see it but the people who looked at their feet, and those were the people I cut it for. He let me keep it in.

On Nulldays I go and sit on that step. It is the last stone in Oakhaven that does not glow, and does not hum, and does not ache in your teeth when you put your hand on it. It is just stone, cold, where I left it. Stone only ever wanted to be left where it was. You would think that was the easiest thing in the world to give it. Nobody did.
