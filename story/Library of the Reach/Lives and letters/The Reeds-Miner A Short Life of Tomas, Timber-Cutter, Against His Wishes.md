# The Reeds-Miner: A Short Life of Tomas, Timber-Cutter, Against His Wishes

*Gwen Fitch, his sister's daughter, for the Timber Compact's harvest supper · 419 SR · Oakhaven · biography*

> A niece writes, for the timber-cutters' supper, the life of the uncle who told her not to: how a man who dug reeds for the Cantors came to fell ringing oaks for the Synod.

My uncle Tomas does not want this written. He said so, at length, across his own kitchen table, with the glove off so that I would have to look at the hand while he said it. I am writing it anyway, because the Compact asked me to, and because I am the only one in the family he cannot frighten, having been sick on him as a baby.

You will all have wondered why he is called the Reeds-Miner, when he has cut timber the whole of your acquaintance. Here is the answer. Before the Fracture, when a wind-reed went sour in the orchards, the Cantors could not simply pull it out. The crown sat a yard down, a knot of root the size of a man's head, and if you left any of it the new reed came up out of tune. So there was a trade for lifting them, with a long narrow spade and a lantern, and the men who did it were called reeds-miners, because they went down a hole in the ground and came up dirty. My grandfather did it. Tomas went down his first hole at eight, because he was the only one small enough. He could tell a sour crown by the smell before he touched it. The Cantors trusted him. He says that was the best thing anyone ever paid him in.

On the day the Spire broke, all the reeds in the Reach went to sugar and blew away in an hour, and his trade went with them. He was thirty-three and had a wife and a spade. Within the month he had a timber licence from the Synod and a grey mare called Dumpling, who is still with us, and still bites.

He was a good timber-cutter and a very loud one. In those days a wagon creaked. Everything creaked, and you could hear him coming down the Greenroot road from a mile off, singing to the mare, badly, in a voice that the Compact men tell me could split a log by itself.

Then the Consortium put the bore into the quarry, and the oaks began to ring like bronze when the wind moved them, and the wagons stopped creaking and began to hum, and the hounds learned that a ringing tree is a dinner bell. Tomas stopped singing. He learned to fell a bough with wool wound on the blade, and to wait for a gust to cover a stroke, and to call the work off when a hound lifts its head, with two notes, falling, which my aunt says is the only tune he has ever got right.

Last year the saw took three fingers off his hand. The fourth grew back, as things grow back here now, and it grew back wrong, and that is all I will say about the glove, because he will read this. He went back to the clearing nine days later. He says he has one good hand and a bad temper and that people may choose which they speak to. Most choose the hand.

He works for the Synod's quota, as some of you do. If he is a load short on quota day they take the wagon and Dumpling, and then he has nothing. So he fells what he is told to, a stone's throw from where the hounds sleep, and carts it up to the works, where it goes to shore the headframe over the shaft. He told me this himself. He said, I know what it holds up. He said he has been thinking it since the summer. I did not ask him what he thinks, because I could see.

At the end of the evening I asked him what he would like written, since it was going to be written whether he liked it or not.

He was quiet for a long time. Then he put the glove back on and said, write that the wagons used to creak.
