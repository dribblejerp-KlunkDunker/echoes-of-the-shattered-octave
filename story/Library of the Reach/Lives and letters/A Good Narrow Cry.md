# A Good Narrow Cry

*Damaris Teasel, midwife of Stillwater Landing · 418 SR · Stillwater Landing · memoir*

> An old river-port midwife sets down forty years at the bed-foot, from the loud births of her girlhood to the whispered ones of now.

I am asked by the girl who keeps the books at the Bonded Store, who can read and write and is not shy about either, to put down what I know before I am too old to know it. I said I had nothing in me worth the ink. She said then I could waste the ink, she would bring more. So this is her ink, and these are my hands, and you must forgive the both of them.

I have been the midwife at Stillwater Landing for forty-one years. I learned from my aunt, who learned from hers, and between us three women we have caught every child born on this basin since before the lock had iron gates. I have delivered the sons of every man who ever lied to the lockkeeper, and a fair few of the lockkeepers' own, and I will say no more about that, because they all still drink here.

When I was a girl a birth was a loud thing. I want that written down first, because the young ones do not believe it. A woman shouted if she liked, and she generally liked. The men stood in the yard and sang to keep from fretting, and if it went long they sang worse, and the whole wharf knew how a labour was going by the state of the singing. There was a reed hung over the birthing door, cut for the household by the Cantors upriver, and it sounded in every wind. And we waited for the first cry the way you wait for the tide to turn, with our whole bodies, and when it came we laughed out loud. I have heard a boatman cry at a first cry and not be ashamed of it. That was the world.

Then the Screaming Year came, and the hounds came with it, and by the next spring every birthing room on the Flume was packed with fleece and hung with felt like a coffin lined for winter. I cut my first bite-strap out of a harness my husband was still using. He noticed. I learned to put my mouth to a woman's ear and say, in the Narrow, love, in the Narrow, and to keep saying it, for hours if need be, until she could hear nothing else. I learned to measure a cry with my palm, a hand's width of voice and no more. I learned to be afraid of the very sound I had spent twenty years praying for.

You can learn anything. That is the dreadful thing about people.

I will tell one story from those years, because it is the one they ask for at the Store. Keziah off the rafts went into labour on a timber raft in the middle of the basin, with the drovers ashore and the lock shut and no boat to fetch her in. I rowed out alone with my bag on my knees. She had the strap in her teeth already and bit it so hard she broke a tooth on it, and she never once rose above a murmur, and I was prouder of her than of any woman I have ever tended. And then the boy came out roaring. A great red roar, the kind I used to laugh at, going out across the open water where there was no Hedge and no wool and nothing at all to stop it. Every soul on the wharf stood still. I could hear the reeds on the far bank go quiet, the way they do. And Keziah, without a word, took him off me and put him to the breast, and he stopped as if she had shut a door. There is no Narrow in the world as good as a full mouth. I have taught that to every girl since. It is the most useful thing I know.

Then they put the drill into the quarry at Oakhaven, and something came down the Flume that you could not see, and I learned to be afraid of the other thing.

I will not write much about the still ones. The Grafters call it infant pallor, and write it in their book, and go on to the next house. I am an old woman and I know what the old women call it, and I will not write that either, because some words you do not leave lying about where a child could pick them up. I will say only this. The first one I lost, I sat with the mother until the lamps were lit, and neither of us said anything, which was the worst thing we could have done and we both knew it. In the morning there was less of him. I have his name in my own book. I have eleven names in it now. I read them over on Nullday, under my breath, so that the room does not go quiet while I do.

It was a midwife upriver who put me right. She has no more voice than I have, a voice like a gate on a windy night, and she told me she had watched the Cantors hum through a clay jar laid to the belly, so that the note goes into the child instead of into the air, and that she did it herself when there was no Cantor to send for. I said I could not sing. She said the jar does not know that. She said a bad note held is better than a good note kept in your mouth, and she said it in a way that made me think she had lost a few before anyone told her, too.

So now I hum. I hum the Root note, low and flat and wandering, and I am sure it is the worst Root note ever sounded on this river. I hum it through the jar while the mother labours, and when the child comes out silent, which they do more often now, I bend over the soft of the head and hum it straight in. And mostly, not always, there is a breath, and a second, and then a small offended sound like a moth bumping a windowpane, and I say what my aunt said and her aunt before her, though the meaning has turned right round: there, a good cry. Only now I say a good Narrow cry. She'll live.

One thousand, eight hundred and six children, by my count, and by the girl's, who checked it. Most of Stillwater, then. If you are reading this at the Store, the odds are you were one of mine, and I have very likely smacked you since.

So here is my instruction, for what it cost me. Keep to the Narrow. Keep a lamp whistling. Keep a jar by the bed. And when your time comes, send for me, and if I am not there to come, hum anyway. Badly will do. Badly has always done.
