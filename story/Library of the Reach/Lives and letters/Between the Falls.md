# Between the Falls

*Marthe Unhaus, of Tier Eleven, for the Bellows · 418 SR · Broadsheet Row, Port Cantabile, from a copy carried out of Cinderhold · memoir*

> A woman from the lowest, hottest tier of Cinderhold tells how four hundred workers with no House learned to organise in the gaps between the hammer-blows.

Set on the Row from a copy carried out of the Caldera in the lining of a boot. The Bellows do not sign their papers. We print the name she gave us, which is not a name, and you will see why.

Still standing.

That is how we say good morning on Tier Eleven, and it is not a joke, though you may laugh. I was born at the bottom of Cinderhold, where it is hot enough that a heel of bread goes to rusk in your pocket between the gate and the furnace. Tier One has clean air and a House. Every tier below it has a House, all the way down, until you come to us, and we have none. So we are written down in the indenture books as Unhaus, of no house, and some of us have taken it for a surname out of spite. I am one. You are born owing on Tier Eleven, for your mother's bed and the water she drank while she carried you, and you work it off, and you die owing more.

Above everything is the Beat. If you have never heard the Great Beat I cannot give it to you, any more than I could give you the heat. It is a hammer the size of a chapel, and it falls in fours, and it has fallen in fours for two hundred years except on two days that we count our history from. You do not hear it after a while. You live in it. A child on Tier Eleven learns to talk in the gaps, between one fall and the next, three counts of speech and then shut your mouth, because on the fall nobody hears anything, not even the person shouting. Outsiders talk across the fall and you see their lips going and you feel sorry for them. I have been told that when we go abroad we stop in the middle of sentences for the rest of our lives. I have never been abroad. I expect it is true.

My father kept his hand on it. That is the best thing you can say of a man in Cinderhold. When an oath is sworn among us it is sworn bare-handed on an ingot just out of the mould, and the one swearing holds his palm there while the oath is said, and a man who takes it off before the end is cold, and there is no worse word. My father took the Bellows' oath with his hand on the ingot for the whole of it. He had the mark till he died. He died cold anyway, not that way, the other way. The Cold Burn went through our furnace the winter I was twelve and the heat went out of him with it. He walked about for a year with his eyes open and no warmth in his hands, one of the ember-blind, and I used to hold them to warm them, and it was like holding two spoons.

We are called the Bellows because a bellows is the only part of a forge that breathes, and nobody has ever thanked one.

There are four hundred of us. We have no leverage, as the Houses are fond of telling us, and no money, and no hall. What we have is the Beat. A meeting of the Bellows is held in the open, in the noise, because no overseer can hear a thing in it. Questions are put between the falls. We vote by tapping the pipe-rail, once for aye and nothing for nay, in the gap after the fall, and a man down the line counts, and four hundred people can decide a thing in the time it takes the hammer to fall eighty times. I have stood in a vote like that with my brother Hanno on one side and a girl I had never met on the other, and felt the rail ring under my palm with four hundred taps, and I will tell you it is the nearest I have come to hearing a choir.

In the spring of four hundred and fifteen we sat down. Three days, every hand on the tier, sitting on the walkways with our arms folded. The Houses brought in slag-folk from the Scraps, who have even less than we do and could not be blamed, and paid them in food, and the furnaces never once went out. On the fourth day we stood up and went back owing more. Hanno says that is how we learned the first law of Cinderhold, which is that nothing done here is louder than the Beat. He says it as if it were the end of something. I think it was the beginning.

I will put down one more thing, because the Row asked for it. Men who come up from the deep galleries say that the glass down there talks. I do not mean it rings. I mean talks. They do not say this above Tier Nine. Below it they say it all the time, very quietly, between the falls, and they say it the way you would mention that a neighbour has been crying in the night, and that someone should go round.

Upstairs the Houses have started calling something the Gasket Strike. They have given it a name before we have, which is how we know they are frightened, because you only name the thing you are expecting.

We have learned to say everything that matters in the space between two blows of a hammer. It has always been room enough. One day the hammer will have to wait for us.

Still standing.
