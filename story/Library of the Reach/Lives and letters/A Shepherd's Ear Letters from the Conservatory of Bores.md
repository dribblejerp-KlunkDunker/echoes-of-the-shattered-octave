# A Shepherd's Ear: Letters from the Conservatory of Bores

*Owen Hatherly, scholar of the Conservatory, to his mother at Wethercombe · 414–416 SR · the Organum · letters*

> A shepherd's son from the Downs wins a place at the Synod's great school for drill-engineers and writes home about forks, ewes and the city that plays the hour.

Nullday the fourth of Sheafmonth, 414. Dear Mam, I am here and I have not been robbed, though a man in the coach-yard tried to sell me a map of the city for two pence and the city is mostly straight lines, so I suppose I was nearly robbed. You will want to know what it is like. It is like standing inside an organ, if the organ were a mile wide and somebody had laid the streets between the pipes. The towers are grey and very tall and have mouths cut near the top, and the wind blows across them all day so that the whole city breathes one long low note you feel in your teeth. Then on the hour the bellows under the Choir-Hall push, and every pipe sounds at once, and the hour comes down on you like weather. The first time, I sat down in the road. Nobody laughed. A woman with a basket said I would stop hearing it in a week, and walked round me.

I lodge over a cordwainer's in a room with a window and a bed and a smell of leather. There are forty of us in the first year. I am the only one from the Reach. There is a book on every desk with a name on the spine from our end of the world, and you know whose, and I shall not write it, and you will not say it at the Bellwether, and that will be best for both of us.

Stasisday the twentieth of Ashfall, 414. Our first lesson was one line, and we spent a week on it. A struck fork held near a second fork a little out of true will beat. Loud, soft, loud, soft. Mam, I knew that before I came. It is the two bells on Father's old wethers on the far side of Hob's Knap, one a hair flatter than the other, so that you could hear them pulsing together across the whole down on a still evening, and tell from the beat which way the flock was moving. I said so in class. Everyone laughed except Master Hollister, who said that I had a shepherd's ear, and that a shepherd's ear was worth more in this building than the Synod's money, and then gave me twice the work.

Memoryday the first of Frostmonth, 414. The woman with the basket was right. On the eighth day I stopped hearing the hour. I know it still sounds, because I see the others look up at it, and I can feel it in the floor of my room if I put my hand there. But my ears have let it go. Brann, who sits next to me and is from the Peaks and says almost nothing, says that is how you know you have become a citizen. How many lambs did we lose in the cold snap? Tell me the true number, not the one you think I can bear.

Rootday the ninth of Seedmonth, 415. I have been spoken to. Brann and I were checking a pair of forks in the practice room, and to save time I hummed the one and he hummed a fifth above it, the way the old Cantors used to, so we could hear if the forks sat true between our notes. Master Hollister came in and stood there until we stopped. He was not angry. He said that we must not do that, that it was a folk habit, and that in this building it was very nearly devotional, in the wrong direction. Then he checked the forks himself, with a little brass meter, and they were true, and he said nothing more about it. Brann has not hummed since. I find I hum all the time now, under my breath, in my room, so the room is not so quiet. It is the Root note. I do not think I could stop if I tried.

Massday the third of Dustmonth, 415. Second year. Today we began the anatomy of the voice. There was a sheep's larynx on a tray at the front of the room, and the lecturer turned it with a probe to show us the folds and where they lie, and how they are stretched to change the note. Mam, it was a Downs ewe. I would know that gristle anywhere, I have helped Father with enough of them. I did not say so this time. I have learned that much. Nobody has told us why men who drill rock must know exactly how a voice is made, and where a wire would sit along it. When Brann asked, the lecturer said we would understand in our fourth year. Brann wrote it in his book. I wrote it in mine.

Memoryday the twenty-seventh of Sheafmonth, 415. We drilled our first sample in the works yard, a block of lively stone from somewhere east, set in an iron cradle with a little bore-head no bigger than a churn. We were to hold the bit beneath the failure-pitch, as the book on every desk says we must, always beneath. I had the meter. And as the bit went in, the stone's pitch climbed away from it, a little, the way a ewe shifts away from the shears when she feels the first cold of the blade. Master Hollister said samples are lively and that we would learn to correct for it. Tarquin, who is from the Organum and very clever, asked whether stone could mind. Everyone laughed. He was not in the hall at the start of term. They said his family had sent for him.

Nullday the eighteenth of Lambmonth, 416. Dear Mam, I am coming home.

I have not failed. Master Hollister says I am the best ear in the year and he wrote it down and I will bring you the paper so you can show it at the Bellwether, which I know you will. I have not been sent away. I have done something worse, which is to leave a scholarship, and Father would have had a great deal to say about it, and I will listen to all of it from you instead.

I will tell you the reason, because you will ask, and because I have only just found it out myself. This morning I walked across Registry Square at the hour, and the whole city sounded, every pipe, and I did not hear it. Not at all. A year and a half I have not heard it. And I thought: they told me I would stop hearing it, and they were pleased when I did, and so was I. And then I thought about the stone in the yard, shifting away from the bit, and the ewe on the tray, and Tarquin, and I understood that I am being taught, very carefully and very well, to stop hearing things.

So I am coming home by the Reach road and I will be at the stile before Reed-Hanging. Please do not take my reed down. I should like to hear it.
