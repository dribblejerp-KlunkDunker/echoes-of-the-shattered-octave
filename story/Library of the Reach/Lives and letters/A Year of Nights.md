# A Year of Nights

*Godwin Pettigrew, lamplighter of Oakhaven · 418–419 SR · Oakhaven · journal*

> An Oakhaven lamplighter keeps a year's notes on his round of whistling lamps, the people who sit up with them, and one night-hummer in particular.

Sheaf on the gate, 418. My mother kept a book of the weather and my father kept a book of debts, and I have neither weather nor money worth the writing, so I shall keep a book of nights. I light two hundred and twelve hush-lamps, from the tannery steps to the top of Cooper's Row, with a pole, a taper and a bad knee. People think a lamplighter's trade is fire. It is not. Any fool can light a wick. A lamplighter's trade is listening. When the flame takes, the reed in the chimney catches the draught and begins to whistle, and every reed has its own note, and I know all two hundred and twelve the way a shepherd knows his ewes. If one is wrong I hear it from the end of the lane.

Last lamp of the round is the one at the corner by the well, which I call Well Corner because nobody else has bothered to call it anything. Tibby Lockwood sits up there. She is a night-hummer, and younger than most of them, being only my age, and she hums lower than anyone in Oakhaven. You have to stand quite close to be sure she is humming at all. I stand quite close. It is my job.

First frost. Oil is four pence the pint and the Consortium says it will be five by the thaw. I said this to Tibby, who said nothing, because she was humming, which is the trouble with courting a night-hummer. I did not say I was courting. I am writing it here so it is written somewhere.

Frost again, harder. The lamp at the end house by the dye-works went wrong in the night. The cold split the reed, and it stopped whistling, and I was at the far end of the Hollow and did not hear it go. In the morning the door was on the latch. Her kettle was cold on the hob. Her shawl was on the back of her chair, folded the way she folded it, and there was nobody in either. Her name was Abigail Starling. She was seventy, and deaf in one ear, and she liked her lamp turned low so it would not keep her up. I have written her name here so that it is written somewhere too. I carry six spare reeds in my coat now, wrapped in flannel against the frost, and I walk the round twice.

Rime on the Hedge. Nails. I cut mine every morning by the basin with the little knife, the same as everyone, and this morning I cut them twice, because I lit the round late and lay in. Tibby says a man can tell how long he has slept by his fingers now, like a tree. That is the most she has said to me in a month. I am counting it.

Catkins on the hazel. Walking home past the thorn at the dead hour I heard my mother call my name from the other side of it. Godwin, your supper is going cold. She has been dead four winters. She used to say it at the door every night of my boyhood, in exactly that voice, a little cross, a little fond. I made the sign, two fingers to the lips and to the ear, and kept walking at the same pace, which is the hardest thing I have ever done with my legs. When I got to Well Corner I must have looked a sight, because Tibby stopped humming and took my hand and held it, and then began again, and did not let go until the hummers changed.

Seed in. At the change of the hummers the old ones murmur all's well, all's quiet, keep to the Narrow, as they hand over the stools, and I have taken to saying it with them under my breath, though I am not one. Old Tam at the Weavers' Cross hums The Miller's Daughter every night of his life and every night he goes for the high note at the end of the verse and misses it. I said to Tibby that one of these nights he would get it. She said she hoped not. She said if Tam ever got that note it would be because something was out there listening hard enough to make him try. I have not been able to get that out of my head.

Reeds hung. The whole street was noisy all day with the tuning of the new First Reeds over the doors, and the Chapel sent a deacon round to say it was a very great deal of second notes for one street, and everyone said yes, deacon, and went on tuning. I looked at the reed over Tibby's door when I passed. Her mother still cuts the new one every year and tunes it against Tibby's breastbone at the kitchen table, as if she might have changed. She never has. It is a good low reed. I would know it anywhere now.

Hay in. I have done something I shall probably have to confess. I cut a new reed for the hush-lamp at Well Corner. I tuned it at home at the kitchen table for nine nights, against the lamp across the lane, which is my own lamp, the one on my own house. I tuned it a third above. The Church teaches that holiness is one note held without end, and that the second note is sin, and I was raised to say it and I said it. But I have listened to two hundred and twelve reeds a night for six years, and I tell you every one of them is lonely.

Eels down the Flume. The Eel-Run, late this year, because everything is late. The lamplighters set out the lanterns all down the bank for the fishing, and then the whole town stood along the water in the dark with traps and lines and nobody spoke at all, because the eels can hear. It is the only festival in the Reach you keep with your mouth shut. Tibby fished beside me for three hours without a word, which I found I liked very much. At the end she put an eel in my bucket, a fat one, and went home. In the Reach that is very nearly a proposal. I am almost sure.

Corn standing. They say at the well that the Church has views about the Last Sheaf this year. Let them have views. The north field will still be standing when they are gone.

Tonight I put the new reed in the lamp at Well Corner. I lit it last, as always, and the reed caught and whistled, and across the lane my own lamp answered it a third below, and the corner by the well made a chord. Very small. No louder than two people breathing in the same room. Tibby came up the lane with her stool and her shawl and stopped dead and listened to it for a long time, with her face doing nothing at all. Then she sat down, and settled her shawl, and began to hum.

She hummed the low note. Under both of them.
