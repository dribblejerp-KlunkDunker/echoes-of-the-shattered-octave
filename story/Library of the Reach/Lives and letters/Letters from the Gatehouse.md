# Letters from the Gatehouse

*Cyril Tolley, junior clerk to the Consortium of Bores, to his sister Prudence · 418–419 SR · the Oakhaven works gatehouse · letters*

> A young clerk from the Organum, posted to keep the ledger at the Oakhaven works gate, writes home to his sister about the Reach, the scrip, and the soldier who talks in its sleep.

Fair copies, kept, as a clerk should keep them.

Rootday the ninth of Seedmonth, 418. Dear Prue, I am here and alive and I have already disgraced myself. At the coaching inn at the edge of the Reach I asked the landlady, in an ordinary voice, whether there was any soup, and she put her hand flat over my mouth like a cork in a bottle and held it there until I stopped. Then she let go and said, very low, that there was. Nobody here speaks above a murmur. They measure their voices with the palm of the hand. Every room has a little lamp with a reed in it that whistles all night, so that the house is never quite silent, and the first night I lay awake listening to mine and thinking it was a kettle someone had forgotten. People greet you with "Does it ring?" and you are to say "True enough." I said "Yes, thank you," and was looked at.

My post is the works gate. I keep the ledger of every cart and wagon in and out, and pull the lever for anyone who holds up the green glass disc, and there are two soldiers of the Legion standing either side of me with their bells tilted to the sky. I miss the hour. Nobody plays it here. I did not know how much I leaned on it until I stood at a gate at what I am fairly sure was the sixth hour of the morning and had no idea whether it was.

Nullday the second of Lambmonth, 418. The soldier on the left of the gate talks in its sleep. They all murmur, you can hear it if you stand close, which you are told not to, but the one on the left says words. The same words, every morning, just before the day shift: second shelf, behind the flour. Second shelf, behind the flour. I am told this is nothing. There is a Catalogue circular pinned inside the gatehouse door which says that speech emitted by a damaged engram is to be disregarded and is not to be recorded. So I have not recorded it. I am telling you, which is different.

Dawnday the eleventh of Longsun, 418. You asked what the town is like. Prue, the fingernails grow. A quarter of an inch in a night. There is a knife beside every washbasin in Oakhaven and I have one by mine and I use it every morning before I shave. Little oaks come up through the cobbles overnight like teeth through a gum. When the wind gets up the big trees ring, actually ring, like spoons on a bowl, and everyone in the street stops and looks toward the Hedge. They took all the bells in the first year of the works, for the bore, and nobody here misses them, which is the strangest thing I have heard yet. At dusk the lamplighters go round, and old people take stools out to the crossings and hum till dawn. There is an old man at the Weavers' Cross who hums the same song every night and misses the same high note every time, and I have started walking home that way to hear him miss it. There is said to be a Synod man living alone outside the Hedge whom the hounds will not touch. Nobody will tell me his name, because I am from the Organum. I find I do not mind as much as I should.

Massday the third of Dustmonth, 418. Father writes that engrams are harmonic templates and possess no identity, and that I should not let rustic talk work on me. He is quite right. A template can hold a pattern of words the way a mould holds the shape of a loaf. The mould does not know it is a loaf. I have written that out twice to be sure I believe it. All the same I have begun to say good morning to the soldier on the left, because it seems rude not to, when one has heard about the flour every day for four months.

Memoryday the twenty-fifth of Firstfruit, 418. They have a rule here for children: never answer a voice you cannot see. Things outside the Hedge call in the voices of people they have heard. Last night at the Listening Hour I was walking the lane past the thorn and something on the other side said my name, quite kindly, in my landlady's voice. My landlady was in her kitchen. I could see her through the window, cutting bread. I did not answer. I walked on at the same pace, as they all do, and I was sick in the yard when I got in, and she held my head and did not ask. This morning I asked a woman at the well whether a voice inside a bell counts as a voice you cannot see. She looked at me for a long moment and then walked away without her water.

Nullday the twentieth of Stillmonth, 419. A lean winter, and the scrip will not change to sovereigns at anything like its face, so I am sending you less than I promised. I did a foolish thing. The Factor walks the line of the Legion every morning with her lenses, and listens to each soldier as if she were reading it, and this morning I asked her whether the one on the left of the gate might be posted somewhere else. I said it unsettled the carters. She looked at me through the coloured glass for a long time and said my colour was a little anxious, and that she would note it. Then she listened to the soldier on the left for longer than to any of the others. Prue, it occurs to me that if she can hear back everything it has heard, then she has heard me say good morning to it every day since Dustmonth. I do not know why that should frighten me. It is only good manners.

Rootday the seventh of Thawmonth, 419. I have stopped saying good morning. I am sorry to say I find I have begun to answer it instead, under my breath, the way you would answer anybody who told you the same thing every day. Behind the flour, yes. I know. I will look. I do not know what I would look for. I do not know whose flour. The circular says not to record it, and I have not, and I have been keeping the gate ledger very neatly.

Nullday the twelfth of Sheafmonth, 419. I have applied for a transfer home. Please do not tell Father until it is granted, and if it is not granted, please do not tell him at all. I am quite well. The harvest is coming in, and they have a festival here when the last field is cut, and I am told the clerk is not invited, which is fair. I only wanted to write down one more thing, and then I will not write about the gate again.

This morning, just before the day shift, the soldier on the left did not say anything about the flour. It said, quite clearly, in a voice like a little girl's, mind the step, Gran. There is no step at the gate. I minded it anyway.

Your loving brother, C.
