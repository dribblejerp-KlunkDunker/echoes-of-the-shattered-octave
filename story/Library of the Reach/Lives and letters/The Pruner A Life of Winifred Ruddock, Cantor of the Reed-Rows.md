# The Pruner: A Life of Winifred Ruddock, Cantor of the Reed-Rows

*Jennet Lindley, of the Oakhaven dye-works, once her pupil · 418 SR · Oakhaven · biography*

> An old dye-works woman remembers the Cantor who taught her for one year, tuned a valley with a pair of shears, and once sang a Church examiner into silence.

They will tell you a Cantor is a voice. Win Ruddock would have told you a Cantor is a pair of shears.

I was her pupil for one year, the year I was twelve, which was so long ago that the people who remember it with me could sit round one small table, and soon will not need to. I am writing this down because the young ones at the vats keep asking what the orchards were, and I find that I cannot tell them without telling them about her.

She was not a big woman. She was brown and narrow and bent a little forward, as if she were always walking into a wind, and she had the shears on a cord round her neck the way other women wear a locket. Every Cantor kept a row of the wind-reeds in those days, and Win kept the longest, the one that ran from the old stone wall clear down to the water, nine hundred reeds by her own count. A reed is tuned by cutting. Shorter sounds higher. Cut a hair too much and it is spoilt for a year. She would stand in her row in the evening with the wind coming down off the hills and listen with her head on one side, and then reach out, quick as a heron, and take the very tip off one reed out of nine hundred, and the whole row would come right. I never once saw her cut twice.

When the wind came properly, the rows sang. I do not know how to make you hear it. Slow chords, big as weather, rolling down the valley one into the next, so that a man ploughing at Greenroot would stop and lift his head. It was the most ordinary thing in the world, like rain. We did not even think it beautiful. You do not think your mother's voice is beautiful. It is just the sound the world makes when it is all right.

I came to her with no voice to speak of. My mother had hopes. Win heard me sing one verse of the harvest song and said, kindly, that I had a very good ear and a voice like a wet sack, and that ears were the rarer thing, and she would have me for a year anyway, because somebody had to hold the ladder. So I held the ladder. I carried the lantern and the twine and the pot of pitch for sealing cuts. And I watched her work, which no one else alive now did, and so it falls to me.

She sang to everything. To apple trees on frost nights, with her lips to the bark, a low note going down into the wood to keep the sap moving. To a farrier's boy in a fever, sitting on the end of his cot for two nights and never once raising her voice. To the cows at the Pellow place, which she said was nonsense but which made the farmer happy, and a happy farmer pays in cheese. She tuned the First Reeds for every child born in our end of the valley. She would lay a raw cane against the baby's breastbone and hum until the cane hummed back, and then cut the slot to what she heard, and that reed went over the door and sounded in every wind, and every Reed-Hanging after, the household cut the new one to the old one's note. My mother kept the first reed Win cut for me in a drawer for sixty years. I have it still. It still sounds.

She had sayings, and she said them so often that I can still hear the exact tune of them. One note is a shout, two notes is a conversation. Never sing to a tree you would not prune. A Cantor who wants to be listened to should go on the stage.

Now the thing everybody asks about, which is the man from the Church.

In the year I was with her, the Choir-Church sent an examiner up from the Organum to look into the Cantors of the Reach. Somebody had written to somebody that we taught the children intervals, which we did, and that intervals were the second note, which they are, and that the second note is sin, which every child in the Reach could recite and none of us believed. He came in white with a clerk behind him, a tall soft-spoken man who was polite to everyone and frightened Win's dog, and he sat in her kitchen and asked her to sing him one note, so that he could judge whether it was pure.

She said she would be glad to, but not indoors. She said a note wants room. She took him out into her row at dusk, and I came behind with the lantern, and she stood him in the middle of nine hundred reeds and opened her mouth and sang one note, the Root note, very plain, and held it.

And the wind came down off the hills.

I have thought about it for sixty years and I do not believe she planned it, and I do not believe she did not. The whole row took up her note and spread it into a chord, and the next row took the chord and turned it, and the next, all the way down the valley in the dark, until we stood in the middle of a sound as wide as the sky, with her one plain note still at the bottom of it like a stone at the bottom of a river. The examiner's clerk sat down on the ground. The examiner did not move at all. When it was over he stood there a long time. Then he thanked her, very civilly, and went back to the Organum, and I have been told by people who should know that his report on the Cantors of the Reach was two words long. Agricultural custom. We have lived inside those two words ever since. I like to think she wrote them.

She grew old the way the reeds did, from the top down. Toward the end she could not climb, and pruned from a stool that I carried behind her down the row, a few yards a day. She died in her chair by the window in the late summer of three hundred and ninety-one with the shears in her lap. We buried her at the bottom of her row, by the water, with her mouth free, as we did then. And the next spring her row came up out of tune for the first time in forty years, and nobody would touch it, and it stayed out of tune until the Cantors who came after her could bear to.

I was at the vats the morning the reeds went. I did not see it. By the time I got to the wall it was over, and the valley was full of something glittering, like frost blown off a roof, and the next gust took it away. People say there was a sound. I listened very hard, standing where I used to hold her ladder, and there was none.

It was the first thing in the Reach I ever heard go without a sound. I am glad, at least, that it was not her.
