## 0. The State of the World, Ashfall–Frostmonth 419

- **The season.** Late Ashfall into early Frostmonth, 419 SR. Book Three runs from the twenty-third of Ashfall to the thirteenth of Frostmonth, three weeks, ending seventeen days before the Longest Night.
- **The arithmetic.** 396 cores in the *Drift-Kestrel*'s hold after the Tramway. Roughly 400 Rendered on the Shelf. Sable with one piece of herself left. This is the book's engine; it is not solved, it is *reframed* (see §4.3).
- **The three bodies of the Synod** are now all on the page: the Arbiterium (writs, Justiciars, columns), the Office of the Catalogue (pressing, cores, the Hall), and the Church (offstage this book, present as stencilled crates). They do not like each other and they share the same machinery.

### The eight-day week — RULING, derived from the manuscript
Days are named for the Anchors and run in a fixed cycle of eight:

**Rootday · Hearthday · Massday · Stasisday · Dawnday · Driftday · Memoryday · Nullday**

Confirmed by Book Three's own chapter headers (2 Frostmonth Nullday, 3 Rootday, 4 Hearthday, 5 Massday, 6 Stasisday, 13 Massday) and consistent with Vol V (19 Frostmonth a Rootday). **Ashfall has 30 days**, derived from 28 Ashfall Stasisday running to 2 Frostmonth Nullday.

> **RULING — the Longest Night is 30 Frostmonth, a Stasisday.** Vol V's "(a Rootday)" is struck; Vol V has been corrected. The eight-day cycle above is canon and 30 Frostmonth falls on Stasisday, which is also the better reading: **Stasis is the law of stopping, and the Night is the night everything is asked to stop.** The Chrono-Dial's own day is the day Vaelen has chosen to let all eight put the weight down — and he did not choose it, as Sable works out in Book Four Ch3: *the Longest Night is not the night he has chosen; it is the night they have.* The gods picked the day named for stasis. Nobody needs to say so out loud in the text.

## 0.1 THE ROTA
### *Why the week has eight days, and what the Watch is really chanting*

**[PROPOSED] — built on canon implication, not yet on the page.** Vol V §4 establishes, from Ilse Brennan's letter, that the orchard Cantors were founded to sing to the Root; that every Longest Night every Cantor in the Reach went down the old stair to sing the god its lullaby; and that *"every Anchor may once have had keepers who came down on the Longest Night."* This section takes that last line seriously and follows it into the calendar. Nothing here contradicts the manuscript. None of it has been stated in the manuscript either. Mark it and use it carefully.

### The proposal
**The eight-day week is not named after the Anchors. It is the keepers' duty roster, fossilized.**

Eight Anchors, eight orders, eight days. Each day is the day its god is attended: the Cantors hum into the hollow of the oldest orchard tree on **Rootday**, the Sounders tap the deep floor on **Massday**, a Reef-Reader listens to the channel on **Driftday**, the Veil reads a page of the dead aloud on **Memoryday**. Ordinary weekly attendance. A god that holds one law for ten thousand years gets tired and forgets the tune, and somebody comes by every eight days to remind it that it is heard.

**The week outlived the duty by three hundred years as pure vocabulary**, because a week is the hardest thing in a culture to kill — it is just how you say *tomorrow*. The Synod abolished the orders, sealed the vaults, absorbed the Veil into the Office, and never touched the calendar, because there was nothing in it to abolish. **Every writ the Arbiterium has ever issued is dated in the keepers' own hand.**

### Why the Long Tending is on Stasisday
This is the piece that makes the ruling earn itself. The Reach's Cantors went down on **the Longest Night**, which is a **Stasisday** — not on Rootday, which is "their" day. The anomaly is the point.

**The weekly rota is ordinary attendance, one order to one god. The Longest Night is the night all eight come at once.** Once a year, every order in the world went down its own stair on the same night and sang the whole chord together, and the eight gods heard, for one night, the thing they were actually made out of. That is what the Octave *was*: not eight stones, but **a relief shift that assembled annually**, and the rest of the year was eight people taking turns keeping eight gods company.

And the rendezvous is on Stasisday because **stasis is the only law that can hold the other seven still long enough to be handed over.** You cannot pass a weight from one hand to another while it is moving.

### What this does to Vaelen
**It makes him right about the rite and alone in it.**

Casimir Vaelen has the correct night, the correct place and the correct instinct. The Organum stands on the **Keynote**, the one spot where all eight roots meet — which is to say, *the only place on the continent where the Long Tending could be performed for all eight at once*, which is to say **the Organum was built on the old rendezvous.** He will go down under his own city on the Longest Night, on Stasisday, to attend eight exhausted gods, and that is exactly what a keeper is supposed to do.

He is simply **the only one who came**, and nobody alive taught him the second half of the song. The lullaby tells the god to sleep, hold, and not be afraid of being bound. Vaelen knows the first clause and has never been told the other two. **He is performing a ten-thousand-year-old act of care, correctly, with half the words, by himself, and it will end the world.**

> This is the strongest possible version of him and it requires no new scene. Vol V already says *"someone will come down the stair to all of them at once, and it will be Vaelen. He knows."* The rota is what makes that line land like a verdict instead of a coincidence.

### What this does to Null-Watch
**The Watch is chanting the roster.**

*Rootday, Hearthday, Massday* — the thing a hundred condemned men say over and over on a wall at the edge of the world to keep from being taken — is **the names of the eight gods, in order, in the old attendance sequence.** It is a litany that has forgotten it is a litany. Men who have been thrown away by the world recite the duty of the people who were supposed to keep the world from needing a wall, and not one of them knows it, and it keeps them alive.

**Do not have anyone point this out.** Let a reader find it. It is already on the page, in Book Three, dozens of times.

### The orders, surviving and degraded
Every realm's old craft is a keeper-order that lost its reason and kept its gesture. All of these already exist in canon; the rota only explains them.

| Anchor | Day | Order | What it became |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth, 174 | Rootday | **the Cantors of the Reach** | an orchard trade — tending notes, felling-songs, midwifery. **The only order that kept the song.** |
| Heat, 285 | Hearthday | *(the Deep Guilds of Cinderhold?)* | guild ritual at the forge-lighting. Unattested; open. |
| Mass, 396 | Massday | **the Sounders of Fortis** | a deep-patrol corps that taps the floor with tuned rods and listens for a ring. They are still attending Mass. They think it is a safety drill. |
| Stasis, 417 | Stasisday | *(the Loom-Wardens of the Sandglass?)* | neutrality and clockwork. Unattested; open. |
| Light, 528 | Dawnday | **the Sky Choirs** | sang the dawn service. The Church kept the hour, dropped the chord, and demanded one note. **The Rite of Pure Unison is the attendance performed wrong, on purpose.** |
| Velocity, 639 | Driftday | **the Reef-Readers** | steering by ear. Auld Bessa, 84, is the last, and she is the one who hears the sea asking. |
| Memory, 741 | Memoryday | **the Silent Veil** | archivists sworn to record and never intervene. **The Chorister recites 74,000 names because nobody comes to recite them with it.** |
| Decay, 852 | Nullday | **the Watch** *(by accident)* | the Refusal: a dawn firing into the edge of the world. Attendance turned into a weapon pointed at the god. |

Two of these are devastating on their own. The Church inherited the Light's attendance hour and ruined the song. **The Watch inherited the Decay's attendance and now shouts at it.**

### What Sable would do with this
**Count.** Eight days between visits, three hundred years since the vaults were sealed: every Anchor has been waiting a specific, countable number of days, and the number is enormous, and she is the one person in Aethelgard who would work it out and write it in a ruled column. The Ninth Stratum counted ten thousand and six years in tally-marks on a chamber wall. **Somebody has been counting on both sides.**

### The hole in the week — reserve for Book Five
Eight Anchors. Eight days. **No day for the Ninth.**

The Ninth Anchor doctrine says the Architects left the chord open to be closed by a living thing. The week has no room in it for that living thing — which is either proof the Architects never intended one, or proof that **the ninth's day is the one you make.** When Kaelen takes the shift, there is no day named for him and there never was, and somebody will have to decide what to call it.

> Pair this with the **Fourteenth Stratum's blank page** (Vol V §1). Two counting systems, each with exactly one blank at the end, left by the people who built the world. Do not resolve them in the same scene.

### Hooks
1. A clerk in the Organum notices that the writ he is dating is dated in a dead order's hand, and says nothing.
2. Somebody on the Watch asks why the days are in that order. Nobody knows. The chant does not stop.
3. Orm's register and Sable's column, laid side by side, and the two numbers agreeing.

## 1. NULL-WATCH: The Garrison That Talks
### *Decay & Silence · 852 Hz · the Continental Rift · Penal Legion*

### Sensory Palette
**Sound:** talk. Constant, flat, unstopping, about nothing. Under it the voiceless wind, which carries nothing and makes no sound of its own. At dawn, the Refusal.
**Light:** grey from above. Lanterns. Beyond the wall, the not-colour, which cannot be looked at.
**Smell:** lead, lamp-oil, wet wool, cold rock.
**Texture:** obsidian and lead mortar, four miles of it. Everything heavy. Everything cold.

### The wall
Forty batteries on four miles of obsidian and lead. Towers numbered; Tower Nine stands above the end of the Shelf and carries the buttress, the crane and the rope. The ground beyond the wall is bare grey rock for thirty yards, and then the land stops being there.

### Standing Order Nine, and the talking
**The central fact: keep talking.** Within about thirty yards of the lip, anyone who goes silent begins to go. Not pulled; *relieved*. The face goes startled, then not startled, then peaceful, and the person walks toward the not-colour at the pace of a man walking home at the end of a shift. The Lead Brothers do not stop anyone who is not theirs.

So the Watch talks. Every trooper, every hour, out loud, about nothing — onions, days of the week, cable-eyes, the price of lamp-oil — the way other soldiers are taught to keep their guard up. They talk while they fight. **This is why the Watch cannot lose a fight on that rock**: the Justiciary fought for their lives, and men fighting for their lives go quiet, and eleven of thirty-two walked off the edge including their commander. Nobody had told them. **The horror is that it is not a trap; it is an omission.**

- **RPG:** on any Rift-lip hex, a character who spends a round silent (no spoken action) makes a save. On a failure they do not take damage — they gain the *Relieved* condition and move toward the edge on their own initiative. Other characters can interrupt it only by talking *to* them, and the interrupter must then keep talking.
- **Horror beat:** a wounded man sits down against a crane with his hand on his belly and his face toward the edge, very peaceful, and has to be dragged back by the collar by a man who does not stop talking while he does it.

### Iron-cant
General **Theresa Ward** shouted her voice to rags on the wall on the first night of the Rift in 413 and it never came back. She commands by striking the iron over her heart with her knuckles — **iron-cant**, a full language, not a signal code. Battery crews learn the battery calls; almost nobody learns the whole of it.
- Four flat heavy blows: *the Watch holds the wall.*
- One blow, struck hard enough to ring: the order to go.
- A hand laid flat on a dead man's chest and tapped with one finger: the discharge rite (see below).

**She needs a voice.** The post is inherited, not appointed, and it is one of the series' great running devices:
- **94 — Elric Orrel**, of Fenwick in the Reach, penal legion from 416 for debt. Talked about the price of lamp-oil. Killed by flung lead on Tower Nine, 6 Frostmonth, mid-sentence.
- **Davey Pruett**, of Coldharrow in the Peaks, who learned the whole of iron-cant because he had nothing to do on the wall but talk and listen. Came up the stair and offered. **Heir confirmed.**

### The Discharge
The Watch does not bury and does not mourn aloud. The General lays her hand flat on the dead trooper's chest and taps the name, the place, the service and the word **Discharged**. Her voice speaks it after her. And then the whole garrison **stops talking** — one breath, two, three, at the edge of the world, which is the most dangerous thing any of them can do, and they do it on purpose. Then someone says *Rootday* in a voice that cracks, and they all pick it up, and go on.

> The silence of the Discharge is the Watch's only funeral rite and it costs them exactly what it looks like it costs. Never write it as a gesture.

### The edge, and what is under it — RULING
- Within about thirty yards of the lip, **Dawning glass goes silent and Vaelen cannot hear.** It is the only such place on the continent. This is the one room in the world where Kaelen can have a private conversation, and he cannot stay in it.
- **The thing below the Maw turned and reached for Kaelen's glass** during the Refusal. He cannot be on the Watch at dawn. He spends the second half of Book Three moored four miles out on the plain.
- **The Refusal** is the dawn firing: forty batteries pouring white noise out over the edge of the world. It is a ritual *no*. It is also the only weapon in Aethelgard that reliably kills a quiet (§6.1).

### People
- **General Theresa Ward** — mute since 413, commands in iron-cant, has refused the Arbiterium's writ for six years by the simple expedient of not acknowledging that it is addressed to her.
- **The Quartermaster — Halvard Greer**, of Greystairs in the Peaks. Condemned in spring 413 for striking an officer who struck a boy. Could not read; could write his name and two words more. Saw a child on a ledge in the summer of 413, went to the stores, and found a basket. Lowered one most nights for six years. Shot in the back on 24 Ashfall holding his own rope as it ran out. **Last words: "Keep talking."**
- **Two-Eleven** — the one with the dog. Big, slow, enormous hands. Inherited the rope and spliced a new one at the fourth hour the same night. Writes a page a night now instead of two words.
- **Nineteen** — grey, scarred eyebrow, fifty. Talked to an emptied boy about chickens for eight days without being asked to.
- **Thirty-Three, Sixty, Eighty-Eight (Davey Pruett)** — the troopers who went to the Organum in dead men's coats. Penal legionnaires drill like Justiciary because most of them were soldiers first, and they talk the whole way, and **nobody in Registry Square thinks anything of it, because nobody in Registry Square ever listens to anybody.**
- **The Lead Brothers** — hooded, faces sealed in lead, stationed along the lip. They do not intervene for anyone who is not theirs.

## 2. THE SHELF: The Rendered
### *A ledge of black rock three hundred feet down the Rift wall, in the lee of the dampening batteries*

### Status at the opening of Book Three
Roughly 400 people. **365 Rendered** (failed presses, each missing one named thing) and **14 Hollows** (fully pressed, empty, sitting at the far end with their hands in their laps). Forty-seven were taken on Dawnday morning when the Lip simply stopped being ground — 39 Rendered and 8 Hollows — without a sound.

### The rope
The only supply line. Lowered nightly from the buttress below Tower Nine. **Every descent gets a knot.** At 2,022 knots the Justiciary cut it and it went into the Rift with six years on it. The new rope is stiff yellow hemp and its first knot is numbered **2,023**.

**RULING, overriding Vol III §B.3:** the Rift opened in **413**, not earlier; the baskets have run six years, not eleven; command has known about five years and has chosen, every ninety days, not to notice.

### The notes
Under the bread, every night, folded small. For six years they said two words in very bad capitals pressed hard enough to go through the paper: **KEEP TALKING.** Nearly two thousand of them are kept in a biscuit tin in a hole in the wall of the Shelf, next to Orrin's tin. After the rope was cut the notes became a page a night, in a big slow round hand, about a dog called Rufus who could open a gate.

> The notes are the Shelf's scripture and its only archive. If anything in this setting is ever adapted to a single prop, it is the biscuit tin.

### Customs
- **You read things aloud.** Letters, notes, names. Always.
- **You say your name and what was taken.** "Maud Teller. Ebb Cross, spring '14. They took the word for mother. I say *the woman who had me*." It is a greeting, a census and a liturgy at once.
- **Nobody is touched.** 365 people who were half-pressed on this man's own form stood round Pellam Quist in the firelight and nobody laid a hand on him. They told him instead, one at a time, for two hours, and he listened with his head on one side and **graded them**.

### People
- **Orrin Hale** — tally-clerk at the Hall of Carriage for nineteen years. Pressed for asking why the velvet trays had staff names on them. **They took his ability to be afraid.** He is not brave; he is missing fear, and he knows the difference, and he spends himself accordingly. He walks the edge at dusk because he is the only one who can do it without wanting to go. **He can draw the Hall from memory, including which tray is Sable's.** His fear sits in tray four as a cracked grey core marked *Incomplete. Retained as example.*
  - His line to Vaelen, delivered on the lip of the new edge, is the thesis of the series: *"You're going to have to say it to my face, with my face like this. Tell me I'd have preferred the rest of it."*
- **Fen — Fenella Ashby.** Pressed at twelve at Ebb Cross, summer 413, the fourth pressing ever attempted (*"right, let's try a small one"*). **They took the growing.** She looks twelve and is eighteen. Her core was F-4, near the door of bin seven, and it broke; it said *Fen—* and stopped, which is the whole of her name, so it broke on the only half there was. She asks to be **last** in the press line so that whoever comes out of the chair at the end finds somebody still waiting. She ends the book holding Bridie Cole's daughter and deciding that she will stay exactly the same so the child has something to come back to.
- **Tessa Wray** — the only person who ever called herself home out of a cracked core and lived. Screamed for nine days. Her core had been set on **Annis**, a living woman still being eaten off Cape Dissonance, and Annis came home with her, lived in her head for nine days as a whole separate person, and took Tessa's grandmother's garden with her when she went. **This is the warning the tenth press does not heed.**
- **Bridie Cole** — the Malting, '17. They took the knowing of her left hand. Gave birth on the Shelf; named the child **Halla** after Halvard Greer. Her piece came home and her seed was a quiet. Died on the press at first light, 6 Frostmonth, having had her own hand back for about ten minutes. Her last word was *mine*. **Heir: Fen, and the child.**
- **The Quartermaster's successor-by-instinct** — see Two-Eleven, §1.

## 3. THE ORGANUM REVISITED
### *The ninth city. A tax office built on a chord.*

Vol IV established the Organum from the outside. Book Three went in. Everything below is new or corrected.

### 3.1 The city is an instrument — RULING
The Organum stands on the **Keynote**: the one place where the eight roots of the eight Anchors meet and every law can be reached at once. The Synod did not build a palace on it. It built an **organ**.

- The towers are **pipes**. Hundreds of them, 100 to 600 feet, grey, narrow, with mouths cut near the top and lead caps, standing in ranks and sweeping banks across the city.
- The streets run between them in straight lines, **laid out like the ranks of a keyboard**.
- Wind off the low land sounds them continuously: a low vast breath, a chord with no top and no bottom, felt in the chest and the teeth.
- Bellows under the Choir-Hall drive the ranks on the hour. The city **sounds the hour** in one chord audible thirty miles out. On feast days it plays hymns. When the Edict of Symmetry was proclaimed in 389 it played for nine hours and three people in the lower town bled from the ears.
- Under all of it, from the undercroft, something very low that the staff have stopped hearing. **Everyone stops hearing all of it within a week. That is the point of the Organum.**

### 3.2 THE LOCK — new mechanic, major
When the Synod wishes nobody to leave, the city plays **the Lock**: every rank at full breath at once, the deepest and widest chord the city can make, so loud it stops being sound and becomes the air turning solid.

- Every door, gate and mooring-ring keyed to a Signature shuts and will not open.
- Anyone **not of the Organum** — anyone whose own pitch has not been tuned to the Keynote by years of living over it — feels it go in at the ears and down, and take hold. **It is not pain. It is weight.** Movement is possible at the speed of walking through cold syrup. Armour rings. Lamps gutter. Glass breaks.
- **Organum-born staff are not held.** It only tells them to sit still, and they do, with their hands in their laps and their eyes closed.
- **The deaf are immune, completely.** It goes in at the ears and there are none. Mags Tennant walks through a locked city at an ordinary pace while everyone around her moves like a diver. **This is why the plan put a bosun who cannot be called on the crew** — and it is the single best piece of foreshadowing in the book, because it was written into the plan on the Shelf as a precaution and becomes the thing that saves bin seven.
- **The goods-hoist is not keyed to a Signature, because it is a winch, and winches do not care who turns them.** Every heist against the Organum has exactly one honest exit and it is a service lift.

### 3.3 The Hall of Carriage, floor by floor
Long, grey, east side of Registry Square, **no plate on the door**, not on any public plan. Three floors and a basement, counting the street floor as the first. **It smells of honey, faintly, and nobody who works there mentions it.**

- **Basement — Sorting.** A whitewashed brick cellar running 200 yards under the building. Racks like wine-racks, floor to vault, pigeonholes with a felt pad in each and a core on each pad. Tens of thousands, glowing lilac, **each murmuring its own name over and over so as not to forget it.** A long scrubbed deal table at the near end where a dozen people in grey smocks and felt gloves grade by ear.
  - *Instructions to Sorters*, framed at the foot of the stair: **CLEAR** — the name is given at once. **FAIR** — given with hesitation. **POOR** — not given, or given wrongly. *Poor cores are returned to stock.*
  - **"Returned to stock" is a brass mill** the size of a coffee-grinder at the end of the table. Hopper, crank, drawer. Poor cores go in; fine bright lilac powder comes out; the powder is **seed** for the next press, because the trench never sent enough. The young man turning the crank hums Hymn 9.
  - **Crates arrive stencilled with their harvest-house** (Ebb Cross, the Malting, Hollin Ford) — and some with the Church's **white sun-disc**, marked *Basilica of the Pure Note. For correction.* Everyone Seraphina ever arrested for singing the second note came here. She never asked where.
- **First floor — Receiving and Carriage.** Long counter, clerks, crates on the stone floor. Carrier's receipts stamped in red wax: **a key crossed with a quill, inside a ring.**
- **Second floor — Assignment, and the Concordance.** Long desks, pigeonholes of dockets. Nothing murmurs here, because here the people are numbers and numbers make no sound. In the corner room at the north end, on a black oak lectern, **the Concordance**: three great books, two feet tall, brass corners and clasps, recording every core by number, with its harvest-house, date, grade — and in the fifth column **the residual**: where the body went once the name was out of it. *Plaza of the Willows, returned to loop. The Malting, residual ward, labour. Schedule F, the lip.* Kept so that a complaining client can be shown why a core is unruly. **It is the only document in the world that can match a core to a body.** Vol. II (416, the Whisper-Grove harvest) is the one that leaves the building.
- **Third floor — the carpet.** Red, thick, the only carpet in the Hall, running the length of the building. **You feel it before you see it and it changes how you walk**; everyone goes quiet coming up the stair without meaning to. No windows. The honey is strongest here. At the north end, a small square room with one lamp and **the cabinet**: walnut, glass-fronted, brass handles, six velvet trays of very small cores in pressed hollows like eggs in a box, each with a hand-lettered ivory tag. One for **every scribe and clerk and officer ever sworn to the Office** — a copy taken at the swearing with a little fork, very gently, while you say the oath. You do not feel it. You are not meant to know.
  - The card in the brass frame on the cabinet front reads: **Against the day.** (In full: *against the day the scribe may need reminding of her duty.*)
  - **Operative effect:** speaking a person's name into their copy-core **stops them**, completely, like a clock stopping, for as long as the speaker goes on. Quist uses it on Sable at ten yards in a crowded cellar and she drops where she stands.

### 3.4 The reconciling bar
An iron stand bolted to the door of bin seven, carrying a tuned bar of dark metal as long as a man's arm, with a jeweller's brass hammer to hand.

**Cracked glass has a flaw and a flaw has a pitch.** Struck, the bar rings at the pitch of the flaw, and the ring carries through the metal — stand to door to frame to shelves — and **every cracked core on the same iron breaks along its crack**, in order, low numbers first, running back into the dark like a crack across a pond. Each voice gets a syllable out and stops with a sound like a thimble dropped on stone. A struck bar rings for about a minute, which is long enough to reach the back of the room.

**It is stopped by putting a body around it.** Mags wrapped her one good arm round it and pulled it against her breastbone and the ring went into her instead; she bled from the nose and the ear and the bin's far half survived. **Casualty ledger: 232 of 365 cracked cores broken; 131 recovered.**

### 3.5 Bin seven
Behind a locked grey iron door with a small grille at face height, at the far end of Sorting. Cracked cores on felt pads, shelved **by docket number, low at the door and high at the back** — which means **the earliest victims were nearest the bar.** The sound through the grille is not the racks' murmur: it is hundreds of voices starting half a name and stopping and starting again, and never reaching the end.

There is a low arch at the back into the dark beyond. Proctors know the other way in. Nobody else does.

**Office doctrine:** incomplete draws are *retained against need* — the Office does not throw away a voice, even a broken one. When the residuals they belong to are disposed of, the draws become **surplus**, and surplus is reconciled. The Null-Watch writ was therefore scheduled to kill 365 people twice.

### 3.6 People of the Organum
- **Registrar-General Pellam Quist.** Sixty-three. Small the way a sparrow is small. Soft pink face, white stock, spectacles, ink to the second knuckle. Pale blue eyes, very tired, very kind. His standing address to incoming clerks, every Rootday since 416: **"Every name is a unit."** He taught Sable to rule a page and has her F/3 docket framed in his office. He is never cruel and never raises his voice, and he sits by a door for a whole morning with a hammer on his knee because he does not think it right to leave it to a clerk. **He dies of a quiet on 6 Frostmonth because he leaned toward it to see it better**, and the expression on his frozen face is that of a man who has at last seen exactly what he wanted to see.
  - **Fen's sentence on him is the moral centre of the book and it fails completely**: she condemns him to watch every return. He is *delighted*. He has always wanted to see one go back. She knows she has given him what he wanted and does it anyway because it is right and there is nothing else on the Shelf that is.
- **Deputy Registrar Hester Vell** (the renamed Vol IV "Halloway"). Grey hair, spectacles on a chain, a cardigan she knits herself, needles going click-click-click under the clerestories. She was kind to Sable on her first day and brought her tea. She is kind to everybody; she brought Orrin Hale a cup of tea on the day they took him down to the chair. **She tells Sable exactly where the Concordance is, warns her about the empty tray, and does not raise the alarm — and then her needles stop, and do not start again.** The Organum is staffed entirely by people like this and that is why it functions.
- **Ambrose**, the coffee stall in the south-east corner of Registry Square. Sixty years under a faded awning. Black with a little sugar in a chipped white cup; he has never asked anyone their name and **has never once asked anybody what they did.** He does not remember Orrin Hale, who came every morning for nineteen years and took the blue cup. **The banality is the horror; play it completely straight.**

### 3.7 Hazards
Not monsters. **Paperwork, recognition, carpet, and the Lock.** A severed access key does not change a face. A clerk who knew you fifteen years ago. A receipt in your own handwriting. A corner room that is never locked in the daytime because nobody has ever needed it to be.

**Proctors** are the Office's hand: grey oilcloth, silver mesh over the face, a felted rod in one hand and a **hand-fork** in the other — brass, tuned to 741, long as a forearm. Eight seconds of contact at the back of the neck, under the skull, takes a name. They are free in the Lock. They are not soldiers and they go down to a knight, but they only have to touch you once.

## 4. THE PRESS, AND THE RETURNING OF NAMES
*The book's great mechanical discovery, and the rules it runs on.*

### 4.1 The machine
A Catalogue press, Office property, taken from the harvest-house at the Malting. A black oak chair with brass fittings; a frame over it like a printing-press; a long brass fork on a pivoted arm tuned to **741**; a little brass cup at the end of the arm where the glass goes. Run forward it takes a name out. **Run backward it puts one in**, through the fork, into the back of the neck, by the same road.

It can do roughly **twenty a day with rest between**, and it must be run at the light, with the ship made fast, the plank rigged and the water lowered — never at night, never in a hurry.

### 4.2 Hollow and Rendered are not the same case — RULING
- **A Rendered person is half, not empty.** Somebody is home, and has been waiting with the door open. The fork only carries the piece across; the person **calls it** themselves — reaching into the shape of the hole and calling into it, the way you call a dog by whistling when you have forgotten its name. The core goes dark quickly, like a coal dropped in water. *"A piece knows its way home, if there's someone home to call it."* **No scribe and no price are required.**
- **A Hollow is empty.** Nobody is home to call. So **someone must stand in the door**: hold it open, let the name through themselves, be the house for the moment it takes.

### 4.3 The rent — RULING, and it reframes the whole series
**Whoever stands in the door pays.** Not something they choose. A thing of equal weight to the name returned, taken out of them, and they do not learn what it was until it is gone.

**The scribe was never required. Anyone with a house can stand in the door.**

Sable paid twice — her true name at the Malting, her mother's hands for Wren — because the Office had never once put a name back, so nobody in the world knew it could be shared. *"I could have asked. I could have asked anyone."* **This is the discovery that lets Book Three end with 131 people coming home instead of one**, and it is paid for in character, not in plot:
- **Nineteen** stood in the door for Davey Pruett. The rent: **the name of the village she was born in.** She can still see the bridge, the forge, her father at the anvil. *"It burned down anyway. I'd have liked to know what it was called, though."*
- **Orrin Hale** stood in the door for Agathe Morrow. The rent: **his mother's face.** He has all the facts of her — the mole by her mouth, the grey eyes, the singing while she ironed — and cannot see her. He does not mind. He can feel the shape of where the minding would go.

### 4.4 Being a Hollow is not being absent — RULING, and it is the worst line in the book
**The Hollow is awake the whole time.** Davey Pruett, returned: *"I was in a room with no doors. For days. And I could hear her. I could hear every word. I couldn't answer. I couldn't find my mouth."* Agathe Morrow knew she was carried across the quiet and could not say thank you.

Every Hollow in Whisper-Grove's plazas, every Unstrung setting a table for four, is in that room. **Deploy this once, in detail, by someone calm, and never soften it afterwards.**

### 4.5 The seed, and the tenth
A core must set around a **seed**. Seed comes from two places, and the Office mixes both without recording which: ground poor cores from the brass mill, and **tuning stock** — bottled trench-voices off the lip at Cape Dissonance, six sovereigns a bottle, forty a month for six years.

**A core set on tuning stock carries whatever was in the bottle home with the name.** And nobody can tell which they have until the stopper is out. The Rust Coast's law applies to cores exactly as it applies to bottles: *if none were real it would be a cheat; if all were real it would be a mercy; it's some.*

Observed outcomes across 131 presses:
- **121 clean.** The piece comes home and nothing comes with it.
- **4 strangers.** A living trench-voice comes home riding the name — a fisherman from the Cape, a girl who went in after her brother — sits in a borrowed head for about a day asking politely whether it is the tide, then goes quiet, **and takes some small thing with it when it goes.**
- **1 mimic** (the sixty-third). Came up saying a name in a woman's voice, glad to be out, and **asked a question it already knew the answer to.** Killed with a boat-hook before it finished the question, then held in the water-butt until it stopped. The woman in the chair was all right.
- **1 quiet** (the tenth). See §6.1.

**A core's seed is invisible.** Bridie Cole's core was lilac, faintly lit, cracked, saying half a name — identical to the nine before it. Sable looked. **The horror is that there is no tell and no precaution except iron, cold water and four people who do not panic.**

### 4.6 The line
A hundred and thirty-one buff cards, each with a pinhole, drawn by lot and laid out in a row on the rock. **After the tenth press killed a woman in front of all of them, not one person left the line.** They sat down where they stood, in the order of the cards, in the rain, and talked, and waited for the light. That is the correct shape of courage in this series: not a charge, a queue.

## 5. THE ARBITERIUM IN THE FIELD

### The Edict of Symmetry (389)
*That law may never wound one without wounding the other, an Arbiter and his heir shall be bound in Symmetry, wound for wound, and neither shall outlive the other by an hour.*

- **Aurelian Vaelen, the Dread Arbiter.** Forty; born late to an already-old father and bound at **nine**, before he could have known what he was agreeing to. Tall, lean, grey-eyed, black coat, silver collar, a small silver scale on a chain with its two pans hanging level. **His face is the face of a man who has spent his whole life doing arithmetic in which he is one of the terms.**
- **Operative effect, and the series' tightest knot:** Casimir Vaelen cannot be assassinated, because killing him kills a son who has done nothing to earn it — and killing the son kills the only man holding eight gods up, or down, depending who you ask. Vane works this out at the Hanging Water and carries a loaded pistol for weeks. He does not fire. *"He came down to listen. You can't shoot a man who's come down to listen."*
- The father walks a mile across open plain alone, and the son stands beside his guns and watches him go and **cannot follow, cannot order anyone to follow, and will feel it in his own ankle if his father turns it on a stone.**

### The column
Two thousand foot in blocks of two hundred, eight field-pieces, two long-barrelled siege guns on iron carriages drawn by twelve horses each, a hundred wagons, and one closed black carriage with four greys and no device on the door. Infantry stand aside for the carriage **the way people stand aside for a funeral**, unordered.

### Justiciary practice
Writs are **posted at the gate for a statutory period**, and the period runs from the posting. A Justiciar reads your file before meeting you. They fight in silence, formally, well, and that is why they died. **Justiciar Corvan Lisle**, twenty-six, polite, the best his Arbiter ever trained, had read every file and heard 94 talking about lamp-oil and filed it as a quaint provincial custom.

**RULING on escalation:** Aurelian does not wait for his writ to fall. He shells **Battery Nine** at dusk on 6 Frostmonth specifically to destroy the only gun that had fired into the Rift, so that nothing can break a quiet at the light the next morning. It is lawful, pre-emptive, surgical, and it kills the General's voice as a side effect. **This is how the Arbiterium fights: not cruelty, foresight.**

### The Visit (7 Frostmonth, Dawnday) — the book's ending
Casimir Vaelen writes first, on heavy cream paper under a plain grey seal with no device, in a hand that has stopped wasting ink. He apologises for Hetty Bram. He does not make an offer. He asks to come and look.

Then he comes down a rope in a wicker basket with his stick across his knees and **walks two hundred yards along a line of a hundred and twenty-one people for three hours**, stopping in front of every one, listening while they tell him their name and what was taken. **His laced glass dims as he goes**, further from the ship, nearer the edge — and he stops at the hundredth card and says, to nobody: *"It's very quiet here. I'd forgotten what it was like."*

Then Orrin Hale, standing on the lip of the new edge with his back to the drop, makes him say it to his face. And the Supreme Arbiter of the Iron Synod opens his mouth, and closes it, and opens it again, and says: **"I can't. I don't have an answer. I thought I did."**

He asks his son to **stay** the writ, not lift it — *"I can't lift it; it's law, and he's law, and I won't ask him to stop being what he is"* — until he has an answer for the man at the end of the line. And he says that if he never gets one, he will go and do it anyway, under the Organum, with all eight, without an answer. *"Which I gather is how everyone else has to do everything. I'd just never had to try it before."*

**This is more frightening than any answer he could have given, and the books should never let him off it or redeem him for it.**

### Casimir Vaelen, physically — RULING
Laced, never set. Fine pale threads of glass under old skin like frost on a window: up out of the collar, along the jaw, under the ear, in the backs of the hands, at the temples, in the whites of the eyes. **A whole man laced through like a leaf with its veins showing, and none of it has ever carried a note.** Faintly lit, visible only in grey light. He has to think about each breath, and there is a sound at the end of each one like wind across the mouth of a bottle.

## 6. BESTIARY ADDITIONS

### 6.1 The quiet, as a battlefield hazard — expanded
Vol IV covered quiets in bottles. Book Three puts one in the open, in daylight, on a crowded deck.

**Onset.** Instantaneous and total within about forty feet. Not deafness and not silence: **the air forgets how to carry.** A ringing fork shivers and makes no sound. Rain bursts on the planks in silence. Your own heart, which you have heard all your life without knowing it, is gone.

**Appearance.** The eye invents, as the ear does with the bottles. Reported here as a low wide wet shape about the size of a seated woman, grey-blue, folded over itself like a tongue, with a moving fringe along its underside.

**Behaviour.** It does not lunge; **it leans**, and the fringe goes over the shoulders the way a shawl does. It takes the warm first and the warm takes a while. The victim's face does not scream — it goes soft, then surprised, then **relieved**. Bystanders within a few feet feel the warmth going out of them through anything they are touching.

**What works** (and the Rust Coast's rule is exact — *iron and cold water and about four people who do not panic*):
1. **Iron pins it.** A boat-hook through the fringe into oak makes it flinch off an inch. It does not kill it.
2. **Cold water shrinks it.** Sixty gallons of ice-water is enough to make it slide off a chair and lose a great deal of size. It is not enough; the water thins, runs off, loses its cold, and the thing **starts to grow again by drawing warmth up through whatever is holding it down.**
3. **White noise kills it.** The Refusal, a dampening battery depressed into the Rift, thirty to sixty seconds of every sound in the world poured down onto it, and the quiet breaks like ice from the edges in. What is left is the size of a fist, grey-blue and slack, and goes over the side.
4. **The deaf and the voiceless are the only people who function inside one.** Everyone else stands with their mouth open shouting nothing at each other. Mags and Lyra had been doing without speech for weeks and simply kept working.

**Horror beat (used, do not reuse):** it flinches off its victim toward the warmest thing in reach that is not holding iron, and that is a tied-up old man on a crate three feet away who leans toward it to see it better and does not draw his legs away.

### 6.2 The mimic in a core
As the bottle-mimic (Vol IV §1.5) but delivered through a press into a person's own returning name. **Tells:** it is glad to be out; it says the name correctly; **it asks a question it already has the answer to.** Kill it before the question finishes.

### 6.3 Office listening-horns
Ten-foot brass trumpets on swivel mounts at a cutter's bow, a man with his ear at the narrow end and his eyes shut, sweeping the air the way a lighthouse sweeps the sea, **but backward — drawing sound in instead of throwing light out.** Range: a man breathing at a quarter-mile; a hold full of cores saying their names at two miles; set Dawning glass at about the same.

**Counter:** a quiet-field. Vesper's thinned carrying hides whatever it covers — **but it covers the bow or the hold, not both**, which is the exact shape of the choice in Book Three Ch8.

### 6.4 Carillon Legionnaires — follow-up
Eleven of the fourteen Malting Hollows had their names assigned to Legions. The Concordance does not follow a core once it leaves the Hall. **There are eleven people in the world whose names are walking around inside brass-skulled soldiers, and the series has no way to find them.** Hold this.

## 8. STATE OF PLAY AT THE END OF BOOK THREE

**Numbers.**
- **395 cores** in the *Kestrel*'s hold (396 less Agathe Morrow), with nowhere yet to go.
- **363 Rendered** on the Shelf, plus Fen, plus Halla.
- **13 Hollows** remaining at the far end, including **Ione Pask**, whose name is in the hold and for whom nobody has yet stood in the door.
- **Tobias Lennart's** name was in the crate that broke over the Valley. He will sit waiting to be called into a room until he goes into the quiet. **Nobody will ever call him.** Sable could not write *broken* a thirty-seventh time and marked it with a clerk's closed-account square instead.
- **131 pieces returned; 232 broken on the bar; 1 killed by a quiet; 1 mimic destroyed; 4 strangers came and went, each taking something small.**

**People.**
- **Kaelen** — glass through the throat, green under the skin of the neck, three notes now (528, 639, and a whisper of green). **Mote ate his fear.** He has agreed, calmly, to walk into the Organum after the press, and his calm is the most frightening thing in the book. He is sliding toward being Vaelen and cannot feel it happening.
- **Lyra** — no voice, and a **whistle** that costs blood out of her ruined throat. Used it to break a 741 lock a hair flat. Spent it, and had nothing left to sing Hetty Bram's Last Note with, so she breathed beside her instead. **This becomes her craft: the breath, not the voice.**
- **Sable** — one piece left (the scriptorium's smell), and **thinner than it was**, because Quist spoke her name into her copy. Her own name-core rolled under a rack and is lost in Sorting forever, among tens of thousands, saying itself to nobody. **Her face is going smooth.** She carries Orrin Hale's fear in her inside pocket, cold, wanting to get away.
- **Vane** — had the shot and did not take it. Counts everything. Face bleached; ribs cracked.
- **Mags** — bled from the nose and ear holding a reconciling bar against her chest; deaf on the other side now too, if she ever heard on either.
- **Seraphina** — drew every light in a two-hundred-yard cellar into the Blade and let it go again slowly instead of dropping it, which she could never do before. Killed a man she did not mean to. Learned where the people she arrested for singing the second note were sent.
- **Vesper** — would have thrown Mote into the Rift on the first night and did not, and knows it.
- **Ignatius Crane** — got a stove lit on the ninth strike with a flint and steel like anybody else, and put on a dead woman's apron, and made something without knowing what it was. **His fire is ordinary now and it burns whether anybody believes in it or not.**

**Politics.** The writ is **stayed, not lifted.** The column sits on the plain with two siege guns that will not fire. Battery Nine is wrecked; Eight and Ten fire the Refusal as usual. The door at the Organum is open and nobody will stop anyone on the stair.

## 9. RPG HOOKS
1. **The biscuit tin.** Two thousand notes, six years, two words each. A party member reads them in order and works out, from the handwriting alone, which nights the old man was ill.
2. **The coffee stall.** Sixty years. He has never asked anybody what they did. What does he know, and what would it cost him to have known it?
3. **Eleven names in eleven Legions.** The Concordance stops at the Hall door. Finding one is a campaign.
4. **Tray three is empty.** Somebody took it. Where does a very small core go when the man who pocketed it dies of a quiet on a ship at the bottom of a rift?
5. **The man who will never be called.** Tobias Lennart sits very straight, waiting to be called into a room, and the party knows why and cannot fix it.
6. **Standing in the door.** Offer a player character the chance to return a stranger's name. Do not tell them the rent. Roll it, secretly, from the things on their own character sheet.
7. **The sixty-third card.** Run the press as a procedure, one card at a time, with a hidden seed table. Let them get comfortable around the fortieth.
8. **The Lock.** A heist city where the deaf character is the only one who can move. Build one dungeon around it and never reuse it.
